Foundation
What Your Meetings Teach
At the director level, the meetings you run set the tone for how your org operates. Not just because they consume time — but because they model something. Every meeting you facilitate demonstrates how you think decisions should be made, whether status reporting and decision-making are the same thing, and whether it's safe to disagree with senior people in the room.
A bad director meeting doesn't just waste an hour. It teaches the org something — that reporting out is the same as alignment, that questions from the leader are rhetorical, that bringing a problem without a solution isn't welcome.
The meetings you run are a form of culture. The norms they establish get replicated by your managers in their meetings, and by their direct reports in theirs. One hour in a room with you ripples three levels down.
The director's meeting job is different from the manager's. You're not just running meetings — you're teaching your managers how to run them.

Three Meetings Directors Often Run Badly
These are the most common traps — not because directors aren't skilled, but because the format defaults get set wrong and never get reset.
1

The Leadership Team Meeting Where Everyone Reports Out

Every function gets 10 minutes. Updates are shared, heads nod, the meeting ends. Nothing was decided. Nothing was challenged. No one is more aligned than before. This meeting exists to feel like coordination without doing the work of it.

2

The All-Hands Where the Leader Presents and Nobody Asks Questions

The director delivers a well-prepared presentation. Silence for questions. A few people say thank you. The room files out. Engagement was performed, not built. The silence was compliance, not alignment.

3

The Strategy Session That Produces a Deck But No Alignment

A full day of discussion. A polished output. Three weeks later, each function is executing a slightly different version of what was agreed. The meeting felt productive; the alignment wasn't real.

The Culture Modeling Job
How you behave in meetings teaches people what's valued. Do you ask questions you don't know the answer to — or only the ones that demonstrate your knowledge? Do you change your mind publicly when someone brings a better argument — or do you defend your original position? Do you name when someone's input changed your thinking — or let that go unacknowledged?
These behaviors accumulate into a culture. A culture where leaders are curious, revisable, and generous with credit runs very different meetings than one where leaders are certain, defensive, and focused on demonstrating command.
The hardest part of meeting culture is that your behavior in the room communicates more than any stated norm. You can say "I want dissent" and then respond in ways that teach people never to offer it again.
The Director Meeting
Three Director Meeting Types
Not every meeting has the same job. The facilitation failure is running all three types the same way. Know what type you're running before it starts — and design it accordingly.

Decision Meetings

  • Explicit decision needed — name it on the agenda
  • Right people in the room — decision-makers, not just stakeholders
  • Options pre-read before the meeting, not introduced in it
  • Time-boxed deliberation with a clear decision owner
  • Decision and owner recorded before the meeting ends

Alignment Meetings

  • Complex cross-functional initiative requiring shared understanding
  • Goal: build shared mental model, surface concerns early
  • Concerns are features, not threats — surface them deliberately
  • End with explicit "what did we just agree to" not assumed
  • Agree on next steps with owners and timelines

Culture and Rhythm Meetings

  • Leadership team norms, quarterly planning, team health
  • Celebrate wins and learn from failures explicitly
  • Not about information — about belonging, identity, and trust
  • Protect these from getting replaced by operational content

Creating Psychological Safety in the Room
The higher the title, the more carefully people manage their input. When a director is in the room, people instinctively calibrate — what does this person value? What's safe to say? What response will their challenge get?
Psychological safety in a director-level room isn't an HR concept — it's a decision quality issue. Better decisions come from rooms where people say what they actually think, not what they believe you want to hear.
1

Ask a Question You Don't Know the Answer To

Modeling intellectual humility — genuinely not knowing, and being curious — teaches the room that not-knowing is safe here. The opposite (asking questions you already know the answer to) teaches them you're testing, not learning.

2

Disagree with Yourself in Public

"Here's where I've changed my view on this." When leaders revise their position publicly, they demonstrate that being wrong is survivable — and that the goal is getting it right, not being right.

3

Call Out When Someone's Input Changed Your Thinking

"That reframed it for me — I was thinking about it differently before this conversation." Naming the influence makes it visible and repeatable. It tells the room: your input has power here.

The Director Meeting
The Status-Reporting Leadership Meeting
Your weekly leadership team meeting has drifted into a round-robin of status updates. Everyone reports what they're working on, a few questions are asked, and the meeting ends. Nothing is ever actually decided in the meeting. You realize this has been the pattern for months. What do you do?
ACancel the meeting — if nothing is being decided, the time isn't justified.
BRedesign the meeting explicitly: move status updates to async, reserve meeting time for the one or two decisions or alignment topics that actually need the group — and name that shift to the team.
CAdd a decision-focused agenda item each week while keeping the status reports — it gives everyone coverage while adding substance.
DAsk each person to limit their update to two minutes — the problem is length, not format.
The Director Meeting
When Nobody Challenges the Senior Person
Your CPO is in a strategy meeting you're facilitating. They share a strong directional view early in the discussion. After that, the rest of the room largely agrees — ideas build on the CPO's framing rather than challenging it. You're not sure the best thinking is surfacing. What do you do?
ALet it run — the CPO's perspective carries strategic weight, and it's reasonable for others to build on it rather than challenge it.
BCreate structured space for challenge: "Before we build further on this direction, let's spend five minutes on what might be wrong with it or what we'd need to believe for it to be right. I'll start."
CPull a few people aside after the meeting to get their real views — the room isn't the right place for it.
DAsk the CPO to step out for a portion of the discussion so the group can think independently first.
Practice
The Manager Who Dominates Every Meeting
One of your managers has a strong presence and consistently takes up significantly more airtime than anyone else in your leadership team meetings — not through interruption, but through length and frequency of comments. Others have gotten quieter over the past few months. What do you do?
AAddress it publicly in the meeting: "Let's hear from some folks who haven't spoken yet."
BHave a private conversation with the manager: name the pattern, share the impact (others are getting quieter), and ask for their self-awareness about it — then redesign the meeting structure to create more distributed input.
CRedistribute speaking time by going around the table — structured turns give everyone equal airtime.
DDon't intervene — some people are simply more vocal, and the team will self-correct over time.
Practice
The Strategy Session That Needs Real Alignment
You're planning a half-day strategy session with your leadership team on a major org initiative. Previous strategy sessions have produced decks and discussions but not real alignment — three weeks later the functions were executing differently. How do you design this one differently?
AMake the session more interactive — use polling and breakout groups to increase engagement.
BBuild in an explicit alignment check at the end: "What did we just decide? What are we each doing differently because of this conversation? Let's name it before we leave." Have someone document decisions and owners in real time.
CFollow up with a recap deck within 48 hours so everyone has the same reference point going forward.
DInvite more stakeholders — broader representation leads to stronger buy-in.
Practice
After a Decision Made in the Room Turns Out Wrong
Your leadership team made a clear decision in a meeting three weeks ago. You're now learning the decision was based on a flawed assumption — and the cost of executing it is becoming apparent. How do you handle this?
AReverse the decision quietly without calling explicit attention to the fact that it was wrong — minimize disruption.
BCall the team together, name what you've learned, acknowledge the assumption was wrong, make the reversal or adjustment transparent, and use it as a teaching moment for how to surface flawed assumptions earlier in future decisions.
CIdentify whose analysis led to the flawed assumption and address it directly with them — accountability is important in decision-making culture.
DAdjust the execution to minimize the impact of the bad decision without formally revisiting it — the decision stands, but you course-correct quietly.

Three commitments for director-level meetings:

Define the meeting type before it starts — Decision, Alignment, or Culture — and design it accordingly
