Foundation
The Leader Who Didn't Know How They Landed
Jordan had always been the go-to person on the team — calm under pressure, fast at decisions, clear communicator. Then Jordan got promoted. Two months in, a team member asked to transfer.
When Jordan asked why, the feedback was hard to hear: "When things get stressful, you get short and dismissive. I don't feel safe asking questions."
Jordan had no idea. The behaviors that felt like efficiency to Jordan felt like aggression to the team.
Self-awareness isn't knowing your strengths. It's knowing how you land on others — especially when things get hard.
Foundation
What EQ Actually Is
Emotional Intelligence isn't about being nice or suppressing feelings. It's about four things:
1. Knowing your own emotional state.
2. Managing that state intentionally.
3. Reading others' emotional state accurately.
4. Using that awareness to manage relationships well.
Most leaders are stronger in some areas than others. The first step is knowing where you are — not assuming you're strong everywhere just because you care about your team.
Foundation
Your Default Style Under Pressure
Every leader has a default mode when things get stressful. Some go quiet. Some get sharp. Some over-communicate. Some disappear.
Your team learns your pattern fast — and starts managing around it.
The question isn't whether you have a stress response. It's whether you're aware of it and can choose your response instead of defaulting to it.
The gap between stimulus and response is where EQ lives. A half-second of awareness — "I'm triggered right now" — changes everything about what you do next.

Carrying Your Last Meeting Into the Next One
You've just come from a frustrating meeting with your leader. Your team member walks in for a scheduled 1:1 looking a little nervous.
What's the high-EQ move?
AGet through the 1:1 quickly — you're not in the right headspace for a long conversation.
BTake 60 seconds before the 1:1 to consciously shift your state, then open with genuine curiosity about how they're doing.
CBe transparent about your frustration so they understand your mood isn't about them.
DReschedule — it's not fair to give them a distracted version of you.
The EQ Framework
Triggers — Know Yours Before They Know You
Every leader has emotional triggers: situations that reliably produce a strong reaction. Common ones include being questioned in front of others, a team member not following through, feeling like your effort isn't recognized, or navigating constant ambiguity.
Knowing your triggers doesn't eliminate them. It gives you a half-second of awareness before you react — and that half-second is everything.
Name your triggers. Write them down if you have to. The leaders who know what sets them off are the leaders who have the most control over what they do next.
The EQ Framework
When Someone Pushes Back in Public
A team member pushes back on a decision you made in a team meeting. You feel your face get warm.
What's the most emotionally intelligent response in the moment?
ADefend your decision clearly and confidently — showing uncertainty undermines your authority.
BTake a breath, acknowledge their perspective out loud, and ask what they'd suggest instead.
CTell them you'll talk about it later — handling it publicly creates awkwardness.
DAgree with them in the moment to reduce tension, then address it privately afterward.

The Team Member You Can't Read
One of your team members rarely shows emotion and is hard to read. You're not sure how they're doing.
The highest-EQ approach is:
AAssume they're fine — if something were wrong, they'd say so.
BAsk directly: "I notice I'm not always sure how you're doing — how are you really?"
CWatch for behavioral signals over the next few weeks before assuming anything.
DAsk their close work friend to give you an informal update.
Practice
Critical Feedback in the Moment
You get critical feedback from a peer in a meeting. Your immediate internal reaction is strong disagreement.
What do you do?
ARespond immediately and clearly so they don't think you agree.
BWrite down what they said, say "Thank you — I want to think about that," and revisit it later.
CStay completely silent so as not to escalate.
DAsk your leader to weigh in on who's right.
Practice
When Your Stress Response Affects the Team
You notice that when you're stressed, you become very directive and stop asking for input. Your team has started going quiet in meetings.
What's the most effective response?
ATell the team you're under pressure and ask them to bear with you temporarily.
BName the pattern to the team: "I've noticed I've been more directive lately. I want to make sure I'm still hearing from you. What haven't I been asking about?"
CWork harder to control the stress so the team doesn't see it.
DWait until the stress period passes — the team will adjust.
