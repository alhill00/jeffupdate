Foundation
Strategy Is Only as Good as the Talent Available to Execute It
VP-level talent strategy means making deliberate decisions about human capability as a competitive resource — not just managing headcount and performance processes.
The talent risk most VPs don't see isn't the bottom of the distribution — it's the middle. Underdeveloped mid-range performers can drag an organization as much as any single low performer.
The strategic imperative: Build capability before you need it. The VP who builds talent ahead of strategy has an organizational advantage that compounds. The one who reacts to talent gaps after they appear is always behind.
Foundation
The Talent Conversation VPs Must Lead
The talent conversation at the VP level isn't just "who's performing?" It's a set of more strategic questions that most organizations never get to.

The Operational Talent Question

  • Who's performing and who isn't?
  • Who needs a performance plan?
  • Who do we need to hire to fill a gap?

Necessary. Not sufficient.

The Strategic Talent Question

  • Who is this organization building toward?
  • What capability do we need in 18 months?
  • Are we building, buying, or borrowing it?

This is VP-level talent leadership.

Foundation
Build, Buy, or Borrow
Every capability your organization needs comes from one of three sources. Deliberate VPs make this decision explicitly — not by default.
1
Build — Develop Internally
Invest in developing the capability in Team Members you already have. Slower to deploy, higher retention payoff, builds organizational knowledge. Best for capabilities that require deep cultural context.
2
Buy — Bring from Outside
Hire externally for capability the org doesn't have internally. Faster to capability, higher short-term cost, cultural integration risk. Best for capabilities with a steep development curve or specialized expertise.
3
Borrow — Access Through Partnership
Use contractors, consultants, or cross-functional borrowing to access capability without building or buying it. Best for capabilities needed temporarily or episodically.
The Talent Framework
Five Talent Decisions VPs Own
These five decisions determine whether your organization has the human capability your strategy requires. Most VPs actively manage two or three of them. The best manage all five.
1
Role Criticality
Which roles most immediately damage strategy if poorly filled? Not every role is equally critical — knowing which ones are lets you invest protection and development resources accurately.
2
Portfolio Composition
What's the performer mix and is it sustainable? An org with all high performers is rare — but one with too many under-performers in critical roles is fragile. Know your composition and manage it deliberately.
3
Development Investment Allocation
Where is development investment going — and is it deliberate? Investment that follows whoever asks loudest is not a talent strategy. Investment allocated to highest-return opportunities is.
4
Inflow Quality
Is the hiring and promotion standard consistent with strategy requirements? Inconsistent standards at the inflow create structural talent problems that take years to work out.
5
Exit Management
When someone is wrong for a role, how quickly does action follow recognition? Slow exits are a culture tax — they signal that performance standards are optional and drain the engagement of Team Members who are performing.
Foundation
The Hidden Cost of Slow Exits
VPs often underestimate the organizational cost of keeping someone in a role they're wrong for. The cost is not just their underperformance — it's what their presence does to everyone around them.

What Slow Exits Actually Cost

  • High performers disengage when standards aren't enforced
  • The team adapts around the gap — building in inefficiency
  • The person stays longer than is good for them or the org
  • Leadership credibility erodes

What Fast, Humane Exits Produce

  • Clarity that performance standards are real
  • Energy freed from managing around the gap
  • An opening to bring in someone who can succeed
  • Leadership credibility with the people who are performing
The insight: Delay isn't kindness. The VP who moves slowly on a wrong-role situation isn't being generous — they're being avoidant. The most compassionate action is often the most direct one.
The Talent Framework
Promoting on Performance, Not Potential
Your organization has a pattern of promoting the highest-performing individual contributors into leadership roles. Several of these promotions have not worked out — the individuals were excellent in their prior roles but are struggling to lead. How do you address this?
AAccept it as an inherent challenge — the best individual contributors are always the logical promotion candidates.
BChange the promotion standard to assess leadership potential explicitly — separate from current performance — and build that distinction into how promotions are evaluated.
CCreate more robust manager training to compensate for selecting on performance rather than potential.
DHire externally for leadership roles to break the pattern of internal promotions that haven't worked.
The Talent Framework
The Long-Tenured Leader Who's No Longer Right
A long-tenured, well-liked leader in your organization is no longer the right person for their role. The strategy has shifted, their skill set hasn't, and their team's results are declining. What's your approach?
AGive them another year and monitor closely — tenure deserves patience.
BHave a direct, honest conversation about the gap between what the role now requires and what they bring, and together evaluate whether there's a path to close it or a different role that fits better.
CRestructure around them — change the role scope to fit their capabilities.
DBuild the business case for a transition and present it to HR without speaking with the leader first.
Practice
Investing in Someone Others Have Written Off
A Team Member in your org has been labeled "not a fit" by their manager after struggling for two quarters. You've observed them in a few situations and see leadership potential that isn't showing up in their current role. What do you do?
ADefer to the manager's assessment — they have the most direct visibility.
BHave a direct conversation with the manager: share what you've observed, explore whether there's a role fit issue vs. a capability issue, and discuss what a deliberate development investment could test.
CMove the Team Member to your direct oversight so you can develop them without the manager's frame.
DSay nothing — intervening in a manager's assessment would undermine their authority.
Practice
Building Capability for a Shift 18 Months Out
Your organization's strategy is shifting significantly in 18 months — requiring a capability your current team doesn't have. The shift isn't public yet. What's your talent strategy approach?
AWait until the strategy is public before making talent investments — premature moves create confusion.
BBegin making build, buy, or borrow decisions now — identify who could develop toward the needed capability and start investing, even if the broader strategy announcement is months away.
CPlan to hire externally at the time of the shift — it's the fastest path to the capability when you need it.
DBuild the capability case for the executive team to fund a new headcount plan before making any investments.
Practice
Critical Role, Wrong Person
A role critical to your strategy execution has been filled for 8 months by someone who is genuinely trying but clearly not performing at the level the role requires. The organization has adapted around them — which is creating its own inefficiency. What's your move?
AGive them another quarter — 8 months isn't enough time to fully assess fit in a difficult role.
BAct on what you know: have the honest conversation, name the gap clearly, determine whether there is a realistic path to the performance the role requires, and if not, begin the transition process.
CRestructure the role to reduce its scope to a level they can perform at.
DAdd a support layer — hire someone junior under them to compensate for the performance gap without changing the leader.
