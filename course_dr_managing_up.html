Foundation
Managing Up Is Strategic, Not Transactional
At the manager level, managing up means keeping your boss informed and getting approvals efficiently. That's transactional managing up — and it's table stakes, not a skill.
At director level, managing up is strategic. You understand your executive's pressures, stakeholders, and priorities well enough to be genuinely useful — not just compliant. You're thinking about their job while doing yours. You surface risks before they become surprises. You align your org's direction with executive intent, especially when intent is ambiguous. You advocate for your team and function in rooms where they're not present.
Directors who master executive relationships get resources, alignment, and organizational air cover. Directors who manage up transactionally get approvals — and not much else.
The goal isn't to impress your exec. It's to make their job easier by being the director who needs the least managing — because you're already doing the work of thinking about what they'd want you to know.
Foundation
Three New Managing-Up Responsibilities at Director Level
These are distinct from what was expected of you as a manager — and they require a different kind of investment in the relationship.
1

Translating Strategy Down

Your exec sets direction; your job is to translate it into something your org can execute. When strategy is ambiguous — which it often is — you interpret it, align your function, and execute without constant clarification. When you need clarification, you ask the right question in the right moment.

2

Surfacing Signals and Risks Early

Executives hate surprises. Your job is to surface risks, early signals of problems, and developments that affect their priorities — before they hear it from someone else. "I wanted to give you a heads-up before this becomes more visible" is one of the most trust-building things a director can say.

3

Advocating for Your Team

Your team needs a champion in conversations they're not part of. When resources, recognition, or organizational decisions are being made, you make the case for the people and the function you lead. You create visibility for work that deserves it. This is a job only you can do for them.

The Most Common Director Managing-Up Failure
Over-briefing. Two slides of background before the recommendation. Three paragraphs of context before the ask. A six-minute setup before the thirty-second point.
Executives filter every communication for three questions: Is this on track? Is there a risk? What do you need from me? When you lead with context before the point, you're making them do the extraction work. You've trained them to wait — impatiently — through your setup before they can assess the signal.
The instinct to build context first comes from a good place — you want them to understand. But at director level, understanding is your job to deliver efficiently, not theirs to extract from your briefing. Lead with the point. Context follows for those who want it.
The 90-second test: If your exec has 90 seconds in a hallway, can you give them everything they need? If not — that's your briefing structure, not the time available.
The Executive Relationship
Four Dimensions of the Executive Relationship
A strong executive relationship requires competence across all four of these — not just the first one.

Informing

Right signal, right time, right format. Early warning on risk. Milestone updates. Clear asks. Your exec should never be surprised by something you knew was coming.

Advocating

Making the case for your team and function in rooms they're not in. Naming their work. Fighting for resources and recognition where it's deserved. Creating visibility for people who built something worth seeing.

Aligning

Ensuring your direction matches executive intent — especially when intent is ambiguous. Asking the right clarifying questions. Checking back when context shifts. Not assuming alignment that hasn't been confirmed.

Challenging

Knowing when and how to push back without damaging the relationship. Bringing data and a better alternative. Disagreeing privately. Committing publicly. Never sandbagging your exec in the room.

The Escalation Judgment Call
One of the most important managing-up skills at director level is knowing what to escalate — and what to handle. Over-escalating makes you look like you can't lead your level. Under-escalating means your exec gets blindsided.
Three-question escalation filter:

1. Would my exec be surprised if this came up at a higher level and they hadn't heard it from me first?

2. Does resolving this require executive authority or resources I don't have?

3. Is this recoverable if I handle it wrong?

If the answer to question 1 or 2 is yes — escalate. If the answer to question 3 is yes and 1 and 2 are no — handle it and update them after.
The goal is to be the director whose judgment on what to escalate is trusted — so your exec knows that when you bring something, it's worth their attention.

Surfacing a Delay Before It Becomes Visible
A key initiative you own is running two weeks behind. The delay is real but recoverable with a resource adjustment. Your exec is presenting the initiative timeline to the executive team in three weeks. What do you do?
AAbsorb the delay internally and try to recover in the next two weeks — no point raising it until you know whether the recovery plan works.
BBrief your exec now: headline (two weeks behind), recovery plan (specific), ask (resource adjustment needed). Give them the full picture before they're in a room presenting an inaccurate timeline.
CSend a brief email update noting the delay and that you're working on a recovery — no need for a full conversation until the plan is finalized.
DAsk your exec's chief of staff how they like to receive delay notifications — better to get the format right before delivering bad news.
The Executive Relationship
Disagreeing with a Direction Already in Motion
Your CPO has made a strategic decision you disagree with — you think it's solving the wrong problem and you have data to support a different approach. The decision was announced publicly two weeks ago and execution has started. What do you do?
AExecute the decision fully while sharing your concerns with your team — you disagree, but they should know where you stand.
BExecute the decision, then request private time with your CPO: share your data and perspective, ask if there's something you're missing, and let them decide how to proceed with full information.
CSlow your team's execution slightly while you build a stronger case — you don't want to be too far in before the decision is reconsidered.
DRaise the concern at the next leadership meeting — transparency with the full team is the right forum for strategic disagreements.

The Hallway Update
You run into your CPO in the hallway and they ask: "How's the platform initiative going?" You have 90 seconds. What do you say?
A"Really well overall — we've been working through some complexity with the vendor but I think we're going to be fine."
B"On track for the March launch. One risk to flag: vendor dependency shifted — I have a recovery option that needs your sign-off. Can I send a one-pager by end of day?"
C"It's been a complex quarter — there's a lot to update you on. Can we get 30 minutes on the calendar this week?"
D"Let me check where we are before I give you an update — I want to make sure I'm giving you accurate numbers."

Advocating for a Resource Before the Room
Budget planning is happening. You need a headcount addition that isn't in the current plan. You know the decision will be made in a leadership meeting you won't be in. How do you position the request?
ASubmit the formal budget request and trust the process — the numbers will speak for themselves.
BBrief your exec before the meeting — share the business case (the problem it solves, the cost of not filling it, the expected return), and make sure they can speak to it confidently if asked in the room.
CAsk to attend the budget meeting so you can present the case yourself — you know it better than anyone.
DBuild alliance with peer directors who might advocate for you — coalition support carries weight in resource discussions.
Practice
Creating Visibility for Your Team
A member of your team built something exceptional — a process improvement that will save the company significant time and money, and demonstrated leadership well above their current level. How do you create visibility for this person at the executive level?
AMention it in your next 1:1 with your exec and let them decide whether it warrants further attention.
BBring it directly into an executive touchpoint: name the person and their work specifically, connect it to a business outcome the exec cares about, and consider whether a brief presentation by the team member directly would be appropriate.
CWrite it up as a team success story and share it org-wide — broader recognition serves the same purpose as executive visibility.
DNominate them for an internal award — formal recognition programs are the right channel for this kind of acknowledgment.

You've finished Managing Up at the Director Level. The directors who manage up well aren't the ones who always agree — they're the ones whose judgment is trusted, whose communication is crisp, and whose team knows they have a champion in the room.

Four commitments for managing up at director level:

Surface risks before surprises — brief your exec before the room, not after

Create executive visibility for the Team Members on your team who have earned it
