Foundation
Structure Is Strategy — Whether You Design It or Not
Most VPs inherit structure and manage within it. A few design structure intentionally. Structure follows strategy — except when it doesn't.
Organizations add structure reactively, one team at a time. The result reflects past priorities, past leaders, past problems — not current strategy.
What structure actually determines: who talks to whom, who has authority to decide what, where information flows, and what teams get credit for. Structure is the invisible architecture of your organization's behavior.
Foundation
What Structure Controls That You Think You Control
VPs often try to solve structural problems with people solutions — more communication, better relationships, clearer expectations. These help. But when the structure is wrong, behavior problems that look like people problems keep coming back.

Symptoms of Structural Misalignment

  • Same escalations repeat despite clear guidance
  • Cross-functional work always requires heroics
  • Strategic priorities owned by no clear team
  • Information arrives at the wrong level too late

What Structure Actually Fixes

  • Decision rights at the right level — permanently
  • Critical collaboration paths made easy, not hard
  • Accountability aligned to strategic priorities
  • Information flowing to where decisions get made
Foundation
Three Triggers That Tell You Structure Needs to Change
Redesign isn't always the right answer. But three signals tell you when it is:
1
Work That Matters Is in Workarounds
Informal coalitions routing around formal structure. If your best work is happening despite the org chart, your org chart is the problem.
2
Strategic Priorities Owned by No One
No team is structured to succeed at the most important goal. When accountability is diffuse, execution is slow and blame is easy.
3
Same Decisions Keep Escalating
Structure isn't creating authority at the right level. If the same class of decisions keeps reaching your desk, decision rights are wrong — not the people.
The Design Framework
Five Organizational Design Questions
Before restructuring anything, run your current org through these five diagnostic questions. Each one surfaces a different class of structural problem.
1
Alignment to Strategy
Does structure align resources to strategic priorities? Or does it reflect the priorities of three leadership regimes ago?
2
Decision Rights
Are decisions made at the right level by the right people? Decision rights determine speed, accountability, and leadership development simultaneously.
3
Collaboration Architecture
Which collaborations are critical? Does structure make them easy or hard? Structure that makes critical collaboration hard will always underperform.
4
Span and Depth
Is the management ratio appropriate for the work type? Too wide and managers can't develop their people. Too narrow and bureaucracy multiplies.
5
Succession Alignment
Does structure create clear development paths? Or does every promotion require a reorganization? Good structure builds the bench as a byproduct.
Foundation
The Redesign VP Most Leaders Skip
Most VPs focus on structure — the boxes and lines. The more difficult redesign is the one that determines who talks to whom, who decides what, and what gets measured.

What Most VPs Redesign

  • Reporting relationships
  • Team names and groupings
  • Headcount allocation
  • Layer count

What Actually Drives Org Behavior

  • Decision rights — who can say yes without asking
  • Information flows — who knows what, when
  • Accountability links — who owns what outcome
  • Collaboration incentives — who benefits from working together
The insight: You can change every box on the org chart and leave the real architecture untouched. Deliberate design addresses decision rights, information flows, and accountability — not just reporting lines.
The Design Framework
Strategy Without a Home
Your organization has a new strategic priority — building a loyalty program that cuts across Guest experience, revenue, and operations. No existing team owns it, and everyone assumes someone else is driving it. Six months in, nothing has moved.
ACreate a cross-functional task force with rotating leadership to ensure all teams feel ownership.
BAssign clear structural ownership to one team or leader — with explicit authority and accountability for the outcome.
CEscalate to your CEO that the priority needs more resources before ownership can be assigned.
DSchedule a quarterly review meeting to increase visibility and social pressure across all three teams.
The Design Framework
The Restructure That Breaks Teams
You need to restructure your org to align with a new strategic direction. The right structure requires moving several strong Team Members under a different leader. You're concerned about disrupting high-performing teams.
AKeep the current structure to preserve team health and find other ways to pursue the strategy.
BDesign the right structure, then invest in a thoughtful transition — clear communication, leader prep, and onboarding support — to protect team health through the change.
CRestructure quickly without announcement to minimize the period of uncertainty.
DAsk the affected Team Members to choose which structure they prefer before deciding.
Practice
The Leader Whose Role Changes
A redesign you're implementing narrows the scope of a well-respected senior leader's role significantly. They haven't been told yet. How do you handle the conversation?
AAnnounce the redesign org-wide first, then have the individual conversation to minimize the awkwardness.
BHave the conversation with the leader before any announcement — explain the strategic rationale, be honest about the scope change, and give them space to respond.
CFrame the scope reduction as a lateral move without acknowledging the change in authority.
DDelay the redesign until you can find a way to keep the leader's role intact.
Practice
Designing a New Function
Your CEO asks you to stand up a new function that doesn't currently exist in the organization. You're designing it from scratch. What's your first structural question?
AHow many headcount can we get approved for this function?
BWhat outcomes is this function accountable for, and what decisions must it own to achieve them?
CWho is the strongest internal candidate to lead it?
DHow do peer organizations in our industry structure this function?
Practice
High Political Resistance
You're proposing a structural change that would consolidate two teams under a single leader. One of the current team leaders is politically influential and is campaigning against the change, though the structural case is clear.
AAbandon the structural change to avoid the political conflict.
BHold the structural decision, invest time with the resistant leader to understand their specific concerns, address what you can, and be clear that the change is moving forward.
CEscalate to the CEO and let them make the call to neutralize the political resistance.
DMake a concession on the resistant leader's scope to reduce opposition and get the change approved.

Course Complete
You've finished Organizational Design. VPs who design structure intentionally create conditions that outlast any single leader — including themselves.

Five commitments of VPs who design well:

Audit structure against strategy annually — not reactively when something breaks.
Fix decision rights before the escalation pattern becomes a crisis.
Design for the collaboration your strategy requires — not the collaboration that's convenient.
Have the hard conversation before any restructure announcement — leaders affected deserve it first.
Name the workarounds. If the informal org is doing the real work, the formal org needs to change.
