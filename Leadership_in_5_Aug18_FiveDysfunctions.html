Why Every Leader Needs This Book

Lencioni doesn't talk about dysfunction the way most leadership books do. He's not describing a toxic culture or a broken organization. He's describing normal teams — teams full of smart, well-meaning people who still manage to underperform, avoid hard conversations, and let individuals win at the expense of the group.

The model is a pyramid. Each dysfunction builds on the one below it. You can't fix accountability if there's no commitment. You can't build commitment if there's no real conflict. And nothing gets off the ground without trust.

This isn't theory. If you've ever led a team, you'll recognize all five within the first 20 pages.

“Not finance. Not strategy. Not technology. It is teamwork that remains the ultimate competitive advantage, both because it is so powerful and so rare.”
— Patrick Lencioni

The Five Dysfunctions — From Bottom to Top

The pyramid starts with the most foundational dysfunction. You can't fix the levels above until you address what's beneath them.

Dysfunction 1

Absence of Trust

The foundation of everything

What It Is

This isn't trust in the "I trust you to do your job" sense. Lencioni means something more vulnerable: the willingness to be open about weaknesses, mistakes, and fears — without the constant calculation of how it looks. On a team without this kind of trust, every conversation carries a hidden performance. People manage perception instead of solving problems.

“

The kind of trust that is necessary to build a great team is what I call vulnerability-based trust. This requires team members to make themselves vulnerable to one another — and be confident that their respective vulnerabilities will not be used against them.

— Patrick Lencioni

Why It Matters for You

When trust is absent, your team's energy goes into self-protection. People work around each other instead of with each other. They don't ask for help. They don't admit when they're in over their heads. They don't flag problems until it's too late.

You'll see this as...

A team that looks functional in meetings — but real issues surface through back channels, not in the room. People agree in public and complain privately. The leader is the last to know what's actually going wrong.

What to Do About It

Model it first. Go first with your own vulnerability — a mistake, a limitation, something you're working on. You can't ask for openness if you're not modeling it.

Create low-stakes practice. Start team meetings with one thing you got wrong this week. The norm shifts fast when the leader makes it normal.

Reward honesty over performance.strong> When someone admits a mistake before it becomes a problem, make that visible. Show that transparency here is safe.

Dysfunction 2

Fear of Conflict

Artificial harmony is not the same as alignment

What It Is

Teams that lack trust can't have real conflict — because real conflict requires vulnerability. So they create artificial harmony: everyone agrees in meetings, debates don't happen, ideas go unchallenged. It looks like a functional team from the outside. Inside, it's a team that's avoiding every conversation that actually needs to happen.

“

Teams that engage in productive conflict know that the only purpose is to produce the best possible solution in the shortest period of time. They discuss and resolve issues more quickly and completely than groups that try to avoid conflict.

— Patrick Lencioni

Why It Matters for You

Good conflict — debate about ideas, challenges to bad plans, honest pushback — is what gets teams to the right answer. When it's avoided, decisions get made based on hierarchy or whoever is most persistent, not what's actually best. And the resentment from ideas that were never heard? That comes out somewhere else.

You'll see this as...

Meetings where everyone agrees — then people grumble afterward in the break room. Important topics that keep getting tabled. A team that defers to the leader on every decision instead of working through it together.

What to Do About It

Separate conflict from disrespect. Model what productive disagreement looks like: “I see it differently — here's why.” Make the debate about the idea, not the person.

Mine for conflict. In meetings, ask: “Who sees this differently?” or “What's the strongest case against this?” Don't let artificial consensus end the conversation too early.

Tolerate discomfort. When a debate gets uncomfortable, resist the urge to end it. Sit in it a little longer. That tension is where the best answers live.

Dysfunction 3

Lack of Commitment

Buy-in without debate is just compliance

What It Is

When teams won't have real conflict, they can't achieve genuine buy-in. People leave meetings having never fully engaged — so they never truly committed. They nod. They say “sounds good.” Then they go execute in whatever direction they personally believe in. Commitment without conflict is compliance. And compliance doesn't hold when things get hard.

“

A team that commits to a decision even when there is little natural agreement trusts that it is better to make a clear decision and be wrong than to make no decision at all. Good teams disagree, debate, and then commit — fully.

— Patrick Lencioni

Why It Matters for You

Lencioni makes a distinction most leaders miss: you don't need consensus to get commitment. You need clarity and the belief that your voice was heard. A team that debates fully and then aligns on a decision — even one they partially disagreed with — will execute better than one that never pushed back at all.

You'll see this as...

Decisions that have to be relitigated over and over. Team members executing in slightly different directions. A sense that “we keep circling back to this.” Plans that look good in meetings and quietly fall apart in execution.

What to Do About It

End every meeting with a clear decision. Not “we'll keep talking about this.” A specific commitment: what's decided, what happens next, who owns it.

Name the uncommitted decision. When something is circling back, say it: “We've talked about this three times. We need to decide and commit today.”

Ask for genuine commitment, not just agreement. “I need everyone to be able to walk out of this room and execute on this — even if it wasn't your first choice. Can you do that?”

Dysfunction 4

Avoidance of Accountability

Peer-to-peer accountability is more powerful than top-down

What It Is

Without clear commitments, there's nothing to hold people to. But this dysfunction runs deeper than follow-through: it's about whether teammates will call each other on poor performance, missed standards, or behavior that's hurting the team. Most won't — not because they don't see it, but because they're uncomfortable. So the standard slowly drifts.

“

The most effective and efficient means of maintaining high standards of performance on a team is peer pressure. If people know their peers are watching and will call them out, they are far less likely to let standards slip.

— Patrick Lencioni

Why It Matters for You

As a leader, you can't be the only one holding the standard. A team where accountability only flows from the top is fragile — it breaks every time you're not watching. The goal is a culture where the team holds itself, and the leader only has to step in for the big stuff.

You'll see this as...

Team members who see a problem and stay quiet because “it's not my place.” Performance that drifts until you catch it. A low hum of frustration from high performers watching others not be held to the same standard.

What to Do About It

Make standards explicit. You can't hold people accountable for standards they've never clearly heard. Say them out loud. Write them down. Name the behavior, not just the outcome.

Give the team permission to hold each other. Explicitly: “If you see someone not following through, I need you to address it — not come to me first.”

Model it as a team act, not a punitive one. Accountability is for the team. Frame it that way: “When we let this slide, we let the whole team down.”

Dysfunction 5

Inattention to Results

When individual wins beat team wins, everyone loses

What It Is

The final dysfunction: when team members care more about their department's success, their personal recognition, or their career advancement than about what the team actually achieves together. It looks like politics. It feels like silos. It sounds like “that's not my area” or “we crushed our numbers” when the broader team didn't.

“

Team members who focus on collective results make the success of their colleagues more important than their own. They celebrate when the team wins — and feel genuine disappointment when it loses. Even when they personally performed well.

— Patrick Lencioni

Why It Matters for You

At Breeze, the team that wins is rarely one person's individual contribution — it's the handoff that worked, the assist that didn't get credit, the teammate who stepped in when it wasn't their job. Lencioni's argument: if you celebrate team results, you get team behavior. If you only celebrate individual wins, you get silos.

You'll see this as...

Departments that “win” individually while the broader operation struggles. Leaders who protect their own numbers while the overall team misses the goal. Recognition that consistently goes to the loudest individual instead of the most valuable collaborator.

What to Do About It

Declare the collective result clearly. Make sure your team knows what winning looks like as a unit — not just for their individual role. Say it often.

Celebrate assists, not just scores. Who made someone else better this week? Who stepped in outside their lane? Recognition shapes behavior.

Call out sub-optimization publicly. When a department “wins” in a way that hurts the broader team, name it — without blame. “We got our number, but the full team didn't land where we needed. That's a team loss.”

Your Team Diagnostic

Read each situation. Click the one that feels most true about your team right now. This isn't a test — it's a mirror.

Which of these sounds most like your team?

Dysfunction 1: Absence of Trust. Start here. Trust is the foundation — nothing else can be fixed until people are willing to be real with each other. Your move: model vulnerability first. Go first. Show the team it's safe.
Dysfunction 2: Fear of Conflict. Artificial harmony is costing you the real conversations. The ideas your team won't say in the room are the ones you need most. Create permission for productive pushback — and mine for it deliberately.
Dysfunction 3: Lack of Commitment. If you're re-deciding what was already decided, the original decision didn't have genuine buy-in. End your next meeting with a named, committed decision — even if it's imperfect. Then execute.
Dysfunction 4: Avoidance of Accountability. Someone is watching the standard slip and staying quiet. Until the team is willing to hold each other — not just wait for you — performance will keep drifting. Name it. Give them permission to address it directly.
Dysfunction 5: Inattention to Results. The scoreboard is wrong — it's tracking the wrong wins. Make sure your team knows what the collective result is, and then celebrate the behavior that actually produces it: assists, handoffs, stepping in.

The Five Levels — Your One-Glance Summary

5. Inattention to Results — team wins matter more than individual wins
4. Avoidance of Accountability — the team holds the standard, not just the leader
3. Lack of Commitment — debate fully, then commit completely
2. Fear of Conflict — productive debate produces better outcomes
1. Absence of Trust — vulnerability is the foundation of everything

Every team has at least one of these. The question isn't whether — it's which one to work on first. You already know. You've known for a while.
