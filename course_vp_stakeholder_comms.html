Foundation
The Ceiling of Internal Excellence
A VP who is excellent with their own team but ineffective with external stakeholders has built a ceiling — and they've built it out of the best materials. They are respected by the people they manage. They are not effective with the people they need. And at VP level, the most important work almost always happens at the intersection of organizations, not within them.
Cross-functional initiatives require peer VP alignment. Strategic investment requires board credibility. Org-wide changes require CEO confidence. Influence over shared resources requires relationships with leaders who have no obligation to prioritize your needs.
The VP stakeholder test: Can you get a significant cross-functional initiative approved, funded, and moved when you have no direct authority over the people you need? If not, stakeholder influence is the constraint — not strategy, not execution.
Foundation
VP Stakeholder Failure Patterns
Three failure patterns that are specific enough to name — not general communication failures, but patterns that emerge specifically at VP level with external stakeholders.

Over-Presenting to the Board

  • Bringing detailed operational information to a strategic audience
  • Defending rather than narrating
  • Not reading the room on what the board is actually asking
  • Treating it like a performance review rather than a strategic dialogue

Under-Communicating to Peers

  • Only engaging peer VPs when you need something
  • Not investing in peer relationships during low-stakes periods
  • Assuming alignment that hasn't been worked
  • Raising issues in the room rather than pre-working them

Managing Up Through Formal Channels Only

  • Waiting for 1:1s and formal reviews to communicate with the CEO
  • No proactive signal between scheduled touchpoints
  • CEO develops a VP impression through absence of signal, not through intentional relationship

Pre-Working: The Most Underused VP Skill
The most common mistake VPs make in high-stakes stakeholder communication is going into the room cold — without having pre-worked the key players.
Pre-working is not lobbying. It's not back-channeling. It's understanding each stakeholder's priority, sharing your key message or ask in advance, and surfacing objections before they become room dynamics that work against you.
The pre-work principle: Every significant ask, recommendation, or decision should have at least one pre-working conversation before it enters the formal setting. "I wanted to share this thinking with you before the meeting — does anything concern you?" is VP-level stakeholder communication. It builds the relationship, surfaces real objections, and prevents the meeting from becoming the first time someone engages with your idea.
VPs who show up having done this work operate at a fundamentally different effectiveness level than those who don't.
The Stakeholder Map
Three Stakeholder Levels
Different stakeholder levels have fundamentally different needs from VP communication. Treating them the same is the most common stakeholder communication mistake.
Tier 1
Board / Executive Level
Needs: Strategic narrative, honest risk assessment, decision-readiness. They are evaluating whether you understand the business context and can be trusted with significant decisions — not whether you know the operational details.
Failure pattern: Over-presenting detail, defending operationally, not getting to the recommendation.
Tier 2
Peer VP Level
Needs: Mutual priority understanding, pre-worked alignment, relationship deposits made before withdrawal moments. They are evaluating whether you're a good partner — easy to work with, aligned on shared org interests, trustworthy in their priorities.
Failure pattern: Engaging only when you need something, surprising them in the room, competing rather than partnering.
Tier 3
Cross-Functional Directors
Needs: Clear expectations, strategic context, recognition of their team's contributions. They are evaluating whether you're worth their team's effort — do you understand what you're asking for, and do you value what they deliver?
Failure pattern: Treating as operational resources, not recognizing team contributions, unclear expectations.
Foundation
Trust as Stakeholder Currency
Trust is the currency that all stakeholder work runs on — and it's built slowly, spent quickly, and very difficult to rebuild once it's damaged. VP stakeholders evaluate trust along two dimensions consistently:

Competence Trust

  • Do you know your function deeply?
  • Do you bring sound recommendations?
  • Are you honest about what you know vs. don't know?
  • Do your plans hold up to scrutiny?

Character Trust

  • Are you straight with me even when it's uncomfortable?
  • Do you follow through on what you say?
  • Do you protect my interests when I'm not in the room?
  • Can I disagree with you and work with you after?
The trust asymmetry: Competence trust gets you in the room. Character trust keeps you there. A VP who is highly competent but low in character trust will be used but not relied on — and will never become a decision-maker in significant moments.

Preparing a Board Presentation
You're preparing a presentation for the board on your function's performance and strategic direction. The session is 30 minutes. You've been given a template with 12 slides of operational data. You have freedom to modify.
How do you approach this?
AComplete all 12 slides as provided — the template exists for a reason and consistency across board presentations helps the board compare across functions.
BRestructure to 5–6 slides: strategic context, 3 key themes, honest risk assessment, decision you need from them. Lead with the strategic narrative, use operational data selectively to support — not as the presentation.
CLead with the operational data to establish credibility — board members make better strategic decisions when they have the operational context first.
DAsk what the board most wants to see and build from there — responding to their priorities is the most credibility-building approach.

A Peer VP Blocking a Cross-Functional Initiative
You need cross-functional support from a peer VP to advance a significant initiative. That VP has raised concerns — some substantive, some that read as protecting their function's resources. They haven't said no, but they're not moving forward. The timeline is tight.
What's your move?
AEscalate to the CEO — this is a cross-functional alignment issue that requires executive authority to resolve.
BRequest a direct 1:1 to understand their specific concerns and interests — work toward a solution that addresses both their needs and yours before escalating.
CMove forward on the parts of the initiative you can control independently — create momentum that makes their support more likely.
DBring the issue to the next executive team meeting — peer accountability in the leadership team will move it faster than bilateral conversation.
Practice
CEO Skeptical of Your Recommendation
You've brought a significant recommendation to the CEO. They've expressed genuine skepticism — not the polite kind, but substantive pushback on your rationale. The meeting is still active. You believe your recommendation is right.
What's the most effective response in the room?
ADefend your recommendation clearly — CEOs respect conviction and this is not the moment to appear uncertain.
BAsk what specifically concerns them, listen to understand the actual objection, then respond to that — not to the surface challenge.
COffer to go back and revisit your analysis — deferring to the CEO on a recommendation they're skeptical about shows appropriate humility.
DFind common ground immediately — identify what parts of the recommendation they agree with and build from there.
Practice
Recovering Credibility After a Stakeholder Communication Failure
A board presentation did not go well. You over-prepared on operational data and under-prepared on strategic narrative. Two board members asked questions you weren't expecting and you were visibly uncertain. You've received feedback that the presentation "didn't land with confidence." You have another board session in 60 days.
What's the right recovery approach?
APrepare more thoroughly for the next session — more data, more coverage of potential questions, more rehearsal.
BHave a direct conversation with a trusted board member before the next session — understand what they needed, share how you're thinking about the next one differently, rebuild the relationship proactively.
CDeliver an excellent next session — the best recovery from a credibility gap is strong performance, not conversation about past performance.
DAsk the CEO to help set lower-stakes expectations for the next board session while you rebuild confidence.
Practice
Building Relationship with a Difficult-to-Engage Board Member
There's a board member who asks sharp questions, rarely seems satisfied with answers, and has a reputation for being hard to read. You interact with them in board sessions only. You sense they are not yet convinced of your credibility and have significant influence over the board's view of your function.
What's your approach?
AContinue performing well in board sessions — credibility with this board member will come through consistently strong presentations, not relationship building.
BRequest a brief informal conversation outside the formal setting — "I'd value your perspective on how I can make our board sessions most useful for you." Build the relationship before the next evaluation moment.
CAsk the CEO or board chair for context on what this board member values — use that intelligence to tailor your next presentation.
DAvoid giving the board member anything that could be challenged — focus your presentation on the areas where your function is performing strongest.
