Foundation
The Question Vision Actually Answers
Vision is not the strategic plan. It's not the aspirational values statement. It's not the annual all-hands tagline. Vision is the answer to one deeply human question that every Team Member is implicitly asking about their work: Does what I do matter, and is it going somewhere?
When the answer to that question is clear and real, people bring more. They tolerate difficulty more. They make better tradeoffs between short-term comfort and long-term direction. They tell the story of their work to others in ways that attract talent and build credibility.
When the answer is unclear or absent, people optimize locally. They execute well without connecting execution to meaning. They leave not because their work is bad but because the work doesn't feel like it's part of something worth building toward.
VP vision responsibility: You are the primary architect of the answer to that question for hundreds of people. Not the only voice — but the most visible one. What vision are you casting, and is it landing?
Foundation
What Makes Vision Fail
Most vision statements fail for predictable reasons — not because leadership doesn't care, but because the common practices of vision communication are structurally weak.

Vision That Fails

  • Abstract — "best in class," "world-class," "excellence"
  • Inconsistent — different framing in every setting
  • Disconnected — not connected to daily work
  • Infrequent — only repeated at the annual all-hands
  • Organizational — describes the org, not the people doing the work

Vision That Works

  • Concrete — specific enough to know if you've arrived
  • Consistent — same framing in every setting, every quarter
  • Connected — Team Members can trace their daily work to it
  • Repeated — in every meeting, every message, every recognition moment
  • Personal — "the work we're doing" not just "the strategy"

The Vision Transmission Problem
The most common vision failure is not a weak vision — it's a transmission failure. The VP has a clear picture of where the organization is going. That picture exists clearly in the VP's mind and is occasionally shared. But it never reaches the manager three levels down with enough clarity to be useful to that manager's team.
The test: Ask someone three layers below you: "What are we trying to build in the next two years?" If the answer is blank, or generic, or disconnected from what you actually believe — you have a transmission problem, not a vision problem.
Transmission problems happen because:

Why Vision Doesn't Transmit

  • VP communicates the vision once and moves on
  • Each level interprets and adapts rather than translating
  • Vision is not connected to day-to-day decisions managers make
  • No mechanism to test whether it has landed

How to Fix It

  • Repeat the same vision framing in every setting
  • Give managers language to translate it to their team context
  • Connect vision to decisions being made right now
  • Ask regularly whether it's landing — and listen to the answer
The Vision Framework
Four Vision Components
A vision that transmits at scale is built from four components — each addressing a different question Team Members are asking.
Component 1
The Picture
Concrete and vivid — specific enough that you'd know if you'd arrived. Not "excellence" but "an organization where every Guest interaction leaves them more likely to fly with us again, and every Team Member knows how their work created that outcome."
Test: Can someone who hears it visualize specifically what success looks like?
Component 2
The Purpose
Why it matters — to the organization, to Guests, and to the people doing the work. Purpose is what connects "what we're building" to "why this is worth building." Without it, the picture is a destination without a reason to care about arriving.
Test: Can a Team Member explain why their work matters to someone outside the organization?
Component 3
The Path
High-level narrative of how you'll get there — not the full strategic plan, but enough direction that Team Members understand what the next chapter of the journey looks like and what their role in it is.
Test: Does the team have a shared understanding of the next 12–18 months of movement toward the picture?
Component 4
The Connection
How this vision connects to each person's specific work. This is the translation layer — VP vision cannot transmit at scale without managers and directors who can make the connection between the organizational vision and the daily work of their teams.
Test: Can each Team Member state how their work contributes to the picture?
Foundation
Testing Whether Vision Is Landing
The only meaningful test of vision is whether it has reached the people who are supposed to be guided by it. Feeling confident about the vision you've articulated is not the test. Saying it again is not the test. The test is what happens three levels down.
The skip-level vision test: Ask someone three layers below you: "What are we trying to build in the next two years?" Listen carefully. Where the signal breaks down is exactly where to intervene — not with more vision communication to the org, but with better translation support for the layer where clarity ends.
Common findings from this test:

When Vision Is Landing

  • Team Member answers with specific language that matches VP framing
  • They can connect their daily work to the larger picture
  • They express ownership — "we're trying to…" not "the company says…"

When Vision Isn't Landing

  • Blank, generic, or "whatever the company priorities are" responses
  • Vision described in terms of metrics rather than meaning
  • Team Member can't connect their work to any larger picture

Recasting Vision for a Team That's Lost Momentum
Your organization has been through 18 months of high change with significant wins and significant losses. Team Member engagement is declining. In interviews and skip-levels, you hear "I'm not sure what we're building anymore." The direction has changed multiple times in response to business conditions. You need to recast vision in a way that is credible — not performative.
What's the right approach?
ACreate a compelling vision deck and present it at the next all-hands — a well-designed vision presentation will rebuild clarity and energy.
BAcknowledge what the last 18 months have been before recasting — validate the difficulty, name what was built and what was lost, then offer a clear and specific picture of where you're headed and why it's worth going there.
CFocus on wins from the period to rebuild confidence — momentum comes from recognizing what worked before it comes from what's next.
DHave directors communicate the renewed vision in their team settings — the vision will land better in small groups than in a large formal setting.
The Vision Framework
Translating Org-Level Vision to Functional Level
The company-level vision is powerful at the organizational level but abstract for your function. Team Members in your org hear "be the most Guest-centric airline" but don't connect it to the operational or technical work they do. You need to create a functional-level vision that connects to the company vision without being redundant or disconnected.
What's your approach?
AAdopt the company vision directly for your function — consistency with the enterprise vision is more important than functional specificity.
BBuild a functional vision that answers: "What does our specific work enable the Guest-centric airline vision to be?" — connect the enterprise picture to the specific contribution your function makes.
CLet each director create their own vision for their team — they're closest to the work and best positioned to make the connection meaningful.
DFocus on operational excellence as the functional vision — your team can understand and rally around being the best at what they do.
Practice
Vision in Conflict with CEO Priorities
You've been building toward a vision you've articulated clearly and repeatedly for your function. A new CEO priority shifts resource and attention in a direction that appears to conflict with the vision you've been casting. Team Members are noticing the tension.
What do you do?
AContinue communicating the original vision — temporary priority shifts don't change the long-term direction.
BName the tension explicitly: "You may be noticing a shift in what we're focused on right now. Here's how I see this priority connecting to the direction we've been building toward — and here's where it may require us to update our picture."
CRecast the vision to align fully with the CEO priority — organizational alignment is more important than functional vision consistency.
DAvoid addressing the tension publicly — discussing conflicts between VP and CEO priorities reduces Team Member confidence in leadership alignment.
Practice
Communicating Vision Through Managers
You've articulated your vision clearly in multiple all-hands sessions. But skip-level feedback reveals that Team Members three levels down are describing "the company's priorities" in ways that don't reflect the specific vision you've been casting. Directors are communicating priorities effectively but not vision. What do you do?
AIncrease the frequency of all-hands vision communication — the issue is repetition, and more direct VP communication will solve it.
BInvest in enabling your director layer to translate and communicate the vision in their own team settings — give them the language, the framing, and explicit expectation that they are the vision transmission mechanism.
CSend a written vision statement to all Team Members directly — bypassing the management layer removes the translation distortion.
DSimplify the vision to a single memorable sentence so translation across layers is easier and less likely to be distorted.
Practice
Creating Vision for a High-Change Team
Your team has been through so much change that any vision you cast gets mentally filed as "we'll see." Team Members have seen strategies come and go. They're skeptical of vision statements — they've heard them before and seen them abandoned. You need to cast a vision that is different in kind from what they've experienced, not just in content.
What makes this vision land differently?
AMake it more aspirational and inspiring — the previous visions were too operational and didn't create enough emotional connection.
BMake it more concrete, acknowledge the history honestly, commit to repeating it consistently rather than pivoting, and connect it to decisions being made right now — so it's experienced as real rather than declared.
CInvolve the team in building the vision — co-creation creates the ownership that top-down vision casting can't achieve after multiple failed iterations.
DDefer on vision until the current change period stabilizes — casting vision during instability creates credibility risk when events require adjustment.

Course Complete
You've finished Casting Vision. The VPs whose organizations feel direction and purpose aren't better visionaries — they're better transmitters. They repeat, connect, test, and adjust until the picture lives three levels down, not just in their own mind.

Your vision casting commitments:

State your vision in one clear, concrete sentence — and repeat it deliberately in every setting this quarter
Create one mechanism to test whether vision is landing three levels down — and listen honestly to what you find
Enable your director layer to translate the vision to their team context — give them the language, not just the picture
Connect vision to decisions being made right now — make the picture relevant to the present, not just the future
When vision and priorities appear to conflict, name the tension directly — credibility comes from honesty, not from pretending the conflict doesn't exist
