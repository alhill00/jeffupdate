Foundation
The Manager Who Burned Bright
He was a high performer. First in, last out. Responded to messages at 10pm. Set the tone: if you care about this work, you show up like I do. His team matched the energy — for a while. They answered messages on weekends. They worked through their PTO. They admired his commitment.
Then his best people started leaving. The ones who stayed were exhausted. He'd built a culture of heroics — and when the heroes ran out of fuel, the team collapsed. He thought he was modeling excellence. He was actually modeling unsustainability.
"Sustainable" isn't about working less. It's about managing energy — not just time — and modeling a pace your team can actually maintain over years, not weeks.
Foundation
What Leaders Model, Teams Absorb
You set the culture of work by what you do — not what you say. If you answer Slack messages at 11pm, your team learns that 11pm messages are expected. If you skip lunch to power through, you signal that skipping lunch is dedication. If you praise overtime, you are programming overtime as the standard.
This isn't about individual choices. It's about what you're communicating — intentionally or not — every single day. Your behavior is a policy signal for your entire team.
The most dangerous unsustainable leaders aren't the ones who burn themselves out. They're the ones who build teams in their image — and then wonder why turnover is high and engagement is low.
Foundation
Energy Management, Not Time Management
Time is fixed — 24 hours, same for everyone. Energy is renewable — but only if you manage it. The difference between sustainable and unsustainable leaders isn't how many hours they work. It's whether they protect the conditions that let them perform well over time.
Context-switching drains energy faster than volume. Reactive mode drains faster than proactive mode. Saying yes to everything drains faster than saying no with context. The goal isn't to do less — it's to operate from a full tank more often.
A depleted leader makes worse decisions, gives worse feedback, and models worse behavior — regardless of how many hours they're logging. Energy is the real performance variable.
The Drain Cycle
The Drain-Restore Cycle
Sustainable leadership means understanding what drains your energy — and what restores it — and managing both deliberately. Leaders who stay in drain mode eventually burn out or burn out their teams.
🔻
Drain Mode
Always reactive — responding to whatever comes in
Constant context-switching with no recovery
Always available — no boundaries on time or communication
Saying yes to everything, building resentment
Praising heroics and overtime as dedication
🔋
Restore Mode
Protected deep work blocks — proactive, not reactive
Deliberate transitions between tasks and contexts
Defined availability — clear off-hours by practice, not policy
Saying no with context — "not this week" with a reason
Modeling sustainable pace as the visible standard
The cycle is a choice — made repeatedly. Every time you model restore mode, you give your team permission to do the same.
Foundation
Visibility Culture and the Always-On Trap
Turning off Slack notifications on weekends isn't a personal preference — it's a policy signal. When you visibly disconnect, you tell your team it's safe to disconnect too. When you visibly stay on, you tell them the opposite — even if you never say a word about it.
The "always-on" trap happens when availability becomes the measure of commitment. It's not. Sustainable leaders separate their availability from their accountability. They're accountable for outcomes — not for responding to messages at all hours.
Model the behavior you want to see. If you want your team to take real breaks, take real breaks. If you want them to leave on time, leave on time. The most powerful sustainability message you can send is your own calendar.

The Drain Cycle
Scenario: The Stakeholder Push
A senior stakeholder asks your team to deliver a project in half the usual time. Your team is already at capacity. You need to respond. What do you do?
AAgree — stakeholders should get what they ask for and your team can find a way.
BPush back with options: "We can do this timeline if we deprioritize X, or we can do the full version in the normal timeline — which matters more to you?"
CAgree, then ask the team to work extra hours to make it happen — don't burden the stakeholder with your capacity issues.
DDecline firmly — your team's sustainability matters more than any one stakeholder request.
Practice
Scenario: The Manager Who Doesn't Protect
Your leader keeps adding work to your team's plate without removing anything. Your team is stretched. You have a 1:1 coming up. What's your approach?
ASay nothing — managing up is uncomfortable and your leader probably knows best.
BName the capacity issue with specifics: "We've taken on three new priorities in the last month. Here's where we are — I need help triaging what to deprioritize."
CTell your team to just push through — that's what leadership expects right now.
DFilter what you share with your team so they don't get overwhelmed by the pressure from above.
Practice
Scenario: The High-Output Trap
Your team has been in "sprint mode" for three months. Results are good. But you notice people are quieter, taking more sick days, and responding more slowly than they used to. What do you do first?
AKeep going — results are good and the work still needs to get done.
BName what you're seeing, acknowledge the sprint, and build in a deliberate recovery period before the next push.
COffer one team event to boost morale and keep the current pace going.
DAdd more structure and accountability — the signals might mean some people are coasting.
Practice
Scenario: The Protected Block
You want to protect one hour of deep work each morning for focused planning. But your calendar fills up with meetings from 8am on, and people message you expecting immediate responses. What's the most effective approach?
ATry harder to find the time — self-discipline is the answer.
BBlock it on your calendar visibly, set a response expectation with your team, and hold it consistently — even imperfectly.
CWait until things calm down — right now isn't the right time to protect anything.
DAccept that managers don't get deep work time — that's the role.
