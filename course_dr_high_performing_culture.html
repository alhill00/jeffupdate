Foundation
Culture Is Not on the Wall
Culture is not a set of values on a wall. It's the answer to "how do things actually get done here?" — what behaviors are rewarded, what gets overlooked, what's tolerated under pressure.
Directors shape culture not through declarations but through daily decisions. The culture you have is the culture you've been building, whether you meant to or not.
The real definition: Culture is the pattern of behavior your team has learned to expect — from each other, and from you.
Foundation
Stated Culture vs. Lived Culture
Most organizations have both. Stated: we value collaboration, transparency, accountability. Lived: whoever moves fastest wins, real decisions happen in private, accountability is selective.
Directors are the translation layer — their behavior converts stated values into lived norms. When a director's actions match the posted values, the culture is coherent. When they don't, the lived culture wins every time.
The gap is the work. The distance between what your organization says it values and what it actually rewards is your leadership agenda.
Foundation
Why Culture Change Is Hard
Culture is self-reinforcing — the existing norms select for people who fit them and filter out those who don't. People who thrive under the current culture advocate for its continuation, often without realizing it.
Changing culture at a director level requires three things happening simultaneously: changing the model (what you visibly reward), changing the stories (what you repeat and celebrate), and changing the consequences (what you tolerate vs. address).
The mistake: Trying to change culture through communication alone. Announcing new values without changing behavior creates cynicism — people learn that the stated culture is performative.
The Culture Levers
Five Levers Directors Control
Culture isn't shaped by one big decision — it's shaped by hundreds of small ones. These are the five levers in your hands every day.
1
What You Reward
Public recognition, promotions, resources you advocate for — these teach the team what's actually valued.
Example: Who gets recognized in all-hands? What behavior does it reinforce?
2
What You Tolerate
Which behaviors you allow to continue without consequence — the most powerful and most unconscious lever.
Example: A missed commitment with no follow-up teaches that commitments are optional.
3
What You Model
The pace, tone, communication style, and standards you personally demonstrate every day.
Example: If you reply to emails at 11 PM, you're signaling an expectation — whether you intend to or not.
4
What Stories You Tell
Which wins and lessons you repeat, who gets celebrated and why — stories encode the culture's values.
Example: Do your stories celebrate speed, or quality? Individual heroics, or team coordination?
5
What You Measure
What you pay attention to in reviews, check-ins, and planning — what gets measured gets managed.
Example: If you only review outputs, not how they were achieved, process doesn't matter.
Foundation
The Hardest Lever: Tolerance
What you allow to continue is a cultural statement whether you intend it or not. A missed deadline with no consequence teaches that deadlines are optional. An aggressive team member who hits their numbers teaches that results justify the method.
Leaders who shape culture explicitly decide what they will and won't tolerate — and act accordingly, consistently. Inconsistent enforcement is worse than no standard at all. It teaches the team that the rule applies selectively, which destroys trust in the whole system.
The test: What behavior have you been aware of for more than two weeks that you haven't addressed? That's your tolerance signal — and it's already shaping your culture.

The High-Performer Who Divides the Team
One of your highest-performing managers consistently gets results but cuts corners on cross-functional collaboration — other teams complain about them. You've had informal conversations but nothing has changed. What do you do?
ALet it continue — results are results, and they're delivering.
BHave a direct, consequential conversation about the specific behavior and what changes, with clarity about what happens if it doesn't.
CReassign them to a role with less cross-functional exposure so the friction stops.
DRaise it with HR before addressing it yourself.

Building Peer Accountability
Your team is building a new quarterly operating rhythm. You want to establish a norm of peer accountability — managers holding each other to commitments, not just holding their teams accountable. How do you introduce this?
AAnnounce it as a new expectation in the next director meeting.
BModel it yourself first — hold your own commitments visibly, acknowledge when you don't, and then name what you're doing and invite the norm explicitly.
CAdd accountability as a value to the team's operating principles document.
DBuild peer accountability into performance reviews so it's formally measured.

Morale Is Low — What's the Move?
A manager on your team reports that their team's morale is low — people feel like their work doesn't matter. What's the most direct lever to pull?
AHost a team event to boost morale and connection.
BConnect the work to outcomes — tell the stories of impact, specifically and regularly, until people can see how what they do changes something that matters.
CSurvey the team and build a formal action plan from the data.
DReassign the manager to see if a new leader changes the energy.
Practice
Two Very Different Team Cultures
You discover that two of your managers have very different team cultures — one is psychologically safe and high-accountability, one is tightly controlled and low-trust. They're getting similar results today. Why does this matter, and what do you do?
AIt doesn't matter if results are equivalent — different styles work for different people.
BThe low-trust team is a retention and succession risk — coach the manager on building psychological safety and appropriate autonomy.
CMerge the teams to balance out the culture through proximity.
DWait — if results stay even, intervention isn't warranted yet.
Practice
Building a Learning Culture
You want to establish a learning culture — normalizing experimentation and learning from failure. Your org currently treats failure as a sign of poor judgment. What's the first move?
ACreate a formal post-mortem process so failures are systematically reviewed.
BPublicly acknowledge a decision you made that didn't work and what you learned from it.
CAnnounce that failure is now acceptable and part of the learning process.
DReward the first team that runs a failed experiment to signal the new norm.

Course Complete
You've finished Building a High-Performing Culture. The culture you lead is the culture you're building — in every conversation, every decision, and everything you let slide.

Commitments for culture-building directors:

Name one behavior you've been tolerating and address it this week — directly and specifically.
Tell a story in your next team interaction that reinforces the culture you want — specific, real, recent.
When something fails, debrief with curiosity before moving on — model that learning is safe.
Develop your managers' culture-building skills — not just their technical performance.
Be the first to demonstrate the norm you want your team to adopt.
