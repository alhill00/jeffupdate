Foundation
Two Managers. Two Problems.
Manager A involves the team in every decision — meeting format, vendor selection, scheduling, priorities. She's inclusive and collaborative. Her team is increasingly burned out on consensus-seeking and can't tell who's actually in charge.
Manager B makes every call himself — fast, confident, and rarely with input from anyone. His team is disengaged. They've stopped offering ideas because they've learned their input doesn't matter.
Neither is a decision-making failure. Both are a calibration failure. The skill isn't deciding more or less collaboratively — it's knowing which type of decision is in front of you and responding accordingly.
Not every decision needs the same process. The manager who over-consults on low-stakes calls and under-consults on high-stakes ones is making the same mistake from two different directions.
Foundation
The Decision Quality Gap
Most managers make decisions the same way regardless of stakes. The result is a predictable mismatch between the weight of the decision and the process applied to it.

Low-Stakes Decisions Get Over-Processed

Meeting formats, communication tools, small schedule changes — these get brought to the team, debated, and consensus-sought. The cost: speed, energy, and a team that stops trusting you to make calls.

High-Stakes Decisions Get Rushed

Irreversible decisions — hiring, restructuring, changing how work is assigned — sometimes get made fast because they feel urgent or because the manager thinks they know the answer. The cost: missed perspectives and downstream resistance.

The fix is classification, not style. Before deciding how to decide, classify the decision. Then apply the right process for that type.
Foundation
What Makes a Decision Hard
Decisions feel hard for different reasons — and the reason determines the right response.

Ambiguity

No clear best option. Multiple approaches could work. The answer depends on factors that are genuinely uncertain.

Stakes

Hard to reverse. Consequential for people, relationships, or direction. Getting it wrong is costly to fix.

Politics

Other people have strong preferences and will be affected by the outcome. Their buy-in matters for execution.

Time

Not enough of it to gather all the information you'd want. You have to decide before certainty is available.

Different combinations require different approaches. A low-stakes decision with time pressure → decide fast. A high-stakes ambiguous decision with politics → deliberate, consult, document.
The Decision Framework
Four Decision Types — and RACI-Light
Classify before you decide. The type tells you the process.
Type 1

Reversible + Low Stakes

Easy to undo. Low cost of being wrong.

→ Decide fast, alone, move. Wrong? Fix it.
Type 2

Reversible + Higher Stakes

Can be undone, but wrong choices have meaningful cost.

→ Consult, decide, communicate reasoning.
Type 3

Irreversible + High Stakes

Hard to undo. Consequential for people or direction.

→ Deliberate, document reasoning, broader input.
Type 4

Ambiguous + Urgent

No clear answer and time is short.

→ Time-box it. Best available info. Clear owner. Decide.

Foundation
Three Decision Failure Modes
Most decision problems aren't about making the wrong choice. They're about using the wrong process for the type of decision in front of you.

Over-Consultation

Seeking consensus on Type 1 decisions because it feels inclusive. Slows the org. Trains the team that everything needs group buy-in. Creates a culture of decision paralysis in managers who report to you.

Under-Consultation

Making Type 3 decisions unilaterally because you're confident in the answer. Misses critical perspectives. Creates resistance during execution from people who had something useful to contribute and weren't asked.

Decision Avoidance

Waiting for certainty that will never fully arrive. "I need more information before I decide" on Type 4 decisions. The team stalls. Momentum is lost. Decide on the best available information and set a review trigger if needed.

When you don't have all the information: name your confidence level, decide on best available data, set a clear trigger for revisiting — "if X happens, we'll reconsider."

The Meeting Format Question
You need to decide on a new team meeting format — weekly vs biweekly, in-person vs hybrid. It affects the whole team but isn't high stakes. How do you handle it?
ABring it to the full team and decide by consensus — it affects everyone so everyone should weigh in.
BMake the call yourself, announce it, and commit to revisiting after a month if it isn't working.
CSurvey the team on their preferences and then make a data-informed decision.
DAsk each person individually before deciding — that way everyone feels heard.
The Decision Framework
Restructuring How Work Gets Assigned
You're about to change how work is assigned across your team — a shift that will affect everyone's day-to-day and is hard to reverse once implemented. How do you approach the decision?
AMake the call and announce it — you've thought it through and implementation needs to be clean.
BConsult the team on their input before deciding, then communicate the decision and your reasoning.
CPut it to a team vote — if it affects everyone, everyone should decide.
DDelay until you have more data on the downstream impacts.
Practice
When the Team Can't Agree
You've consulted your team on a meaningful decision and they're split — strong opinions on both sides with no clear consensus emerging. What do you do?
AKeep the conversation going until consensus emerges — the team needs to own this decision.
BMake the call — you heard the input, you understand the trade-offs, and continued deliberation isn't adding value. Communicate the reasoning clearly.
CGo with the majority position — whoever has more people on their side wins.
DEscalate to your leader — this level of disagreement needs someone above you to resolve it.
Practice
Communicating a Solo Decision
You made a Type 1 decision without team input — fast, appropriate for the stakes. Now you need to communicate it. What's the right approach?
AJust announce the decision — no explanation needed for small calls.
BShare the decision and the reasoning briefly — what you decided, why, and what you'd revisit if it doesn't work.
CAsk the team for feedback on the decision after you've announced it.
DApologize for not including them and commit to involving them next time.
Practice
When You Were Wrong
You made a decision that turned out to be wrong. The team is affected and frustrated. How do you recover?
AStick with the decision — reversing course too quickly signals lack of conviction.
BName what happened, own the outcome, explain what you'll do differently, and reverse or revise the decision if it's the right call.
CExplain why the decision made sense at the time — help the team understand your reasoning.
DAsk the team what they think should happen next and follow their recommendation.

Course Complete
You've finished Decision Making. The best decision-makers aren't always right — they're calibrated. They know which process to use, they decide, and they own the outcome.

What sharp decision-making managers do differently:

Classify decisions before making them — type determines process.
Reserve consensus-seeking for Type 3 decisions — not Type 1.
For Type 1 decisions, decide fast, communicate the reasoning, and revisit if needed.
Communicate the reasoning behind decisions, not just the decision itself.
Name who is Accountable before the decision is made — confusion about ownership creates the most decision delays.
