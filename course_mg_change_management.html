Foundation
The Reorg Nobody Left Because Of
Leadership announced a reorg. The new structure made sense — clearer ownership, better alignment to strategy, fewer handoffs. Leadership was energized. The announcement went out with a deck and a FAQ document.
Three weeks later, three Team Members had updated their resumes. Not because the change was bad. Because of how it was handled — announced, explained, and expected to be accepted. Their concerns were never surfaced. Their losses were never named.
Change management isn't about the change. It's about the people experiencing it.
The best-designed change fails when the human side is managed as an afterthought. Your job as a manager isn't to implement the change — it's to bring your people through it.
Foundation
Resistance Isn't the Problem
Most change resistance isn't about the change itself. It's about loss.

What People Lose in Change

  • Familiar routines and structures
  • Sense of control over their work
  • Relationships built over time
  • Identity tied to "how we do things here"

What This Looks Like

  • Skepticism about whether it will work
  • Frustration with the pace or process
  • Withdrawal from engagement
  • Outspoken pushback in meetings
Resistance is a sign that people cared about what existed before. Teams that don't resist change don't resist because they're adaptable — they resist because they were never invested in the first place.
Foundation
What Managers Get Wrong
Three patterns show up repeatedly when managers struggle to lead change effectively.

Announcing Without Context

"Here's what's changing" without "here's why it's happening and what we know and don't know" creates a vacuum that anxiety fills.

Rushing to Execution

Expecting the team to be in Phase 4 (Commitment) before they've worked through Phase 2–3. Skipping the emotional process doesn't accelerate it — it delays it.

Treating Resistance as a Performance Issue

When someone is frustrated or skeptical, responding with performance pressure ("I need you to get on board") shuts down the dialogue and drives the resistance underground — where it's harder to address.

You can't force Phase 4. You can create the conditions for it — by acknowledging the loss, surfacing the concerns, and being honest about what you know and don't know.
The Change Curve
Four Phases — and Your Role in Each
The Change Curve (adapted from Kübler-Ross) maps how people typically move through transition. Your job is to recognize where each person is and respond accordingly.
1

Shock / Denial

Initial reaction — disbelief or minimizing. "This won't really stick" or "It's probably not as big a deal as they're making it."
"I'm sure things will go back to normal."
Manager's move: Provide information. Be patient. Don't interpret denial as agreement.
2

Frustration / Resistance

The loss becomes real. Frustration, anger, pushback. Energy is high but pointed in the wrong direction.
"I don't agree with this. This isn't how it should work."
Manager's move: Listen. Acknowledge the loss. Don't argue. Don't rush.
3

Exploration

Energy starts to shift. "Maybe there's a way through this." Beginning to engage with the new reality.
"What would it look like if this actually worked?"
Manager's move: Invite their ideas. Give them ownership of small pieces.
4

Commitment

Fully engaged with the new reality. Contributing, building, moving forward.
"I'm on board. Here's how I think we can make this work."
Manager's move: Sustain momentum. Recognize progress. Don't declare victory too early.
Foundation
Three Conversations for Every Change
Every significant change requires three distinct conversations — and the order matters.

Conversation 1: What & Why

What's changing, why it's happening, what you know and what you don't. Be honest about uncertainty — "here's what I know, here's what I'm still learning."

Have this first. Context before reaction.

Conversation 2: What It Means for You

Individual impacts. What changes for this specific person. What stays the same. What they're losing, and whether you're naming it or avoiding it.

This is the conversation most managers skip — and it's the one people needed most.

Conversation 3: How We'll Navigate

Their role. Your role. The check-in plan. How you'll know if it's working. When you'll revisit.

This creates forward momentum — from loss to path.
The order matters. "What this means for you" before "what and why" creates anxiety — people need context before they can process personal impact. Don't skip to Conversation 3 until Conversation 2 has been had.
The Change Curve
The Usually-Positive Person Who Isn't
One of your most reliably positive Team Members is visibly resistant to a change you genuinely believe in. She's usually the one who helps others see the bright side. Now she's not. What's your move?
ARemind her that the decision is made and you need the team moving forward.
BHave a 1:1 — acknowledge what's being lost for her specifically, listen to her concern, and connect her to the why without dismissing the resistance.
CReassign her temporarily so her resistance doesn't affect the rest of the team.
DGive her extra time — she'll come around when she sees it working.
The Change Curve
You Disagree with the Change
You're implementing a change you personally have concerns about. A Team Member asks you directly: "What do you actually think about this?" How do you respond?
ATell them you disagree — it builds trust and shows you're on their side.
BBe honest that you have questions, explain why leadership made this call, and commit to working through it together.
CAct as if you fully support it — your job is to implement, not editorialize.
DSay nothing about your personal view — redirect to the task at hand.
Practice
When Change Takes Longer Than Expected
The change is taking significantly longer to take hold than anyone expected. The team is tired of the transition and starting to disengage. What's your response?
APush harder — the team needs to move faster and the fatigue is a sign they're not committed enough.
BAcknowledge the fatigue directly, name what's been hard, mark visible progress, and revisit the check-in plan — the transition is real, not a failure.
CPause the change — the team isn't ready and implementation should wait.
DReassure them that it will all be worth it — keep energy positive and forward-looking.
Practice
Leadership Transition Above You
Your direct manager — someone the team trusts — is leaving. You've been asked to lead the team through the transition to a new leader. What's the highest-priority action in the first week?
AFocus on maintaining business continuity — keep everyone focused on the work.
BHave individual conversations with each Team Member — acknowledge the loss of their trusted manager, listen to concerns, and be honest about what you know.
CSend a message about how excited you are about the new leader's arrival.
DWait for the new leader to arrive before having any formal conversations — let them set the tone.
Practice
When a Change Should Be Slowed
Three weeks in, your team is struggling in ways that suggest the implementation is creating real operational problems — not just transition friction. How do you tell the difference, and what do you do?
APush through — all change creates friction and slowing down now signals weakness.
BDistinguish friction (normal) from operational failure (requires intervention) — if the change is creating real harm, surface it with data to your leader and advocate for a pause or revision.
CTake the team vote — if most of them think it should slow down, that's your answer.
DSlow it down on your own — you're closest to the team and can see what's happening.

Course Complete
You've finished Leading Through Change. The managers who keep their teams through transitions are the ones who name the loss, meet people where they are, and don't rush past the hard part.

What change-capable managers do differently:

Name what's being lost alongside what's being gained — always.
Have all three change conversations — in order.
Meet people where they are on the Curve — don't try to skip them to Phase 4.
Acknowledge resistance before pushing for commitment.
Be honest when you have questions about a change — and committed to working through it together.
