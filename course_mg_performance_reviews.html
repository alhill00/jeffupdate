The Manager Who Wrote Glowing Reviews
David was known for being easy to work for. He kept things positive, avoided awkward conversations, and wrote generous performance reviews — "exceeds expectations" for almost everyone. His team loved him. Or so he thought.
When one of his strongest Team Members applied for a promotion, she didn't get it. David was blindsided. The feedback from the panel: her reviews were glowing but vague, and she had no record of being coached through development areas.
The feedback that would have helped her grow — the honest, specific kind — had never been given. David had protected the relationship. He'd failed the career.
A performance review is only valuable when it's honest. A review that avoids the hard truth isn't a kindness — it's a missed opportunity disguised as one.
Foundation
Three Goals. One Conversation. That's the Problem.
Most managers dread review season. Not because they don't care — but because they're trying to do three things at once inside one conversation.

What They're Trying To Do

  • Evaluate performance fairly
  • Motivate the person forward
  • Avoid damaging the relationship

Why It Doesn't Work

  • Honest evaluation can feel demotivating
  • Motivation can inflate the rating
  • Protecting the relationship softens everything
These goals pull in opposite directions. The manager who tries to do all three ends up doing none of them well — and the Team Member leaves without the signal they actually needed.
Foundation
Two Review Failure Modes
Most review problems come from one of two failure modes. Both are management failures dressed up as kindness.

The Inflation Mode

Everyone is "exceeds" or "meets." Nobody gets a real signal. Ratings have no meaning. High performers don't know they're high performers. Low performers don't know there's a problem.

Result: The rating system becomes organizational noise.

The Recency Mode

The review reflects the last 4 weeks, not the full year. A strong Q1–Q3 gets erased by a difficult Q4. Or a great December rescues a mediocre year.

Result: The person who performed well doesn't feel seen.

Both feel easier in the moment. Inflation avoids conflict. Recency requires less tracking. But both rob Team Members of the honest picture they need to actually grow.
The Honest Review
What Makes a Performance Review Actually Work
A high-quality review has four components — and names the biases that undermine it.

1. Specific Evidence

Behaviors and outcomes — not adjectives. "In Q2, she led the process redesign that cut onboarding time by 40%" — not "she's a team player."

2. Honest Assessment

Includes real development areas — not just strengths. If there's nothing to develop, the review isn't honest or it's missing something.

3. Forward Focus

Where do we go from here? Not just what happened. The best reviews spend as much time on the future as they do on the past.

4. Conversational

Their response matters as much as your assessment. A review delivered as a monologue is half a review. Their perspective belongs in it.

Recency Bias

The last few weeks dominate what you recall about the whole year.

Halo Effect

One strength colors everything — strengths in one area inflate ratings in all areas.

Leniency Bias

Rating everyone positively to avoid conflict — the most common review failure.

Similarity Bias

Rating people who remind you of yourself higher than those who approach work differently.

Structuring the Review Conversation
The document is not the review. The conversation is. Here's how to structure it so both people leave with clarity.
1
Ask for their self-assessment first. "Before I share mine, how would you describe your year?" This surfaces their perception and sets up a real dialogue — not a download.
2
Validate what they got right. Start with specific evidence for their strengths. This earns credibility for what comes next.
3
Add your perspective where it differs. Use specific evidence, not impressions. "Here's what I observed" beats "I feel like."
4
Name development areas directly. Not buried in the summary. Not softened until invisible. Specific behavior, specific impact.
5
End with forward. "What does growth look like for you in the next 12 months?" The last thing they hear should be where they're going — not where they've been.

The Self-Assessment Gap
Your Team Member's self-assessment is significantly more positive than your assessment. She rates herself "exceeds" in three areas where you see "meets" or below. How do you handle the conversation?
AAdjust your rating upward to avoid conflict — the gap is too uncomfortable to navigate directly.
BStart with your assessment and move through it without engaging with her self-assessment.
CAcknowledge where you see it differently and walk through the specific evidence for your view.
DDefer the conversation — send your assessment in writing first and discuss later.

The Warm Relationship, Hard Rating
You need to give a Team Member a "needs improvement" rating. She's been on your team for 3 years and the relationship is warm — you genuinely like her. How do you approach this?
ASoften the rating to avoid damaging a relationship that matters to the team.
BBe direct about the rating, specific about why, and clear about what improvement looks like and by when.
CWait until next cycle — give her one more chance before it's official.
DPut it in writing first and ask her to review before you discuss in person.
Practice
When They Push Back
Your Team Member disagrees with your review in the meeting — emotionally, saying you don't see their contributions. The tone is hurt, not hostile. What's your move?
ADefend your position with authority — you've done the assessment and you stand by it.
BPause, ask them to share the specific contributions they feel were missed, listen carefully, and then respond with your evidence.
CAgree to revisit the review after they've had time to process their emotions.
DStand firm and end the conversation — emotions aren't useful here.
Practice
Systematic Inflation
Mid-review season, you realize your ratings have been systematically inflated — nearly everyone is "exceeds." You suspect leniency bias has crept in. What do you do?
AStick with the ratings — calibration is a broader org problem, not yours to fix alone.
BRecalibrate before finalizing, even if some conversations will be uncomfortable as a result.
CSubmit the ratings and note your awareness of the inflation in the comments section.
DAsk HR to handle the recalibration so it doesn't come from you.
Practice
The Mixed Performance Picture
A Team Member's year was genuinely strong in 3 of 4 performance areas — and clearly weak in one. How should the review reflect this?
ARound up — strong overall performance means a strong overall rating.
BRate the overall performance honestly and name the development area specifically in the written review.
CFocus the review on the weak area and note the strengths briefly.
DAverage the four areas mathematically to arrive at the rating.

Course Complete
You've finished Performance Reviews. The managers who change careers are the ones who tell the truth with care — and do it before it's too late to matter.

What honest review managers do differently:

Ask for the self-assessment before sharing yours — every review, every cycle.
Name one real development area for every Team Member — with specific evidence, not impressions.
Audit ratings for leniency bias before submitting — not after calibration.
Prepare for the conversation, not just the document — they're different things.
End every review with a forward question: "What do you want to build in the next 12 months?"
