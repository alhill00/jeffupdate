Foundation
Managing Change vs. Building Capacity for It
Most organizations manage change one initiative at a time. A restructure happens, a project team is formed, communications are drafted, resistance is navigated, and eventually the initiative lands — or it doesn't. Then the next change arrives, and the process begins again.
VPs who lead through growth and change effectively are doing something different. They are building the organizational muscle that makes the next change faster, easier, and less costly in human terms. This is the distinction between change management and change capacity.

Change Management

  • Manages one initiative at a time
  • Reactive to each change event
  • Focuses on communication and resistance
  • Resets between initiatives

Change Capacity

  • Builds the muscle that makes all change easier
  • Proactive infrastructure investment
  • Focuses on resilience, learning, and adaptive leadership
  • Compounds across initiatives
Foundation
What Limits Change Capacity
Three things consistently limit an organization's ability to absorb and adapt to change — and all three are within VP control to address.

Fatigue

  • Too many initiatives running simultaneously
  • Insufficient recovery between major transitions
  • No prioritization of what's truly strategic vs. just urgent
  • Change fatigue reads as resistance — but it's exhaustion

Fear

  • Org learns that change = role jeopardy
  • Failure during transitions is punished rather than learned from
  • No psychological safety to raise concerns early
  • Leaders privately resist changes they publicly support

Passivity

  • Leaders wait for direction rather than adapting
  • Middle layer executes but doesn't lead the change
  • Team Members comply rather than genuinely adopt
  • Workarounds proliferate instead of new behaviors
Foundation
Pacing Change at Scale
One of the hardest VP judgment calls is knowing when the organization is at or near its absorption limit — and sequencing accordingly. Three signals that you have exceeded change capacity:
Signal 1: Key leaders are burning out or leaving. Change volume has a human cost, and it typically shows up at the leadership layer first.
Signal 2: Quality is declining as volume increases. When the org is adapting to change while trying to maintain performance, something gives — and it's usually quality.
Signal 3: The org is complying but not adapting. Behaviors have changed on paper but not in practice. Workarounds are growing. People are going through the motions of the new initiative while quietly maintaining the old way.
When you see these signals: sequence and consolidate rather than pause indefinitely. A pause that creates clarity about what's next is stabilizing. A pause with no clarity is its own change anxiety.
The Change Capacity Model
Three Change Capacity Elements
Change capacity is built through three organizational infrastructure investments — not through better change management on any individual initiative.
Element 1
Resilience Infrastructure
Bench depth so changes don't leave critical roles uncovered. Clear decision rights so changes don't create decision paralysis. Psychological safety so honest concerns surface early rather than underground.
Diagnostic: Can your org absorb the loss of a key leader during a major initiative and keep moving?
Element 2
Learning Culture
Norms that make iteration, experimentation, and honest failure review possible. Organizations that can learn from change experiences adapt faster to the next one. Without this, change leaves lessons behind instead of building capacity.
Diagnostic: After the last major change, did the org conduct a genuine after-action and update its approach based on what it learned?
Element 3
Adaptive Leadership Layer
Directors and managers who can sense change signals, lead their teams through uncertainty, and adapt rather than execute. This layer is the change transmission mechanism — without it, VP-level strategy doesn't become team-level behavior.
Diagnostic: Can your director layer lead change — not just manage through it — without significant VP involvement in every step?
Foundation
The Difference That Compounds
Change capacity investments compound. An org with high resilience infrastructure, a genuine learning culture, and an adaptive leadership layer doesn't just handle the current change better — it handles every future change better. The value of capacity investment is measured across initiatives, not within them.
This is why VPs who manage change well one initiative at a time can still find their org structurally fragile. They solved the immediate problem but didn't invest in the muscle that would have made the problem easier. They borrowed against the future.
The VP question to ask quarterly: Am I investing in managing the current change, or in building the capacity that will make the next one easier? The answer should be both — but deliberately both, not accidentally one.
The Change Capacity Model
Org in Initiative Overload
Your organization is currently running six significant initiatives simultaneously. Performance metrics are declining. Three of your four directors have mentioned burnout in recent conversations. Team Member engagement survey data shows a significant drop in the "I can handle the changes happening in my org" item. The business is still adding new priorities.
What's the right VP move?
ACommunicate that you know the pace is hard but that the business requires it — honesty about difficulty without reducing the load is the most authentic response.
BTriage the initiative portfolio — identify which 2–3 are truly strategic, consolidate or pause the others, and communicate the sequencing decision clearly with rationale.
CAdd a change management workstream to each initiative to improve adoption — the issue is execution quality, not volume.
DTell your director layer to protect their teams from the full weight of the initiative load — they can absorb more at the leadership level so Team Members don't feel the overload.
The Change Capacity Model
Building Capacity After a Period of Stability
After 18 months of relative stability, you have a window before the next significant growth phase. You have discretionary capacity — time, attention, and some budget — to invest. What's the highest-leverage change capacity investment you can make right now?
ALaunch a Team Member engagement initiative to build morale reserves that can be drawn on during the next change period.
BInvest in the director layer's change leadership capability — develop their ability to sense, adapt, and lead rather than execute.
CConduct a comprehensive process audit to identify inefficiencies that will become bottlenecks in a change environment.
DDocument lessons learned from the last 18 months and archive them for future reference.
Practice
Structuring a Large Change for Absorption
You need to implement a significant organizational restructure that affects reporting relationships, roles, and team compositions across your function. You have flexibility on timing and sequencing. What's the principle that should guide your design?
AMove as quickly as possible — extended uncertainty is more damaging than a single short period of disruption.
BSequence the change in waves — stabilize one part of the org before disrupting the next — so capacity is preserved rather than overwhelmed simultaneously.
CMove all changes simultaneously to limit the total duration of organizational uncertainty.
DDelay implementation until all questions are answered and the org has full clarity on outcomes.
Practice
Developing Directors as Change Leaders
You've noticed that your director layer manages change well when given clear instructions, but struggles to lead through ambiguity independently. When you're not in the room, they tend to wait for direction rather than adapt. You want to shift them from change managers to change leaders. What's the most effective development approach?
ASend them to an external change management certification program — formal training builds structured capability.
BGive them deliberate stretch experiences with real accountability — let them lead a change piece with ambiguity, check in rather than direct, and debrief what they learned from navigating it.
CInclude them more frequently in your strategic conversations so they understand context better before adapting.
DCreate a director council that meets regularly to share change leadership practices across the function.
Practice
Communicating Rationale When Human Cost Is Real
A change you're implementing has a clear business rationale and also a real human cost — some roles will change significantly, and the change will require more from people who are already working hard. You need to communicate the change in a way that is honest about both dimensions.
What's the right approach?
ALead with the business case — once people understand the strategic rationale, the human cost will feel more acceptable and the resistance will decrease.
BAcknowledge both dimensions explicitly — name the business rationale and acknowledge the human cost as real, not as something to minimize. Respect the difficulty rather than explaining it away.
CFocus the communication on what stays the same — continuity messaging reduces anxiety more effectively than a balanced view of the change.
DHave HR lead the change communication — they're better positioned to handle the human cost dimension without it affecting your leadership credibility.
