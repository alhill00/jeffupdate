Foundation
The Team That Shapes Everything Else
The VP's most important team is often the leadership team of which they're a member — not the team they lead directly.
What makes senior leadership teams different from other teams: they require real trust that allows real disagreement, shared accountability for organizational outcomes, and the ability to challenge the CEO when the situation calls for it.
The most common failure mode: Looks aligned externally. Functions as competing fiefdoms internally. Every member wins their functional battle while the organization loses the strategic war.
Foundation
What High-Performing Senior Teams Actually Do
The behaviors that distinguish high-performing leadership teams aren't the ones you'd expect. They're not about harmony or alignment — they're about productive tension.

Low-Performance Senior Teams

  • Agreement in the room, resistance in the hallway
  • Functional wins prioritized over org outcomes
  • Hard conversations happen around the table, not at it
  • "They" when the org underperforms

High-Performance Senior Teams

  • Disagreement surfaces in the room, resolved together
  • Org-level outcomes shared across all members
  • Conflict happens at the table — and the team functions well after
  • "We" when the org underperforms
Foundation
The VP's Role in Building the Senior Team
Building the senior team isn't only the CEO's job. Every VP either strengthens or weakens the team's dynamics by how they show up.
1
Invest in Understanding Peer Priorities
Not to be nice — to build relational credibility that makes disagreement safe. You can't challenge a peer you don't understand. And peers won't listen to someone who hasn't listened first.
2
Challenge Peers in the Room, Not Around Them
Hallway conversations about peer decisions are organizational corrosion. The VP who challenges a peer's call in the exec meeting — respectfully, directly — is building team trust. The one who does it in side conversations is destroying it.
3
Demonstrate Collective Accountability
"We" not "them" when the org underperforms. This isn't semantics — it's the signal that you hold yourself accountable for outcomes beyond your functional lane. Senior teams that use "we" in adversity outperform those that find the team member to blame.
The Senior Team Model
Four Performance Conditions
High-performing senior teams share four conditions. Absent any one of them, team effectiveness degrades predictably.
1
Trust Under Pressure
Can have hard conversations and function well after. Trust here is not friendliness — it's confidence that conflict won't result in retaliation or relationship damage that persists.
2
Productive Conflict
Surfaces disagreement in the room, not the hallways. Teams that eliminate conflict eliminate the process by which bad ideas get challenged. Productive conflict is a leadership team's immune system.
3
Shared Accountability
Each member holds themselves accountable for org-level outcomes, not just functional ones. When the organization underperforms, shared accountability means no one wins a functional argument while the business loses.
4
Collective Clarity on Priorities
Explicit, agreed-upon priorities and trade-offs — not implicit competing ones. Teams that can't say what they're not doing are teams that can't align around what they are doing.
Foundation
Trust Is Not Friendliness
The most common confusion on senior teams is mistaking friendliness for trust. A team can be cordial, collegial, and personally warm — and still have no real trust.

Friendliness Without Trust

  • Positive social dynamic
  • Agreement on easy things
  • Conflict avoided to preserve relationships
  • Hard conversations happen outside the room

Trust Under Pressure

  • Confidence that conflict won't become retaliation
  • Disagreement in the room, resolution together
  • Hard conversations at the table
  • Team functions well after conflict
The build strategy: Trust under pressure is built by having conflict and coming through it intact — not by avoiding conflict to keep things comfortable. The team that never disagrees is the team that can't be trusted when it says it agrees.
The Senior Team Model
Alignment That Isn't
Your leadership team consistently reaches consensus in meetings — but you've noticed that after meetings, several members are raising concerns in side conversations that they didn't raise in the room. The CEO thinks the team is highly aligned.
ALet it continue — if the CEO is satisfied with the alignment, surfacing the side conversations would create unnecessary tension.
BRaise the dynamic directly: "I've noticed decisions are being relitigated after meetings. I think we need to talk about how we surface disagreement in the room."
CTalk to each peer privately to encourage them to raise their concerns in the next meeting.
DBring a facilitator in to improve meeting dynamics without naming the specific issue.
The Senior Team Model
The Peer Who Protects Their Function
A peer VP consistently advocates for decisions that benefit their function at the expense of organization-wide outcomes. They're well-liked and politically influential. How do you build trust with them while being honest about the impact?
ACompete — advocate for your function's interests with equal force to create a balance of competing voices.
BInvest in genuinely understanding their priorities first, then have a direct conversation about the pattern you're seeing and what it costs the organization.
CEscalate the pattern to the CEO and let them address it directly.
DAccept it as their leadership style and work around it in cross-functional planning.
Practice
Raising a Concern in the Exec Meeting
In a leadership team meeting, a peer VP presents a decision that you believe is strategically wrong. The room is moving toward quick agreement and the CEO seems ready to close it.
ALet the decision go — raising concern now will slow the meeting and seem obstructionist.
BSpeak up directly: "Before we close this — I want to share a concern. I may be missing something, but here's what I'm seeing."
CCatch the CEO after the meeting to share your concern privately.
DEmail the peer VP after the meeting to flag your concern and suggest they reconsider.
Practice
After a Difficult Period
Your leadership team just came through a difficult stretch — a major operational failure, public scrutiny, and significant internal disagreement about what went wrong. Team dynamics are strained. What do you do?
AGive it time — teams need space to recover naturally after stress.
BAdvocate for a structured debrief — what happened, what did we get right, what did we get wrong, and what are we doing differently? Then move forward together.
CFocus on the next win — positive momentum will rebuild the team dynamic faster than any debrief.
DInvest in team-building activities to rebuild the social dynamic before returning to hard strategic questions.
Practice
When Collective Accountability Has Broken Down
After a poor organizational performance quarter, the leadership team meeting devolves into each VP explaining why their function performed well and why other functions are responsible for the shortfall. What do you do?
APresent your function's performance data clearly to ensure your team isn't blamed unfairly.
BName the dynamic: "I notice we're each defending our function's performance. I'd rather ask: what did we miss collectively, and what are we going to do about it?"
CLet the CEO facilitate — it's their role to redirect the conversation when the team loses focus.
DAgree with the functional performance framing — each team needs to own its own results.
