Coaching vs. Telling

When you answer every question, you become the answer.

The best leaders make themselves unnecessary — one question at a time.

There's a place for telling — when there's a safety issue, a time constraint, or a genuine knowledge gap that needs to be filled immediately. But most of the time, when a Team Member brings you a problem, they don't need your answer. They need your help finding theirs. The shift from telling to coaching is the single biggest upgrade most leaders can make.

The Tool

The Coaching vs. Telling Decision Tree

1

Is it urgent or safety-critical?

→ Tell. There's no time for questions when there's an aircraft on the ground or a Guest in crisis.

2

Does the person have the knowledge to figure it out?

→ Coach. They know more than they think. Your questions surface it faster than your answers.

3

Have you answered this same question more than twice?

→ Coach, then build a process. If you keep answering the same question, the system is broken — not the person.

4

Do you just have a preference, not a requirement?

→ Coach. Let them own the how. You own the what and why.

 

Sound Familiar?

A Team Member comes to you: "I'm not sure how to handle the crew scheduling conflict on next Tuesday's rotation." You know exactly what to do. You tell them. Three weeks later, same Team Member, same type of problem.

You solved it for them instead of with them. Every time you give the answer, you delay their growth by one week.

Your Challenge This Week

Every time a Team Member brings you a problem this week, ask one question before giving any answer: "What have you already tried?" That one question starts the shift from telling to coaching. Try it once and notice what happens to the quality of the conversation.
 

Quick Pulse — 1 Question

In your last week of conversations with your team, what percentage were coaching vs. telling? Be honest.
