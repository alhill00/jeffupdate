Foundation
Managing Up Is Not Politics
Managing up has a reputation problem. When people hear it, they think of flattery, self-promotion, or playing the game. That's not what it is.
Managing up is understanding your leader well enough to communicate effectively, surface the right information at the right time, and advocate for your team in ways that actually land. It's a professional skill — and one of the highest-leverage ones you can develop.
The managers who are most effective at it aren't the ones who manage perceptions. They're the ones who make it easier for their leader to make good decisions — by giving them the context, the timing, and the framing they need.
Managing up is a service to your leader, your team, and your organization — not a political maneuver. The manager who does it well gets more resources, more autonomy, and more trust.
Foundation
What Most Managers Get Wrong
Three patterns undermine most manager-leader relationships — all of them addressable.

Waiting to Be Asked

If your leader has to come to you for updates, you've already created a problem. Proactive communication is the job — not a bonus. If they're surprised, you've waited too long.

Presenting Problems Without Recommendations

"We have a problem" without "here's what I recommend" puts the decision burden on your leader without adding value. You're closest to the problem — you should have a view.

Not Knowing What Your Leader Actually Cares About

Every leader has priorities that aren't fully visible in their org chart or their instructions. The managers who communicate most effectively know what those are — and connect their work to them deliberately.

Your leader is busy. The manager who makes their leader's job easier by surfacing the right things, at the right time, with a recommendation — earns more autonomy and more support than the one who waits to be directed.

The Visibility Gap
Here is the most important thing most managers don't understand about managing up:
Your team's work is invisible to your leader unless you make it visible. That is your job — not theirs.
Your leader has a wide span of attention. They see outcomes, surprises, and problems that escalate. They don't see the daily complexity your team navigates, the creative solutions they come up with, or the effort that didn't show up in a metric.

When You Don't Make It Visible

  • Your team doesn't get recognized
  • Your team's needs don't get resourced
  • Your work gets undervalued at every table you're not at

When You Do

  • Your leader makes better decisions with more context
  • Your team feels seen and advocated for
  • You build the trust that earns autonomy
The Leader Relationship
Four Managing-Up Moves
1

Understand Their Priorities and Pressures

Not just their instructions — what they're actually trying to accomplish, what they're being measured on, what keeps them up at night. This context changes everything about how you communicate.

2

Communicate in Their Style

Data-heavy vs. narrative-first vs. bullet-point concise. Some leaders want the bottom line first. Some need context before they can process the conclusion. Match their style, not yours.

3

Bring Recommendations, Not Just Problems

"Here's the issue, here's what I recommend, here's what I need from you" — this structure turns every problem conversation into a problem-solving conversation. It also signals that you've thought it through.

4

Push Back Through the Relationship

When you disagree, bring it directly — through a conversation, with a specific concern and a recommendation. Not around the relationship (complaining to peers), and not in a way that undermines the decision publicly.

The Escalation Test — and Timing
You can't — and shouldn't — escalate everything. But some things need to get to your leader before they surface somewhere else.

The Escalation Test

"Would my leader be bothered if this came up at a higher level and they hadn't heard from me first?"
If yes → escalate. Give them the situation, recovery plan, and what you need from them.
If no → handle it. You have the authority and context. Decide.

The Situation + Recommendation + Ask Structure

Situation
What's happening, in plain language. No editorializing — just the facts and the context.
Recommendation
What you think should happen and why. You've been closest to this — you should have a view.
Ask
What you need from them specifically — a decision, a resource, an introduction, awareness only.
Timing matters. Deliver bad news early — not at the deadline. Surfacing a problem early gives your leader options. Surfacing it late eliminates them.

You know — two weeks out — that a project is going to miss its deadline by at least two weeks. You might be able to recover if things go well. How do you handle it?
AWait until you're closer to the deadline — surface it only if recovery fails.
BBrief your leader now — give them the situation, your recovery plan, and what you need from them.
CSend an email outlining the risk and wait for a response before taking further action.
DHave the Team Member who owns the project deliver the update directly.

The Hallway Direction That Creates a Problem
Your leader gave you direction in a 5-minute hallway conversation three days ago. Since then you've realized the direction — if followed as given — will create a downstream problem. What do you do?
AFollow the direction and fix the problem on the back end — it's not your place to question the call.
BRequest 10 minutes to share the specific concern with a framing and a recommendation for an alternative.
CWork around the direction quietly — implement something slightly different without flagging it.
DAsk your peers how they'd handle the downstream problem before bringing it up.

Advocating for a Resource
Your team needs a resource — a hire, a tool, a budget — that you haven't been able to get. You have 10 minutes with your leader. How do you structure the ask?
A"We really need [resource] — the team is stretched and it's affecting morale."
B"Here's what's happening [situation], here's what we need and why [recommendation], here's what I need you to decide or approve [ask]."
C"Can we get [resource]? I'll follow up with a business case if you need one."
D"I wanted to flag that we're understaffed — but I know it's a tough budget environment."
Practice
Getting Attention When They're Stretched
Your leader is extremely busy — you've been trying to get 30 minutes for two weeks on a decision that needs to be made. What's the right move?
AKeep requesting the 30 minutes — if it's important enough, they'll make time.
BReduce the ask — send a concise email or message with the decision clearly framed, the options, and your recommendation. Ask for a response or 10 minutes.
CMake the decision yourself and inform them after — they'd want you to move.
DEscalate to their assistant and request a priority slot on their calendar.
Practice
They Found Out Before You Told Them
Your leader found out about a problem from someone else before you told them. They're not upset — but you can tell they noticed. How do you recover the trust?
ADon't address it directly — bring them a great update next week to restore confidence.
BName it directly — "I should have brought that to you sooner. Here's what I was managing and what I'll do differently."
CExplain why you didn't surface it — give them context so they understand your reasoning.
DAsk them how they'd like to be kept informed going forward — let them set the standard.

Course Complete
You've finished Managing Up. The managers who get the most for their teams are the ones who make their leader's job easier — and do it proactively, not reactively.

What strong managing-up managers do differently:

Surface risks before they become surprises — early, with a plan.
Bring the recommendation alongside the problem — every time.
Know their leader's top priorities and connect their work to them.
Push back through the relationship — directly, specifically, with an alternative.
Make the team's work visible — because if they don't, no one will.
