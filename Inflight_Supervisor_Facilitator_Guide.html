Slido Tips
Activate each activity just before you need it — not all at once. Participants will only see the one that's live.
Drop the event code in Teams chat every time you open a Slido activity. Say it out loud too: "Event code is [CODE] — also in the chat."
For the Module 3 open-text: read responses privately before sharing. Pick ones that are specific and safe to discuss — not the ones that might make someone identifiable or uncomfortable.
For the Module 4 rating: don't linger on the number too long. The point is to normalize the tension, not judge anyone's score. "Most of us lean one way naturally — what matters is knowing your default."
Breakout Group Planning
Principles
Always cross-base in pairs
For Modules 1, 2, and 4 — never pair someone with their own base supervisor. The whole point is building new connections.
Module 3 — groups of 4–5
Collapse 7 pairs into 3 rooms of 4–5. Larger groups create more debate on the scenario, which is the point. Mix bases.
Same partner for Modules 1, 2, and 4
Reusing the same cross-base pair builds comfort. By Module 4 (hard conversations practice), they'll be willing to go deeper because they already have rapport.
How to Assign in Teams
Use Teams breakout rooms. Create 7 rooms before the session (labeled Room 1–7). Assign pairs in advance in your notes, then move people into rooms manually when it's time.
Give a clear time check when you open rooms: "You have 8 minutes — I'll call you back at [TIME]." Set a Teams timer if you use one.
For Module 3 groups: collapse 7 pairs into 3 rooms of 4–5 (put 2 pairs per room, with one room taking a third pair if needed). Give them 10 minutes on the scenario.

When results come in: read 3–4 words out loud. Pick one that surprises you and ask: "Who chose this? Tell me what's behind it." Get 1–2 people talking before you move on. Don't rush this — the energy here sets the whole session.

Watch For

0:20 – 0:50  ·  30 Minutes
Who We Are
Slide 5–7  ·  Be Seriously Nice
Purpose
This module exists for one reason: to make the room feel safe. If people don't feel connected to each other, nothing in Modules 3 and 4 will work. Don't shortchange this.
Talking Points
Frame the cross-base breakout: "We rarely get all of us in the same room. Your counterpart in BOS is dealing with something you've already solved in BNA — and vice versa. That's about to change."
Set the breakout tone: "This is a safe space. What you share stays in this room — or in that breakout room, specifically. Be honest. Be curious. Ask follow-up questions."
For large group debrief: ask 2–3 people to share what surprised them. Look for the themes: usually it's some version of "we're all dealing with the same thing and didn't know it."
Close the module with: "The supervisor across the country from you isn't your competition. They're your most underused resource. We're going to do something about that today."
Watch For
Watch For
People keeping it surface-level in the breakout (talking about weather, delays, easy stuff). That's okay for the first 2 minutes. The second question — what keeps you up at night when you think about leading your base — is the one that matters. Give them the full 8 minutes.
Anyone who seems reluctant to share in large group debrief. Don't put people on the spot — invite, don't require. "Anyone from a pair where something landed — feel free to share."
Deeper Knowledge — Be Seriously Nice
What the principle actually means for hard moments: "Be Seriously Nice" is not the soft principle. It's the hardest one. Being seriously nice means you care enough to stay in an uncomfortable conversation rather than exit. It means you tell the truth with warmth, not avoidance with politeness. Every module today connects back to it.
When someone pushes back on "niceness" as weakness: Name it directly — "This isn't about being a pushover. Seriously Nice means you never sacrifice honesty for comfort. The 'serious' part is doing it even when it's hard. The 'nice' part is doing it in a way that keeps the person whole." These aren't opposites.
What this looks like on a hard shift: A seriously nice supervisor is the one who calls the FA after a bad flight — not to debrief the operation, but to ask how they're doing. That's it. That one call does more for retention and loyalty than any recognition program.
Module 1 Close — Tie Back to Principle
Before you move to Module 2, land the principle. Don't rush past it: "Everything you just did — asking honest questions, actually listening, sharing something you don't usually say out loud — that's what Be Seriously Nice looks like in practice. It's not about being pleasant. It's about genuinely caring about the person across from you. And you just practiced it."

Then move. Don't add more. That landing is enough.

0:50 – 1:25  ·  35 Minutes
Your Daily Toolkit
Slide 8–14  ·  Champion Others
Opening Question First (Slide 9 — New)
Before You Explain Anything — Ask First
Slide 9 asks participants which of the 3 tools they already do consistently — A, B, or C — in the Teams chat. Read what comes in before moving to the tools slide.

This changes the dynamic completely: you're no longer delivering information, you're responding to what they just told you about themselves. Use their answers to frame the tool explanations — "Several of you said C — recognition. So let's start with why that one is actually the hardest to do consistently..."
Talking Points — The Three Tools
30-Second Check-In: "This is not a status update. It's not 'did you get the memo.' It's: how are you? And then actually listening to the answer. Thirty seconds. Before a shift. Before you hang up. It costs you nothing and it changes everything for your FA."
The Coaching Question: "When a FA comes to you with a problem, your instinct is to solve it. That's what got you promoted. But every time you solve it for them, you take an opportunity away from them. Try this: before you give the answer, ask — 'What do you think?' You'll be surprised how often they know."
More coaching question examples you can use or share: "What would you do if I wasn't here?" / "What's your gut telling you?" / "What options do you see?" / "What have you already tried?" / "What would you tell a colleague in this same situation?" — These all do the same thing: they return ownership to the FA instead of reinforcing dependency on you.
The Recognition Hit: "Not a trophy. Not a monthly email. A specific moment where you name the exact thing they did and say why it mattered. 'Hey, the way you handled that passenger in row 12 yesterday — that was textbook. I want you to know I saw it.' That's it. That's the whole thing."
Teams Chat Discussion — After Tools Overview
2 Minutes — Teams Chat (Slide 11)
Ask everyone to drop A, B, or C in the Teams chat: which of the 3 tools do they use the least consistently? A = 30-Second Check-In, B = Coaching Question, C = Recognition Hit.

After responses come in: Call out what you see. "Looks like [A/B/C] is the most common — let's talk about why that one is the hardest." Don't move on without naming the pattern. This makes the tools feel personal, not generic. Keep it to 2 minutes — you're about to practice one of them.
Practice: 30-Second Check-In
Breakout Pairs — 5 Minutes
Instructions to give before breakouts: "Same pair as Module 1. I want you to use the 3-step structure from your workbook: Open with a genuine question — not operational, not 'did you get the brief.' Listen — actually wait for the answer, don't fill the pause. And respond — acknowledge what you heard before you move on. One person is the supervisor, one is the FA. Then switch. When you come back, I want to know: what felt natural, and what felt awkward?"

After breakouts: Ask 2–3 pairs to share. Common answers: Step 2 (the listening/silence) is what people find hardest. Name it: "Most of us are already thinking about what to say next. That's the habit we're breaking." Normalize the awkward: "It's supposed to feel a little different. That's how you know it's working."
Deeper Knowledge — Champion Others
What champions actually do differently: They have a habit of noticing — specifically what someone did, why it was good, and when to say it. Most supervisors wait for a big moment to recognize. Champions do it constantly for small moments. A specific, timely "I saw that" is worth more than a quarterly recognition email.
When a FA seems resistant to check-ins: Some FAs will deflect ("I'm fine, don't worry about me"). Don't interpret that as "they don't need it." It usually means they're not used to it. Stay consistent. After 3–4 genuine check-ins, most FAs start opening up. The habit is on you, not on them.
The coaching question with a FA who always says "I don't know": Don't give the answer. Try: "What's your best guess?" or "If you had to make a call right now, what would it be?" Those reframes bypass the reflex. Most "I don't know" responses are about risk-aversion, not actual lack of knowledge.

1:35 – 2:05  ·  30 Minutes
When the Pressure's On
Slide 15–18  ·  Do What's Right
Introducing the Framework
"You make decisions under pressure every day. Gate conflicts, crew disputes, last-minute changes. You rarely have full information, you have seconds — and people are watching you to know how to feel. This framework isn't about slowing you down. It's about making sure you're doing all three steps, not just the one you're comfortable with. A lot of us are good at Assess. We're decent at Choose. We underinvest in Communicate — and that's usually where things fall apart."
Slido — Activity 3
Open Text Responses
"Describe a recent moment you had to make a call under pressure."
Read responses privately as they come in. Select 2–3 to discuss live — look for ones that are specific, relatable, and safe to share. Don't pick anything that could embarrass or identify someone. You can paraphrase: "Someone shared something like this..." before reading it.
Scenario Groups (Slide 18)
Groups of 4–5 — 10 Minutes
The scenario is in the participant workbook (Section 3). It involves an FA-passenger altercation 45 minutes before departure with multiple stakeholders watching.

Room setup: Collapse 7 pairs into 3 rooms (groups of 4–5). Put 2 pairs per room with one room taking a third pair if needed.

What to listen for in debrief: Did groups skip Assess and jump straight to Choose? Did they think about who to Communicate with, or just what to do? Surface these differences — they're the learning.
Watch For
Watch For
Groups landing in very different places on the scenario — that's good. If everyone agrees, push back: "What if the captain was pushing you to just get rid of the FA? Does your answer change?"
Supervisors who focus only on procedure (SOP, chain of command) without thinking about the human element. Gently redirect: "What does your FA need from you in that moment, not just the operation?"
Deeper Knowledge — Do What's Right
Assess — when it goes wrong: The most common failure here is skipping it entirely. Supervisors jump to a decision because hesitation feels like weakness. Name it: "The fastest decision is not always the right one. Assess doesn't mean pause — it means making sure you know what you're actually responding to before you respond."
Phrases for tough calls in the Communicate step: "Here's what I'm seeing, here's what I decided, and here's why." / "I may not have all the information, but here's my call right now — and I'm open to adjusting." / "I want you to hear this from me directly before it comes from anyone else." These phrases do three things: they establish authority, signal transparency, and invite dialogue without surrendering the call.
When Assess, Choose, and Communicate feel like too many steps under pressure: It is. Under real pressure, most of the framework is unconscious. What you're building today is the muscle that becomes instinct. The goal is not to run through 3 steps consciously in a crisis — it's to realize afterward that you skipped step 3 and commit to closing that loop.
When "Do What's Right" conflicts with what's operationally easy: That tension is real. Sometimes the right call is the harder one. Help supervisors name the difference between what's convenient, what's comfortable, and what's actually right. "The fact that it's hard is often a sign you're on the right track."

2:05 – 2:45  ·  40 Minutes
The Hard Conversations
Slide 19–25  ·  Communicate Openly
The Seriously Nice Paradox (Slide 20)
"Seriously Nice is one of our leadership principles, and I think it's the one we most often misapply. We think being seriously nice means being gentle, keeping the peace, not rocking the boat. But think about the most caring leader you ever had. Did they avoid hard conversations with you? Or did they care enough to tell you the truth — in a way that felt like it came from someone who was in your corner? That's what Seriously Nice actually means. Avoiding a hard conversation isn't kindness. It's avoidance. And your FAs can feel the difference."
SBI Framework Notes
Walk through SBI slowly. Give an example of each part: "On Tuesday's flight to BNA [Situation]... I noticed you walked past the passenger in 14C three times without acknowledging them [Behavior]... The impact was that she filed a complaint and said she felt invisible [Impact]."
Emphasize: Behavior, not character. "You were rude" is a character statement. "You walked past her without acknowledging her" is a behavior statement. One starts a fight. One opens a conversation.
The coaching question: after SBI, pause. "What do you think?" Then stop talking. The silence is uncomfortable. Let them fill it. What they say next is almost always exactly what you needed to hear.
Private Workbook Reflection (Slide 23)
4 Minutes — Individual, Private
Give a full 4 minutes of quiet here. This is the most important thing you'll ask them to do all day. Say: "I want you to write out a real conversation you've been putting off. You won't have to share what you wrote. But you will practice it in a few minutes. The more specific you are right now, the better the practice will be."

Go quiet. Let them write. Don't fill the silence.
Practice: The Hard Conversation (Slide 24)
Breakout Pairs — 8 Minutes
Before you send them to breakouts: "You're going to use what you just wrote — the real conversation you've been putting off. One person plays supervisor, one plays FA. Supervisor: deliver your SBI. Use the actual words you wrote. Your partner's job is to give you one piece of feedback: Was it specific? Was it about behavior or character? Did you stay in it, or did you soften the impact? Then switch."

While they're in breakouts: You can drop into a room briefly to listen — don't intervene unless someone is clearly stuck or distressed. Let the practice unfold.

Large group debrief after: Ask 2 questions: "What was hard to say out loud?" and "What did it feel like to hear it as the FA?" Surface this: most people say the impact piece is the hardest to deliver without softening it. That's the muscle you're building.

Common facilitation moments:
— If someone says "I don't have a real situation to use": push gently. "Think of the last time you had a conversation you wished you'd handled differently. Use that." Almost everyone has one.
— If the practice stays polite and surface-level: normalize going deeper. "Try it again — this time actually say the impact out loud. Don't soften it. Your partner can handle it."
— If someone gets emotional in the reflection: let it land. You don't need to fix it. "That reaction tells you this conversation matters. That's exactly why we're practicing it here."
Slido — Activity 4
Rating Scale (1–5)
"When things get hard, do you lean toward accountability or empathy?"
After results: "This is not a test. There's no right answer. But notice — most of us lean one way. The goal isn't to be in the middle. The goal is to know your default so you can flex when the situation needs something different. If you lean empathy — practice naming impact. If you lean accountability — practice asking 'what do you think?' more."
Watch For
Watch For
Supervisors who write a vague conversation ("I need to talk to someone about their attitude"). Coach them: "Can you be more specific? What did they do? When? What was the effect?" The more specific the SBI, the better the practice.
Practice that stays polite and surface-level. Push pairs gently: "That was good. Now try it again — but this time really say the impact. Don't soften it." Softening the impact is the most common habit to break.
Anyone who seems emotionally activated by the reflection. This is real for some people. If someone needs a moment, let them. Don't rush Module 4.
Deeper Knowledge — Communicate Openly
When supervisors can't name the behavior (stuck in character language): This is the most common SBI failure. "She has a bad attitude" is not a behavior — it's an interpretation. Help them get specific: "What did she do? What did you see or hear? If a camera was there, what would it show?" Observable behavior is the only thing you can address in a conversation without it becoming a debate about character.
Phrases for opening a hard conversation without it feeling like an ambush: "I want to talk to you about something I saw — and I want to hear your side too." / "This isn't about questioning who you are. I want to address a specific moment." / "I'm coming to you directly because I think you can handle this — and because I respect you." The opener sets the tone. A well-delivered opener often determines whether the conversation is heard or defended against.
When a FA gets defensive in a real conversation: Don't retreat and don't escalate. Acknowledge the emotion, then return to the behavior: "I hear you, and I'm not here to attack you. I'm here because [behavior] happened and we need to talk about it." Stay on the behavior. If they won't de-escalate, name what you're observing: "I can see you're frustrated right now. I want to finish this conversation when you're ready — but we will finish it."
"Communicate Openly" and the coaching question working together: SBI delivers the feedback. The coaching question opens the learning. Together they create a conversation, not a verdict. After you land the I in SBI — pause. Ask: "What do you think about what happened?" Then listen. What they say next is almost always what the conversation needed to be about all along.

Learning from Turbulence
Slide 28–33  ·  Promote Ownership
Re-Engagement First (Slide 27 — New)
2 Minutes to Get the Room Back After Break
Don't open Module 5 cold. Slide 27 asks: "Think of the best leader you ever had. Did they avoid hard conversations with you?"

Let 2–3 people respond before you move on. This gets energy back in the room and does the setup work for Module 5 for you — they'll name what made honest feedback feel different, and you can reflect it back: "That's exactly what this module is about." Then move to the opening frame.
Opening Frame (Slide 28)
"The debrief is the leadership development. Every hard day, every difficult situation, every near-miss with your team — that's a curriculum. If you create the space to learn from it. Most of us don't. Not because we don't care. Because we're already on to the next thing. This module is about building one habit that turns experience into growth — and it takes five minutes."
The Live Debrief (Slide 30)
Large Group — 10 Minutes
Give them 3 minutes to write their answers to all 4 questions in Section 5 of the workbook — they're not writing for you, they're writing for themselves. Then open the verbal group debrief. Ask who wants to share. Don't read out loud from the slide — you know the 4 questions; run it like a real debrief.

Watch the tone you set: Genuine curiosity. No defensiveness about the session. If something didn't work, name it yourself first. "Question 3 — what would we do differently? I'm going to start: I would've given you a little more time in the Module 3 scenario..."
Deeper Knowledge — Promote Ownership
Why most debriefs fail — and what to say when they do: When a debrief produces nothing useful, it's usually one of three things: (1) It happened too late — the moment has lost its charge. (2) It was too vague — "let's do better" isn't actionable. (3) People aren't safe — if they're afraid of being blamed, they won't be honest. Name whichever of these applies and adjust. "We're going to do a quick one right now while it's fresh. 5 minutes. No blame — just: what happened, what worked, what do we take forward."
When a FA resists the debrief ("We already talked about it"): "Talked about it" is not the same as debriefed. Usually "we talked about it" means someone vented, someone apologized, and everyone moved on. That's not a debrief — that's conflict management. The debrief is what comes after: what did we learn, and how do we use it? They're different conversations.
"Promote Ownership" and the debrief: The debrief is where ownership is built. When you ask "what would we do differently?" instead of "what did you do wrong?" you transfer ownership of the learning from the supervisor to the team. That's the whole principle. People own what they helped create — including insights about their own behavior.

Flying Together
Slide 32–35
Leader Guide Introduction
Say This — Then Share Your Screen on the Leader Guide for 60 Seconds
"Before we close out — I want to show you one more thing. Everything we covered today lives in one place. It's called the Leader Guide and it's yours. It's not a training you have to take again. It's organized by the same five principles you've been seeing all morning, and it lives in your browser — open it any time you need to reference a tool, revisit a framework, or find a phrase when you're about to have a hard conversation. I'm dropping the link in Teams chat right now. This is your resource from here."
→ Share screen on Leader Guide. Point to the principle tabs and show one example (the 30-Second Check-In or SBI card). Keep it to 60–90 seconds — they've had a full session, don't add new content. Then switch back to the presentation.
Talking Points
"You are not alone in this. But that's easy to say and hard to feel — especially when you're at your base, dealing with a situation at 6am, and the nearest peer is 2,000 miles away. That's what accountability partners are for. This isn't a mentoring relationship. It's just: someone who gets it. Someone you can call."
Announce the pairs you've pre-planned. Give people 2 minutes to connect with their partner in the Teams chat before you close out. Seed the first message: "Start by just saying hi and dropping one thing you're taking away from today."
Float the idea of a monthly supervisor touchpoint — not operational, just theirs. Plant the seed: "I'm thinking about setting up a standing 30-minute call — just the 14 of you. No agenda from me. What would you use it for?"
Slido — Activity 5 (Closing Word Cloud)
Word Cloud — Leave it Live Through Your Close
"In one word — what are you taking away from today?"
Share the word cloud on screen. Read a few words out loud. Let the visual be the backdrop for your closing remarks. Don't rush off it — let it sit.
Deeper Knowledge — Flying Together
Why isolation is the real leadership risk for supervisors at remote bases: These supervisors don't have hallway conversations. They don't overhear how a peer handled a situation. They don't get informal modeling. That isolation means they're building leadership instincts in a vacuum — and usually defaulting to how they themselves were managed. The accountability partner structure breaks that loop.
If supervisors are skeptical about the accountability partner idea: Don't oversell it. "I'm not asking you to become best friends. I'm asking you to send one message in 30 days. That's it. If it's useful, you'll know. If it isn't — you're no worse off." Low commitment, low risk. High upside if it sticks.
"Flying Together" and all 5 principles: Module 6 is where all five principles converge. Being seriously nice is choosing to invest in a peer relationship. Championing others means not just supporting your FAs but supporting the supervisors around you. Doing what's right means showing up for the group even when it's easier to go it alone. Communicating openly means being real with your accountability partner when things are hard. Promoting ownership means taking responsibility for the health of this network — not waiting for someone to build it for you.

Commitments & Close
Slide 38–39
Post-Training Survey
Direct Them to the Survey — Before the Lightning Round
Say: "Before we do our final close — scroll to the very bottom of your workbook. You'll see a Session Feedback section. It takes about 3 minutes. Your responses go directly to me and help shape the next session. No one sees your answers except the L&D team. Go ahead and fill it out now while the session is still fresh — I'll give you 3 minutes."

Go quiet. Let them complete it. When time is up, say: "Hit submit whenever you're ready — then come back up to the chat." Then move into the lightning round.
Lightning Round (Slide 34 — New)
Before the Closing Word Cloud
Ask everyone to drop one thing in the Teams chat — 10 words or less — something specific they're doing differently for their FAs next week. Give them 60–90 seconds. Read a few out loud before you open Slido. This is the energy you want going into your close — their voices, not yours.
Closing Script
Your Closing (After Word Cloud)
"Before we go — I want you to look at that word cloud for a second. That's you. That's 14 people who came from different bases, spent four hours doing real work, had honest conversations, and are leaving with something. That's not nothing. That's actually pretty rare. The commitments you wrote in your workbook — those are yours. Nobody's grading you on them. But your FAs will feel whether you made them. And I will be back in 30 days with one question. Just one. And I want a real answer. Go lead. You know how."
The 30-Day Follow-Up
Commit to This in the Room — Then Do It
Send this Teams message at exactly 30 days:
"30 days ago you made a commitment. Here's my question: did you make the call to your accountability partner? And what's one thing that's different about how you're leading your FAs because of our time together? I want a real answer — even if the real answer is 'I got busy and I didn't.' That's data too."
Set a calendar reminder today before you close out. This follow-up is what separates a training from a habit.
After You Close
Stay on for 5–10 minutes after the formal close. Let people linger. This is often where the most honest conversations happen.
Send a follow-up Teams message within 24 hours: thank them, drop the Leader Guide link again, remind them of their accountability partner's name and base.
At 30 days — send the follow-up message above. Don't skip this. The commitment page in the workbook primes them to expect it — and your FAs will feel whether they followed through.
