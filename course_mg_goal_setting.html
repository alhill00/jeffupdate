Foundation
The Goals Nobody Thinks About After January
Jordan set clear quarterly goals with her team in January. She was thorough — every goal was documented, shared, and agreed to. By March, nobody was thinking about them. Not because they were lazy. Not because the goals were bad.
The goals were handed to them. Not built with them.
Goals people build, they own. Goals handed to them, they track — when reminded. The difference in engagement isn't the goal. It's who made it.
Co-creation is the highest-leverage move in goal-setting. The time you spend building a goal with your Team Member is the investment that makes them care about it for the rest of the quarter.
Foundation
Two Goal Failure Modes
Most goal problems come from one of two directions — both of which make the goal feel pointless.

Activity Goals

We did 50 things. We held 12 meetings. We completed the checklist. The team was busy — but nobody can say what changed as a result of all that activity.

Outcome Goals

We reduced onboarding time by 30%. We closed the backlog. Guest satisfaction in this area went from 72 to 81. Something measurably changed.

Impossible Goals

Aspirational but unachievable — so the team disconnects from them immediately. Nobody works hard toward something they've already stopped believing in.

Stretch Goals

Hard enough to require real effort. Achievable enough to sustain belief. The best goals sit at the edge of what the team thinks is possible.

What Makes a Goal Motivating
Not every goal motivates equally. Research on goal motivation points to four factors — and you can influence all of them.

Clarity

We know what "done" looks like — specifically enough that we'd agree if we got there. Vague goals produce vague effort.

Challenge

The goal is hard enough to require real effort. Easy goals don't produce pride or growth. Stretch is the point.

Meaning

The person can see why it matters — to the team, to Guests, to the org. "Because leadership said so" is not meaning.

Autonomy

They have some ownership over how they get there — not just what the goal is. Ownership over the path = commitment to the destination.

A goal that lacks clarity, challenge, meaning, or autonomy will underperform — even if the person executing it is capable and well-intentioned.
The Goal Framework
OKR-Style Goal Structure
OKRs — Objectives and Key Results — give your goals a structure that distinguishes what you're trying to achieve from how you'll know you achieved it.
Objective
What we're trying to accomplish. Inspiring, qualitative, clear enough to guide decisions — even when specific metrics aren't available. Should answer: "Why does this matter?"
Example: "Make our onboarding experience something new Team Members talk about in month 1 and beyond."
Key Results
How we know we achieved the Objective. 2–3 measurable indicators — not activities, but outcomes. Each should be falsifiable: you can tell if you hit it or not.
Example: "New hire satisfaction score above 85% at Day 30. Time-to-productivity under 6 weeks. 90-day retention above 95%."
Milestones
Check-in points every 4–6 weeks. Not to micromanage — to surface whether we're on the path before it's too late to adjust.
Set the check-in rhythm in the goal-setting conversation itself — not later.
The co-creation rule: The best goals are co-created, not handed down. Bring the draft, ask for their input, and finalize together. This single step changes ownership.
Foundation
The Goal Conversation — Four Moves
Setting the goal is not enough. How you have the conversation determines whether they'll own it.
1
Start with "why." Connect the goal to something that matters — to the team, to the Guest, to their own growth. "Leadership asked me to set this goal" is not why.
2
Ask for their input before finalizing. "What would you add to this?" or "Does this feel right given what you know about the work?" Their fingerprint on the goal is the ownership transfer.
3
Agree on how you'll measure it. Not whether it was done — whether it was actually achieved. Define success in concrete, observable terms before you start.
4
Set the check-in rhythm now. In the goal-setting conversation itself — not "we'll figure it out." Specific date or cadence: "We'll review this every 4 weeks in your 1:1."
The Goal Framework
Busy But Not Moving
Your Team Member is completing tasks every week — their to-do list gets checked off, they're busy, they show up engaged. But the larger project goal hasn't moved in six weeks. What do you do?
AAdd more tasks to their list — clearly the scope isn't enough to move the needle.
BRevisit the goal together — are the tasks actually connected to the outcome, or just adjacent to it?
CExtend the deadline — they're working hard, the timeline was probably too aggressive.
DHave a performance conversation — consistent task completion without progress is a problem.
The Goal Framework
The Business Changed. The Goal Didn't.
A Team Member's quarterly goal becomes irrelevant midway through — a business change shifted priorities in a way that makes the original target no longer meaningful. What do you do?
AHold the Team Member to the original goal anyway — changing mid-quarter undermines accountability.
BFormally revise the goal with the Team Member to reflect current reality — explain what changed and what the new target is.
CLet the goal lapse quietly and set a new one next quarter.
DReplace it with a harder goal to compensate for the lost time and momentum.

Setting a Goal for Something Hard to Measure
You want to set a goal around "improving team culture" for a Team Member in a leadership-adjacent role. How do you make a culture goal measurable?
ASkip the measurement — culture goals are inherently qualitative and shouldn't be reduced to numbers.
BDefine observable behaviors and leading indicators — what would we see if culture improved? Use that as the Key Results.
CUse a general rating like "culture feels better" and assess at quarter-end.
DWait for a survey score to be available — only measurable things can be goals.
Practice
The Goal Is Too Easy
A Team Member sets a quarterly goal in your goal-setting conversation that you know they'll hit by week 3. It's well within their current capability — there's no stretch to it. How do you respond?
AAccept the goal — they built it, so they own it. Forcing a harder goal undermines autonomy.
BAsk what they think would happen if they pushed the goal 20–30% further — open the conversation about stretch before finalizing.
CReject the goal and set a harder one yourself.
DAccept it and add a second "bonus" goal they can pursue if they finish early.
Practice
The Off-Track Goal Check-In
A Team Member's goal is clearly off track at the midpoint. You need to address it in your 1:1 without it feeling like a performance conversation. What's your approach?
AName the gap directly: "You're behind on this goal — what's your plan?"
BAsk how they think things are tracking against the goal, then work through what's in the way and what would help.
CExtend the deadline quietly and revisit at quarter end.
DSkip the goal discussion this 1:1 — they probably know they're behind and don't need you to point it out.
