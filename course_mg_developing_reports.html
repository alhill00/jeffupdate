The Person Who Left Because She Stopped Growing
Lauren was capable, reliable, and consistently strong. Her manager assumed she was fine — she was hitting her goals, the relationship was good, no red flags. No extra attention needed.
Six months later, she left. Exit interview: "I didn't feel like I was growing. I wasn't learning anything new. I felt like I'd hit a ceiling."
Her manager was blindsided. From the outside, Lauren had everything she needed. From the inside, she'd been quietly disengaging for months because nobody was intentionally investing in her next chapter.
Development isn't about programs. It's about intentional conversations and deliberate stretch — and it's what separates the managers who keep their best people from the ones who lose them to organizations that pay attention.
Foundation
The Development Myth — and the Fix
The most common reason managers give for not developing their people: "I don't have time." Here's the math on what it actually takes.

What Managers Think Development Is

  • Training programs and workshops
  • Career pathing decks
  • Annual development plans that nobody reads
  • Something HR owns

What Development Actually Is

  • One career conversation per quarter
  • One development check-in per month in 1:1
  • One stretch assignment per person
  • Real-time feedback on the stretch work
Three deliberate conversations per quarter plus one stretch assignment per person. That's the whole program. It takes less time than you think — and produces more than any training platform.
Foundation
Development vs. Performance Management
These are not the same conversation. Mixing them up makes both less effective.

Development

  • Forward-looking — where are we going?
  • Chosen — the person has input
  • Builds on strengths and ambitions
  • Stretch by design
  • The person is invested in it

Performance Management

  • Backward-looking — what happened?
  • Reactive — addressing a gap
  • Addresses what's falling short
  • Often structured and formal
  • The person may feel defensive
Keep them in separate conversations. When development feedback appears in a performance conversation, the person hears it as corrective even when it isn't. They deserve a conversation where growth is the whole point.
The Development Plan
Three Components — and How Often to Have Them

1. Where They Want to Go

Their goals — not your assumption about their goals. Ask, don't assume. "What would feel meaningful to be really good at a year from now?" gets you further than "where do you see yourself in 5 years?"

Conversation cadence: one career conversation per quarter

2. The Gap

What they have vs. what they need to get where they want to go. Not a deficit list — a map. "Here's what I see in you that's already there, and here's what the next level requires that we can build toward."

Conversation cadence: development check-in monthly in 1:1

3. The Path

Three levers: stretch assignments (experiences that build the capability), skill feedback (real-time coaching on the stretch work), and connections (relationships that open doors they can't open from where they sit now).

Real-time: feedback on stretch work as it happens
Ask, don't assume. The career conversation most managers have starts with "where do you want to be?" The better opener: "What parts of your work right now feel most alive to you?" Start with energy, not destination.
Foundation
The Stretch Zone
Development happens in the stretch zone — not the comfort zone, and not the panic zone. Your job is to calibrate the challenge and provide the structure for learning.

Comfort Zone

Already mastered. No growth happening. Safe but stagnant.

Stretch Zone

Hard but achievable with effort and support. This is where development lives.

Panic Zone

Too far beyond current skill. Leads to failure without growth. Builds fear, not capability.

The Manager's Job in the Stretch Zone:
🎯
Calibrate the stretch. Match challenge to current skill level — hard enough to grow, not so hard they're set up to fail.
🤝
Provide support — not solutions. Be available, ask coaching questions, but don't rescue the work. The difficulty is where the growth is.
💬
Debrief the experience. After the stretch: "What did you learn?" Not "how did it go?" — learning, not just reporting.
The Development Plan
The Team Member With No Direction
A Team Member tells you she doesn't know what she wants to do long-term. "I don't really have a five-year plan." How do you start a useful career conversation?
ASkip the career conversation until she figures out what she wants — no point without a direction.
BAsk questions that explore what energizes her now — start with what's most alive in her current work, not what she wants in the abstract.
CTell her what you think she should develop based on what you've observed.
DConnect her with HR for a career assessment — they have the tools for this.
The Development Plan
Struggling Midway Through a Stretch
You gave a Team Member a stretch assignment. Midway through, she's struggling — the scope is larger than she expected, she's not sure how to move forward, and she's starting to lose confidence. What do you do?
ATake the assignment back — protect the outcome and give her something more appropriate.
BCoach her through it — ask what's hard, what she's tried, what she needs — without solving it for her.
CReduce the scope of the assignment to something she can achieve confidently.
DBring in another Team Member to help without telling her — protect the outcome quietly.
Practice
Developing Someone Who's Already Strong
One of your Team Members is already excellent — high performer, well above expectations. She doesn't have obvious gaps. What does development look like for her?
ALeave her alone — she's exceeding expectations, so there's nothing to develop. Development is for people with gaps.
BFocus on expanding her scope and visibility — stretch assignments beyond her current lane, exposure to higher-level conversations, and connection to opportunities she can't yet see.
CAsk her to mentor others — she's reached the ceiling of her development so she should invest in the team.
DMove her into a harder role — she's ready and further development requires a title change.
Practice
The Person Who Wants to Stay Put
A Team Member tells you clearly: "I'm not interested in moving up. I love what I do and I want to keep doing it well." What does development look like for someone who doesn't want advancement?
ASkip the career conversation — if they don't want to advance, there's nothing to develop.
BFocus on deepening expertise, staying current, and finding meaning in their current contribution — depth, not height.
CEncourage them to reconsider — staying put isn't a sustainable path in a growing organization.
DAssign development goals anyway — every Team Member needs a formal development plan regardless of their preference.
Practice
Real-Time Developmental Feedback
After observing a Team Member handle a difficult Guest interaction well but miss an opportunity to de-escalate earlier, you want to give developmental feedback in the moment. How do you frame it so it doesn't feel like criticism?
A"You handled that well — but you should have de-escalated sooner."
B"You recovered that really well. What did you notice about the moment before things got tense? I want to share an observation I had about an earlier opportunity."
CSave it for the next 1:1 — real-time feedback in the moment feels like criticism.
D"There's something you could have done differently — can I share it?"

You've finished Developing Your Direct Reports. The managers who keep their best people longest are the ones who make growth visible, intentional, and ongoing — not accidental.

What development-focused managers do differently:

Have one career conversation with every Team Member this quarter — not annual.
Assign one stretch task per person — then debrief what they learned from it.
Distinguish development feedback from performance feedback — keep them in separate conversations.
Start career conversations with energy questions, not destination questions.
Build their stretch tolerance, not their dependency on you.
