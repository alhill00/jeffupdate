The New FA Gap
Most supervisory touchpoints happen reactively — when something goes wrong or when the FA reaches out first. The highest-risk window isn't the first solo flight or the belonging question — it's the three weeks before the FA ever reaches your base. While they're in training, they're already forming their first impression of what their supervisor relationship will look like. By the time they arrive at your base, someone has already reached out to them. Make sure it's you.
The 30/60/90 Framework
Four Touchpoints. One Habit.
Day 0 — Pre-Arrival Welcome (while they're still in training): "Hey — I know you're still in training. I'm [your name], your base supervisor in [city]. I'm looking forward to having you on the team. Reach out if you have any questions before you get here." That's it. They arrive already knowing your name.
After first solo flights — First-Flight Check-In: "Hey, I know you just finished your first trips — I wanted to check in before you tell me how it went. What surprised you? What are you proud of?"
Around day 45–60 — First Hard Thing: "You're a couple months in now — what's been the toughest part so far?"
Around day 75–90 — Belonging Check: "Do you feel like part of the team yet? Not the job — the team."

When the Pressure's On
Do What's Right
Do What's Right — Assess → Choose → Communicate
When pressure hits, most supervisors skip straight to Choose. Use these questions to work through each step — you don't need all the answers, just the right ones before you act.
Assess: What's actually happening — and what am I assuming is happening? Who's affected? What do I control right now? Do I have 60 more seconds, or do I need to move?
Choose: What's the best call with what I know right now — not the perfect call, the best available one? Do company policies or procedures speak to this? If my call aligns, lean on that. If the situation goes beyond policy, be clear on my reasoning. Is this reversible? Can I stand behind this call and explain it?
Communicate: Who's watching this and doesn't know what's happening? What do they need — information, a decision, or reassurance? Have I shared both the call and the reason? Did I close the loop when it resolved?

The Other Hard Conversation
SBI is for corrective feedback — when you've seen something specific. But there's a different conversation: the one you have when an FA who used to be engaged isn't anymore. No observable behavior. Just a feeling — something is off.
The Retention Conversation — Three Parts
1. Name what you noticed — without making it an accusation: "I've noticed you seem a little different lately — not in a bad way. I just wanted to check in."
2. Ask the real question: "Are you doing okay? Are you good in this role?"
3. Stop talking. Wait for the actual answer. No structure. Just presence and a direct question from someone who clearly wants to hear the answer.

The 5 Leadership Principles
Your foundation
These aren't aspirational values — they're the standard. Every framework in this guide connects back to one of these five. When you're not sure what to do in a moment, come back to which principle applies and let it orient you.
Be Seriously Nice
Genuine care over comfortable silence. You're the one who stays in the hard conversation because you care too much to walk away. The "seriously" is what separates it from just being pleasant.
Champion Others
Invest in your FAs as people, not just performers. Check in genuinely, ask before you answer, recognize specifically. Do this consistently and your team will run through walls for you.
Do What's Right
Make the call, own it, communicate it. Even with incomplete information, a clear decision you stand behind is better than paralysis or politics. Then tell people what you did and why.
Communicate Openly
Say the thing. Specific, honest, early. Hard truths delivered with care are the most respectful thing you can offer someone — far more respectful than protecting them from a conversation they deserve to have.
Promote Ownership
Create the conditions for your FAs to own their work. Ask instead of tell. Debrief instead of move on. People grow when they build the answer themselves — not when someone hands it to them.
Be Seriously Nice
Module 1 Principle
Be Seriously Nice is the most misunderstood of the five principles. It doesn't mean being gentle or avoiding conflict. It means you genuinely care — and that care is what makes you willing to stay in hard conversations instead of taking the easy exit. The "serious" part is doing it even when it's uncomfortable. The "nice" part is doing it in a way that keeps the person whole.
What it looks like for inflight supervisors
Calling an FA after a rough flight — not to debrief the operation, but to ask how they're doing.
Addressing a pattern early, before it becomes a formal issue, because you care too much to let it grow unchecked.
Being the one person who tells them the truth — not the one who softens every message until it means nothing.
Staying fully present in a hard conversation instead of looking for the exit ramp. Being there completely, even when it's uncomfortable.
When you feel yourself wanting to avoid
Ask yourself: am I protecting them — or protecting myself? Usually it's the latter. The message you're softening is the message they need to hear.
When you've waited too long: start with this — "I should have said this sooner. I want to say it now because I respect you." Then go.
When the relationship feels fragile: the conversations you avoid are the ones that slowly erode trust. The ones you have — carefully and honestly — are the ones that build it.
Common mistake: Confusing niceness with avoidance. Keeping the peace to protect yourself from an uncomfortable moment isn't being seriously nice — it's protecting yourself while your FA misses the chance to grow.
The 3 Daily Tools
Module 2 · Champion Others
Three things you can do with your FAs every day — not in a formal conversation, not in a big meeting, but in the small moments that are actually where leadership happens. None of them require more than a few minutes. All of them compound over time.
Tool 1 — The 30-Second Check-In
What it is: A brief, genuine human connection — not a status update. Three steps: Open (real question, not operational), Listen (actually wait for the answer, don't fill the silence), Respond (acknowledge what you heard before you move on).
When to use it: Before a shift starts. Before you end a call. After a hard flight. After anything difficult. It takes 30 seconds and it signals: you're seen, not just managed.
The "actually" matters: "How are you actually doing?" signals you want a real answer, not a reflexive "fine." Try it once. Notice the difference in what you hear back.
Try this
"Before we get into the brief — how are you actually doing?"
"That was a hard shift. I just want to check in before we move on to the next thing."
"I saw what happened on that flight. Before we talk about next steps — how are you?"
Common mistake: Asking "how are you?" while already thinking about the next thing. Your FA can feel that. Make it real or don't do it — a performative check-in is worse than none at all.
Tool 2 — The Coaching Question
What it is: "What do you think?" — the question that builds ownership instead of dependency. When you give answers, your FAs follow your lead. When you ask what they think, they own the answer — and the next one.
When to use it: When a FA brings you a problem you could solve in 10 seconds. Pause. Ask first. You'll be surprised how often they already know. Your job is to help them trust that they know.
When they say "I don't know": Don't give the answer. Try "What's your best guess?" or "If you had to make a call right now, what would it be?" Most "I don't know" responses are about risk aversion, not lack of knowledge.
Try this
"What do you think?"
"What would you do if I wasn't available?"
"What options do you see right now?"
"What's your gut telling you?"
"What have you already tried?"
Common mistake: Asking the question and then immediately filling the silence with your own answer. The pause is the point. The discomfort of waiting is exactly where the learning happens — for them and for you.
Tool 3 — The Recognition Hit
What it is: Specific, timely, genuine. Name the exact behavior. Say why it mattered to the team, the guest, or the operation. Do it close to when it happened. Not a trophy — a moment they'll actually remember.
The formula: [Name] + [what they did, specifically] + [why it mattered]. Three parts. Done in under 30 seconds. That's the whole thing.
Don't wait for a big moment: Notice the small things — the FA who de-escalated quietly, the one who checked on a nervous flyer without being asked, the one who stayed to help when they didn't have to. Those moments deserve to be named.
Try this
"The way you handled that passenger in row 12 — staying calm, getting ahead of it — that kept the whole cabin steady. That was exactly right."
"I noticed you checked on the nervous flyer in 4A before anyone asked. That's the kind of thing that makes a difference to that person's whole experience."
"You didn't have to stay and help with that turnaround. You did. The team noticed, and I noticed."
Common mistake: Generic recognition. "You did a great job today" means nothing. "You handled the delay communication before guests had a reason to be frustrated — that's exactly the standard" means everything. Specificity is what makes it stick.
Assess → Choose → Communicate
Module 3 · Do What's Right
A three-step decision framework for moments when you have limited time, limited information, and everyone is watching you to know how to feel. It doesn't tell you what to decide — every situation is different. It gives you the right questions to ask yourself at each step so you don't skip one. Most supervisors are decent at Assess and Choose. Nearly everyone underinvests in Communicate — and that's usually where things fall apart.
Navigator — guiding questions for each step
Step 1 — Assess
What's actually happening, and what do I need to know before I act?
· What's actually happening right now — and what am I assuming is happening? (These are often different.)
· Who's affected, and what's the scope?
· What's the worst case if I wait 60 more seconds? Do I have that time?
· What do I actually control here, right now?
Step 2 — Choose
What's the best call I can make right now?
· What's the best call with what I know right now — not the perfect call, the best available one?
· Do company policies or procedures speak to this situation? If my call aligns, lean on that. If the situation goes beyond what policy covers, be clear on my reasoning and prepared to explain it.
· Is this reversible? If yes, decide faster. If not, take one more beat on Assess.
· Can I explain this call clearly and stand behind it? If yes — that's the call.
Step 3 — Communicate
Who needs to know, and what do they need from me?
· Who's watching this situation and doesn't know what's happening? Start there.
· What do they need from me — information, a decision, or just reassurance that someone is in control?
· Have I shared both the call and the reason? The reason is what builds trust.
· Did I close the loop when the situation resolved, or did I just go quiet?
Phrases for communicating under pressure
"Here's what I'm seeing, here's what I decided, and here's why."
"I may not have all the information yet, but here's my call right now — and I'm open to adjusting as we learn more."
"I want you to hear this from me directly before it comes from anywhere else."
"We're still assessing. I'll have a clear update for you in [X] minutes — sit tight."
When assess goes wrong
You jumped to a decision because hesitation felt like weakness. Most of the time, the mess that follows wasn't because the call was wrong — it was because you didn't know what you were actually responding to.
You conflated what's convenient with what's right. Those are different things. "Do What's Right" means sometimes the right call is the harder one. The fact that it's hard is often a sign you're on the right track.
You made the call but didn't close the Communicate loop. If key people found out through someone else — that's a Communicate failure, not an Assess or Choose failure. Fix that loop next time.
Common mistake: Skipping Assess because hesitation feels like weakness. The 10 seconds you spend on Assess often saves you 10 minutes of cleanup after a poor call. Fast and right beats fast and wrong every time.
The SBI Model
Module 4 · Communicate Openly
SBI is a framework for giving feedback that's specific, fair, and actionable — without turning into a character attack. Situation anchors the conversation in fact. Behavior names what happened without editorializing. Impact makes the conversation real and gives the other person a reason to care. Together, they create a feedback moment that's hard to argue with because it stays on what happened — not who someone is.
The three parts — and why each one matters
Situation: Set the specific time and place. "On Tuesday's flight to BNA" is a Situation. "You always do this" is not. Anchoring in a specific moment removes the "but that's not who I am" defense before it starts — because you're not talking about who they are, you're talking about what happened at a specific moment.
Behavior: Name what they did — observable action only. "You walked past the passenger in 14C without acknowledging her" is a Behavior. "You were rude" is a character statement. One opens a conversation. One starts a fight. If you can't describe it as something a camera would have captured — you're not at the Behavior level yet.
Impact: Say what it cost — the team, the guest, the operation, or you. Don't soften this. "It kind of affected morale a little" is not an Impact. "Three FAs came to me after that shift and said they didn't feel supported" is an Impact. Your FA can't change what they don't fully understand. Say it plainly.
How to open the conversation
"I want to talk to you about something I observed — and I want to hear your side too."
"This isn't about questioning who you are. I want to address a specific moment."
"I'm coming to you directly because I think you can handle this, and because I respect you."
After SBI — the coaching question
After you deliver the Impact — pause. Ask: "What do you think about what happened?" Then stop talking. The silence will feel uncomfortable. That's okay. Let them fill it.
What they say next is almost always exactly what the conversation needed to be about all along. When you ask instead of lecture after SBI, it becomes coaching — not correction.
When they get defensive: Don't retreat and don't escalate. "I hear you, and I'm not here to attack you. I'm here because [behavior] happened and we need to talk about it." Return to the behavior. Stay there. The conversation becomes coaching the moment they feel safe enough to be honest.
Common mistake: Softening the Impact until it has no meaning. If you end with "and I just want us to be a good team," you've lost the whole point of the conversation. Land the Impact clearly — then ask the coaching question. The combination is what makes the conversation stick.
The 4-Question Debrief
Take-Home Reference  ·  Promote Ownership
The debrief is where ownership is built. When you create space to learn from hard situations — instead of just moving on to the next thing — you turn experience into growth. Five minutes, four questions, works anywhere. Use it after a conflict, a difficult passenger interaction, a crew moment that didn't go as planned, or even a great shift you want to understand better. Use it with new FAs specifically after their first rough trips — it's one of the most powerful belonging signals a supervisor can send.
The four questions — in order (order matters)
Q1 — What happened? Facts only first. Get everyone on the same page before drawing conclusions. This alone often surfaces things people saw differently.
Q2 — What worked? Find this before you go to what didn't. Even in the worst situations, something worked. Skip Q2 and the debrief becomes a blame session — even when you don't mean it to.
Q3 — What would we do differently? Forward-looking, not "what went wrong." Specific enough to act on: not "communicate better" but "tell the gate agent first before the captain."
Q4 — What do we take forward? One clear agreed-on takeaway. If you can't name one everyone agrees on — the debrief isn't finished.
How to open it
"Before we move on, I want to spend five minutes on what just happened. Four questions — honest answers. No blame. What did we learn?"
"That was a hard one. I want to debrief it right now while it's fresh. Five minutes."
"I want to run our 4-question debrief. Q1: What happened? Walk me through it."
Use it with new FAs specifically
Running a 5-minute debrief after a new FA's first rough flight is one of the most powerful belonging signals a supervisor can send. It says: what you went through matters, and we learn from it together.
Ask Q2 (what worked?) with new FAs even when nothing felt like it worked. They almost always did something right. Finding it and naming it builds confidence faster than any amount of training.
Common mistake: Starting with Q3 (what went wrong) without doing Q2 first. The moment you skip "what worked," the whole exercise becomes a blame session. Q2 is not optional — it's what makes everything else possible.
