Breakout Group Planning
Principles
Module 1 — 7 cross-base pairs
Never pair someone with their own base supervisor. The whole point is building new connections across bases.
Module 3 — groups of 3–4
New groupings for the journey builder activity. Groups build their FA journey map in the online tool and submit before returning to the full group.
Module 4 — groups of 4–5
Collapse into 3 rooms of 4–5. Larger groups create more debate on the scenario, which is the point. Mix bases.
How to Assign in Teams
Use Teams breakout rooms. For Module 1, create 7 rooms (labeled Room 1–7) and assign cross-base pairs in advance. For Module 3, create 3–4 rooms for the group breakout. For Module 4, reuse rooms but reassign into groups of 4–5.
Give a clear time check when you open rooms: "You have [X] minutes — I'll call you back at [TIME]." Set a Teams timer if you use one.
For Module 4 groups: collapse pairs into 3 rooms of 4–5 (put 2 pairs per room, with one room taking a third pair if needed). Give them 10 minutes on the scenario.

0:20 – 0:50  ·  30 Minutes
Who We Are
Slide 5–7  ·  Be Seriously Nice
Purpose
This module exists for one reason: to make the room feel safe. If people don't feel connected to each other, nothing in Modules 3 and 4 will work. Don't shortchange this.
Talking Points
Frame the cross-base breakout: "We rarely get all of us in the same room. Your counterpart in BOS is dealing with something you've already solved in BNA — and vice versa. That's about to change."
Set the breakout tone: "This is a safe space. What you share stays in this room — or in that breakout room, specifically. Be honest. Be curious. Ask follow-up questions."
For large group debrief: ask 2–3 people to share what surprised them. Look for the themes: usually it's some version of "we're all dealing with the same thing and didn't know it."
Close the module with: "The supervisor across the country from you isn't your competition. They're your most underused resource. We're going to do something about that today."
Third breakout prompt — add this after the two existing questions: "And one more: tell your partner about a new FA at your base right now — someone in their first year. How are they doing? Do you actually know?" This seeds Module 3. Some supervisors will realize in this conversation they don't know. Don't comment on it yet — just let it land.
Watch For
Watch For
People keeping it surface-level in the breakout (talking about weather, delays, easy stuff). That's okay for the first 2 minutes. The second question — what keeps you up at night when you think about leading your base — is the one that matters. Give them the full 8 minutes.
Anyone who seems reluctant to share in large group debrief. Don't put people on the spot — invite, don't require. "Anyone from a pair where something landed — feel free to share."
Deeper Knowledge — Be Seriously Nice
What the principle actually means for hard moments: "Be Seriously Nice" is not the soft principle. It's the hardest one. Being seriously nice means you care enough to stay in an uncomfortable conversation rather than exit. It means you tell the truth with warmth, not avoidance with politeness. Every module today connects back to it.
When someone pushes back on "niceness" as weakness: Name it directly — "This isn't about being a pushover. Seriously Nice means you never sacrifice honesty for comfort. The 'serious' part is doing it even when it's hard. The 'nice' part is doing it in a way that keeps the person whole." These aren't opposites.
What this looks like on a hard shift: A seriously nice supervisor is the one who calls the FA after a bad flight — not to debrief the operation, but to ask how they're doing. That's it. That one call does more for retention and loyalty than any recognition program.
Module 1 Close — Tie Back to Principle
Before you move to Module 2, land the principle. Don't rush past it: "Everything you just did — asking honest questions, actually listening, sharing something you don't usually say out loud — that's what Be Seriously Nice looks like in practice. It's not about being pleasant. It's about genuinely caring about the person across from you. And you just practiced it."

Then move. Don't add more. That landing is enough.

0:50 – 1:22  ·  32 Minutes
Your Daily Toolkit
Slide 8–14  ·  Champion Others
Opening Question First (Slide 9 — New)
Before You Explain Anything — Ask First
Slide 9 asks participants which of the 3 tools they already do consistently — A, B, or C — in the Teams chat. Read what comes in before moving to the tools slide.

This changes the dynamic completely: you're no longer delivering information, you're responding to what they just told you about themselves. Use their answers to frame the tool explanations — "Several of you said C — recognition. So let's start with why that one is actually the hardest to do consistently..."
Talking Points — The Three Tools
30-Second Check-In: "This is not a status update. It's not 'did you get the memo.' It's: how are you? And then actually listening to the answer. Thirty seconds. Before a shift. Before you hang up. It costs you nothing and it changes everything for your FA."
The Coaching Question: "When a FA comes to you with a problem, your instinct is to solve it. That's what got you promoted. But every time you solve it for them, you take an opportunity away from them. Try this: before you give the answer, ask — 'What do you think?' You'll be surprised how often they know."
More coaching question examples you can use or share: "What would you do if I wasn't here?" / "What's your gut telling you?" / "What options do you see?" / "What have you already tried?" / "What would you tell a colleague in this same situation?" — These all do the same thing: they return ownership to the FA instead of reinforcing dependency on you.
The Recognition Hit: "Not a trophy. Not a monthly email. A specific moment where you name the exact thing they did and say why it mattered. 'Hey, the way you handled that passenger in row 12 yesterday — that was textbook. I want you to know I saw it.' That's it. That's the whole thing."
Teams Chat Discussion — After Tools Overview
2 Minutes — Teams Chat (Slide 11)
Ask everyone to drop A, B, or C in the Teams chat: which of the 3 tools do they use the least consistently? A = 30-Second Check-In, B = Coaching Question, C = Recognition Hit.

After responses come in: Call out what you see. "Looks like [A/B/C] is the most common — let's talk about why that one is the hardest." Don't move on without naming the pattern. This makes the tools feel personal, not generic. Keep it to 2 minutes — you're about to practice one of them.
Deeper Knowledge — Champion Others
What champions actually do differently: They have a habit of noticing — specifically what someone did, why it was good, and when to say it. Most supervisors wait for a big moment to recognize. Champions do it constantly for small moments. A specific, timely "I saw that" is worth more than a quarterly recognition email.
When a FA seems resistant to check-ins: Some FAs will deflect ("I'm fine, don't worry about me"). Don't interpret that as "they don't need it." It usually means they're not used to it. Stay consistent. After 3–4 genuine check-ins, most FAs start opening up. The habit is on you, not on them.
The coaching question with a FA who always says "I don't know": Don't give the answer. Try: "What's your best guess?" or "If you had to make a call right now, what would it be?" Those reframes bypass the reflex. Most "I don't know" responses are about risk-aversion, not actual lack of knowledge.
Tool 4 — The Base Pulse
Frame it this way: "The three tools we just covered are all one-on-one. They work. But you have 30, 40, maybe 50 FAs across your base — and you can't have a 30-second check-in with every one of them every week. So how do you stay informed? How do you know which of your FAs is quietly struggling before they submit a resignation? This is a five-minute habit. Once a week. Three questions. That's all."
Question 1 — Who haven't I heard from in the last 10 days? Not because they reached out with a problem — just at all. The FA who goes quiet before she quits is different from the FA who calls with a complaint. Silence is data.
Question 2 — Who's been giving me short answers lately? You reach out, they respond with two words. That's a signal worth noticing.
Question 3 — Who's been calling out more than their normal pattern? This isn't a disciplinary question — it's a wellbeing question. Increased call-outs often mean something is going on. The supervisor who notices and reaches out — not to address the call-outs, but to ask how they're doing — is the one who finds out before it's too late.
"This isn't surveillance. It's care at scale. It's how you make sure no one falls through the cracks when you're managing a team you can't see every day."
Run the Scan Right Now — 3 Minutes Individual
Ask supervisors to open their workbook to Tool 4 and actually run the scan on their base — in their head, right now. Write a name in each row, or write "clear." Give them 2–3 minutes of quiet. Then ask: "Did anyone land on a name they weren't expecting? Who wants to share what they noticed?" Don't require sharing — but this usually opens a real conversation.

The New FA Experience
Slide 16–20  ·  Champion Others
Purpose
This module does two things: it surfaces how little supervisors know about what new FAs actually go through, and it gives them a concrete 30/60/90 framework to fix that. The build-it activity does the heavy lifting — supervisors discover the gap themselves, which lands completely differently than being told about it.
Phase 1 — Opening Question (2 min)
Say This to Open
"Quick question before we start — and I want you to actually think about it, not just give me an answer. Right now, today: who is the newest Flight Attendant at your base? Not the newest one you've worked with — the newest one, period. Do you know their name? Do you know how they're adjusting? [Pause — give 15 seconds of real silence.] If you couldn't answer that — this is the most important module of the day for you. And if you could answer it — good. This module is about what happens next."
Phase 2 — Group Breakout — Build the Journey (15 min, groups of 3–4)
Groups of 3–4 — Online Tool
Before you open rooms: Drop the journey builder link in Teams chat. Say: "I'm dropping a link in the chat right now — open it on your device. Select your group number at the top. You'll build your map in that tool and submit it when you're done. I'll be watching the submissions come in."

Journey builder URL: https://breeze-aviation.github.io/leaderguide/Leading%20at%20Altitude/Inflight_FA_Journey_Builder.html

Instructions to give: "Before I show you anything — I want you to build it. Think back to what it's like to be a brand new FA. Or if you were never an FA, think about the newest one you've worked with. Your group is going to map out what that person actually goes through from the moment they start training to their first 90 days at base. Not the operational checklist — what are they experiencing? What are they feeling? What are they worried about? What are they proud of? And for each stage — when does their supervisor show up? The tool has three questions. The third one is the important one: what is the single most important moment where supervisor presence changes everything? Go."

While groups work: Monitor your Formspree inbox — submissions come in by group number. If a group hasn't submitted with 2 minutes left, do a quick time check: "Two minutes — wrap up and get that submitted."

Before you send them: seed Stage 1 specifically. Say: "Pay special attention to Initial Training — Weeks 1 through 3. That FA is not at your base yet. For the supervisor presence question at that stage — be honest: when do you actually show up during those three weeks? Write down what really happens." Don't answer it for them. Just make sure they're thinking about it before they start building.
Phase 3 — Group Presentations + Convergence (20 min, large group)
Flow: Call each group back one at a time — Groups 1 through 4. Give each group 3 minutes: what stages did you map, what were the gaps you found, and what did you name as the single most important moment? You're listening for where they converge and where they diverge — that's the material for the debrief.
After all groups present — the convergence question: "Across all four maps — where did every group land the same thing? What's the gap that showed up in every version?" Let the room answer. The answer is almost always: the supervisor shows up reactively. When something goes wrong, or when there's a formal milestone. The proactive presence — especially in that first window — is usually the gap.
Zoom in on Initial Training — it's the reveal within the reveal. "Look at every group's map right at the beginning — Weeks 1 through 3, Initial Training. That FA has been away from home for three weeks, learning a new job, wondering what their base is going to be like. They've probably already heard from their union rep. Have they heard from you?" Pause. Let that land before you move forward.
After the convergence moment, ask one question and go quiet: "In your map — when does the supervisor show up? Count how many stages say 'when there's a problem' or nothing at all. What does that tell you?" Give 15–20 seconds of silence. Let them sit with it.
Then add the layer they almost certainly left out: "Here's what I notice isn't on most of your maps: the inside track. What's happening emotionally at each stage. Because that's actually what drives whether a new FA stays or leaves — not the operational milestones, but whether they feel like they belong. So let me ask: in your map, where does the belonging question show up? Where does your newest FA ask herself — am I good at this? Do I fit here? Does anyone know I exist? [pause] That's usually the column that's empty."
Phase 4 — The 30/60/90 Framework (7 min, large group)
The Reveal and the Framework
"Here's what we know about FA retention. The first 90 days are the highest-risk window — and the defining factor almost always comes down to one thing: did anyone show up for them before they needed something? Not reactively. Proactively. The supervisor who called after their first solo flight — not because anything went wrong, but just to ask how it felt — that supervisor created a relationship. The supervisor who didn't, created a gap. And FAs who feel like they're in a gap eventually fill it by leaving. So here's the framework. Four touchpoints. The first one happens before they ever arrive at your base. This isn't a checklist — it's a habit."
Day 0 — The Pre-Arrival Welcome: "Hey — I know you're still in training. I'm [name], your base supervisor in [city]. I'm looking forward to having you on the team. Reach out if you have any questions before you get here." That's it. The FA arrives at base already knowing your name — which changes what every subsequent touchpoint feels like. This is the gap the AFA has been filling. It takes 60 seconds to close it.
After their first solo flights — the First-Flight Check-In: "Hey, I know you just finished your first trips — I wanted to check in before you tell me how it went. What surprised you? What are you proud of?" Not operational. Not 'any problems?' Just genuine curiosity.
Around day 45–60 — the First Hard Thing: "You're a couple months in now — what's been the toughest part so far?" By this point they've had a hard flight. They probably haven't told you. This question tells them they can.
Around day 75–90 — the Belonging Check: "Do you feel like part of the team yet? Not the job — the team." Most new FAs have never been asked this by a supervisor. The question itself is the answer.
Phase 5 — Individual Application + Teams Chat (3 min)
Workbook Individual → Teams Chat Commitment
Give 3 minutes for supervisors to fill in the application section of their workbook: name a specific new FA, identify which touchpoint they owe them, write exactly what they'll say. If the FA is still in training — their touchpoint is Day 0. Tell them: "If your newest FA is still in training right now, write this in your workbook: I'm sending them a message today. Then actually send it before this session ends."

Then Teams chat close: "Before we break — drop one thing in the chat. The name of your newest FA and what touchpoint you're doing this week. 10 words or less. If they're still in training — say that: 'My newest FA is in training. I'm sending them a message today.'" Read a few out loud before the break. These are now public commitments. That matters.
Watch For
Watch For
Supervisors who can't name their newest FA in Phase 1. Don't put them on the spot — but don't skip past it either. The discomfort is data about what they need from this module.
Journey maps that only show operational milestones with no emotional arc. This is the most common result — and exactly what you want to surface. Name it gently: "What would you add if you had to describe what they're feeling at each stage, not just what they're doing?"
The supervisor who says "I already do all three touchpoints." Ask: "Is that proactive or reactive? Did you reach out, or did they come to you?" That distinction is the whole module.
If a supervisor shares a story about losing a new FA — let it breathe. Those stories are the real curriculum.
The First Holiday Season — Why This Module Is Urgent Right Now
Name the timing directly: "Here's why this module is on the agenda today specifically. We're heading into the highest-risk retention window of the year — not just because it's busy, but because for your newest FAs, this is their first holiday season. More shifts. Less seniority. Working Thanksgiving, Christmas, New Year's away from family for the first time. The supervisor who reaches out before the chaos hits is the one that FA remembers. The supervisor who waits for a problem is the one that FA doesn't think about — until they're deciding whether to come back."
Early warning signals for new FAs at risk: They stop asking questions / messages become purely operational with no personal tone / they stop showing up early / their energy in the cabin changes — crew notices before you do / call-out frequency changes from their baseline pattern.
None of these alone is a crisis. All of them together are a signal worth acting on. The action is always the same: reach out — not to address the signal, just to check in.
Deeper Knowledge — Champion Others for New FAs
Why the first-flight check-in changes everything: Most new FAs don't know if their supervisor knows they exist until something goes wrong. A proactive call after their first solo flights rewires that assumption. It tells them: this person is paying attention, and I'm safe to reach out.
When supervisors say "I don't have time for 30/60/90 touchpoints with every new FA": They're right that they can't do this for all 40 FAs. They're wrong that they can't do it for the 2–3 newest ones. That's not a time problem — that's a prioritization problem.
The belonging check is the hardest one to do: It feels presumptuous. Name this directly: "If you're right that they don't feel like they belong, you just changed something. If you're wrong, the FA walks away knowing their supervisor noticed them. That's not a bad outcome either way."

When the Pressure's On
Slide 20–24  ·  Do What's Right
The 4-Step Triage
"You make decisions under pressure every day. Sick calls at 5am, crew disputes on the ramp, last-minute gate changes. You rarely have full information and you have seconds. This framework gives you a consistent process — four steps that slow the reaction just enough to make a better call."
Slido — Activity 3
Open Text Responses
"Describe a recent moment you had to make a call under pressure."
Read responses privately as they come in. Select 2–3 to discuss live — look for ones that are specific, relatable, and safe to share. Don't pick anything that could embarrass or identify someone. You can paraphrase: "Someone shared something like this..." before reading it.
Scenario Groups (Slide 18)
Groups of 4–5 — 10 Minutes
The scenario is in the participant workbook (Section 3). It involves an FA-passenger altercation 45 minutes before departure with multiple stakeholders watching.

Room setup: Collapse 7 pairs into 3 rooms (groups of 4–5). Put 2 pairs per room with one room taking a third pair if needed.

What to listen for in debrief: Did groups skip Assess and jump straight to Choose? Did they think about who to Communicate with, or just what to do? Surface these differences — they're the learning.
Watch For
Watch For
Groups landing in very different places on the scenario — that's good. If everyone agrees, push back: "What if the captain was pushing you to just get rid of the FA? Does your answer change?"
Supervisors who focus only on procedure (SOP, chain of command) without thinking about the human element. Gently redirect: "What does your FA need from you in that moment, not just the operation?"
The holiday resilience question sometimes opens something real. If a supervisor shares they're already running on empty — take it seriously. That person needs Module 6 (accountability partner) more than anyone in the room.
Deeper Knowledge — Do What's Right
Sort it: Safety / People / Operations / Everything Else. This is not about ranking importance — it's about routing. A safety observation and a crew scheduling question both need a response, but they don't need you in the same way. Naming the category tells you who else might need to be involved before you act.
Test the urgency: "What breaks in 30 minutes? Today? This week?" The most common trap is treating everything as 30-minute urgent. When leaders run this question honestly, they usually find that most things that feel urgent today are actually this-week problems. That distinction changes what gets priority in the next hour.
Check ownership: "Is this mine to solve — or am I absorbing someone else's urgency?" This is the step most leaders skip. If a crew member brings you a problem that scheduling should handle, taking it feels helpful in the moment. But it costs you time and signals that every problem should route through you. The question isn't "can I solve this?" It's "should I be the one who does?"
Decide: Act now / Schedule it / Route it / Release it. The goal of the triage is to get here quickly and confidently. "Act now" is for the things that only you can do and that break in 30 minutes. "Schedule it" is for important things with no immediate deadline. "Route it" means someone else owns it. "Release it" means it doesn't need a response at all — which is more common than most leaders realize.
The Apology Pattern
"Last question before we move on — and this one is just for you. What do you find yourself apologizing for most often? Not in general. Specifically. The thing that comes up again and again. And when you're apologizing for it — is it something you actually have the power to fix? Because there's a difference between apologizing because you made a mistake and apologizing because the system put you in an impossible position. One of those is ownership. The other is absorbing someone else's problem. And you should be able to tell the difference."
3 Min Individual — Private
No sharing required here. This is purely private reflection. Give them quiet time to write. You'll often see people nodding or pausing — the recognition lands quickly.

Optional large group close: "Can anyone share what category their apology falls into — not the specific situation, just: is it something you have the power to fix?" This usually opens a really honest conversation about what supervisors actually control vs. what they absorb.
If supervisors say they apologize for everything: That's worth naming. "When you apologize for everything, the apology stops meaning anything — to you and to Guests. An apology from someone who owns it lands differently than a reflexive 'I'm sorry' that's really just a nervous habit. Which one are you doing?" This is uncomfortable but necessary.
If someone identifies a recurring system problem: Take note. These are real operational gaps that training can't fix — but leadership can advocate for. If multiple people name the same situation, that's data worth surfacing. You can say: "That's worth bringing up. These sessions are partly how we identify the things that need to change at the system level, not just the individual level."
The empowerment question: Some supervisors know exactly what would eliminate their apology pattern and feel like no one has ever asked. "If you could change one thing that would make that apology go away — what would it be?" Don't promise it. But asking the question signals that their experience matters. That's worth more than most people realize.
Staying in the Game — Holiday Resilience (5 min)
"One more question — and this one is specifically about the season we're heading into. Because here's what happens to supervisors who absorb everything: by mid-November, the check-ins get shorter. The recognition hits disappear. The FA who's quietly struggling doesn't hear from you — not because you stopped caring, but because you don't have anything left to give. And they feel that. They just feel like they're on their own. So I want you to think about one specific thing: what's the signal that tells you you've hit your wall? Not 'I feel tired.' The actual behavioral change. What do you do differently when you're running on empty? And the second question: what's one thing you will protect during the holiday stretch — that you will not let the operation take from you?"
3 Min Individual — Private Workbook Reflection
Give 3 minutes of quiet. Supervisors write in their workbook: their specific running-on-empty signal, what they do when they hit it, and one thing they're protecting.

Optional share: "Can anyone share their signal — not the whole situation, just: what's the first thing that changes about how you show up when you're depleted?" This is usually a surprisingly honest conversation.
Why supervisor energy is a retention variable: When a supervisor is running on empty, the first things to go are the proactive behaviors — the check-ins, the recognition hits, the belonging checks. Those are the exact behaviors that keep FAs engaged. Supervisor depletion and FA attrition are connected.
If someone shares something heavy: Acknowledge it without fixing it. "That's real, and I appreciate you saying it out loud." Note: "This is exactly what your accountability partner in Module 6 is for."

The Seriously Nice Paradox (Slide 20)
"Seriously Nice is one of our leadership principles, and I think it's the one we most often misapply. We think being seriously nice means being gentle, keeping the peace, not rocking the boat. But think about the most caring leader you ever had. Did they avoid hard conversations with you? Or did they care enough to tell you the truth — in a way that felt like it came from someone who was in your corner? That's what Seriously Nice actually means. Avoiding a hard conversation isn't kindness. It's avoidance. And your FAs can feel the difference."
SBI Framework Notes
Walk through SBI slowly. Give an example of each part: "On Tuesday's flight to BNA [Situation]... I noticed you walked past the passenger in 14C three times without acknowledging them [Behavior]... The impact was that she filed a complaint and said she felt invisible [Impact]."
Emphasize: Behavior, not character. "You were rude" is a character statement. "You walked past her without acknowledging her" is a behavior statement. One starts a fight. One opens a conversation.
The coaching question: after SBI, pause. "What do you think?" Then stop talking. The silence is uncomfortable. Let them fill it. What they say next is almost always exactly what you needed to hear.

4 Minutes — Individual, Private

Large Group — SBI Scenario Walkthrough (Slide 24)
Large Group — 5 Minutes
Rather than breakout practice, run a scenario together as a full group. Read a scenario out loud — one specific FA behavior on a recent flight (you can use the workbook example or build one that fits your group's context). Walk through SBI live: "Let's build this together. What's the Situation? What's the specific Behavior — not the attitude, the behavior. What's the Impact — on the FA, the guest, the operation?"

Invite the group to refine it. Start with a vague version: "She had a bad attitude in the galley." Then ask: "Is that specific enough to deliver? What would you need to know to make it SBI?" Let 2–3 people add specificity. Build toward a version that's deliverable.

Close with: "The hardest part of SBI isn't the structure — it's naming the impact without softening it. Most of us instinctively pull our punch at the end. That's the habit we're breaking today."
Watch For
Watch For
Supervisors who write a vague conversation ("I need to talk to someone about their attitude"). Coach them: "Can you be more specific? What did they do? When? What was the effect?" The more specific the SBI, the better the practice.
Practice that stays polite and surface-level. Push pairs gently: "That was good. Now try it again — but this time really say the impact. Don't soften it." Softening the impact is the most common habit to break.
Anyone who seems emotionally activated by the written reflection. This is real for some people. If someone needs a moment, let them. Don't rush past it.
Deeper Knowledge — Communicate Openly
When supervisors can't name the behavior (stuck in character language): This is the most common SBI failure. "She has a bad attitude" is not a behavior — it's an interpretation. Help them get specific: "What did she do? What did you see or hear? If a camera was there, what would it show?" Observable behavior is the only thing you can address in a conversation without it becoming a debate about character.
Phrases for opening a hard conversation without it feeling like an ambush: "I want to talk to you about something I saw — and I want to hear your side too." / "This isn't about questioning who you are. I want to address a specific moment." / "I'm coming to you directly because I think you can handle this — and because I respect you." The opener sets the tone. A well-delivered opener often determines whether the conversation is heard or defended against.
When a FA gets defensive in a real conversation: Don't retreat and don't escalate. Acknowledge the emotion, then return to the behavior: "I hear you, and I'm not here to attack you. I'm here because [behavior] happened and we need to talk about it." Stay on the behavior. If they won't de-escalate, name what you're observing: "I can see you're frustrated right now. I want to finish this conversation when you're ready — but we will finish it."
"Communicate Openly" and the coaching question working together: SBI delivers the feedback. The coaching question opens the learning. Together they create a conversation, not a verdict. After you land the I in SBI — pause. Ask: "What do you think about what happened?" Then listen. What they say next is almost always what the conversation needed to be about all along.
The Other Hard Conversation — Retention (10 min)
After SBI Walkthrough
"SBI is for when you've seen something specific — a behavior you can name, an impact you can describe. That's the corrective conversation, and you just practiced it. But there's another hard conversation. And it's harder, honestly, because it doesn't follow a formula. It's the conversation you have when you notice that an FA who used to be engaged isn't anymore. Not a performance problem. Not a behavior you can describe with Situation-Behavior-Impact. Just a feeling — this person is pulling back, and if I don't do something, they're going to be gone. Most supervisors never have this conversation. Not because they don't care — but because it feels presumptuous. Here's the thing: if you're right, you just kept someone. And if you're wrong, the FA walks away knowing their supervisor noticed them. That's not a bad outcome either way. The conversation is shorter than you think. Three things."
Part 1 — Name what you noticed, without making it an accusation: "I've noticed you seem a little different lately — not in a bad way. I just wanted to check in."
Part 2 — Ask the real question: "Are you doing okay? Are you good in this role?"
Part 3 — Stop talking. Wait for the actual answer. No structure. No follow-up framework. Just presence and a direct question. The supervisor who can ask that — and who the FA believes actually wants to hear the answer — is the supervisor who finds out before the FA decides to leave.
3 Min Individual — Workbook Reflection (Private)
"Think of an FA at your base who seems different lately — not a performance issue, just a vibe change. Write their name in your workbook — it's private. Write what you've noticed. Write how you'd open that conversation. Write when you'll have it."

No sharing required. Give 3 minutes of quiet. The FA has a name now — that's what makes the commitment real.
When supervisors say "I don't have an FA like that right now": Push gently. "Think about the last FA who left that surprised you. If you'd had that conversation three months before they quit — what do you think they would have said?" Almost everyone has an answer.
The difference between this and SBI: SBI names a behavior. This asks a question. SBI is about accountability. This is about investment. FAs know the difference.

Deeper Knowledge — Flying Together
Why isolation is the real leadership risk for supervisors at remote bases: These supervisors don't have hallway conversations. They don't overhear how a peer handled a situation. They don't get informal modeling. That isolation means they're building leadership instincts in a vacuum — and usually defaulting to how they themselves were managed. The accountability partner structure breaks that loop.
If supervisors are skeptical about the accountability partner idea: Don't oversell it. "I'm not asking you to become best friends. I'm asking you to send one message in 30 days. That's it. If it's useful, you'll know. If it isn't — you're no worse off." Low commitment, low risk. High upside if it sticks.
"Flying Together" and all 5 principles: Module 6 is where all five principles converge. Being seriously nice is choosing to invest in a peer relationship. Championing others means not just supporting your FAs but supporting the supervisors around you. Doing what's right means showing up for the group even when it's easier to go it alone. Communicating openly means being real with your accountability partner when things are hard. Promoting ownership means taking responsibility for the health of this network — not waiting for someone to build it for you.
