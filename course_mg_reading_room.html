Foundation
The Manager Who Ran Great Meetings
She ran tight meetings. Agenda, decisions, action items — captured and sent within the hour. Her calendar was full, her output was consistent, and nobody ever complained to her face. She thought things were going well.
Then turnover started creeping up. Engagement scores dropped. A strong performer left without warning. The exit interview said the same thing she'd heard from others: "It felt like nothing was really being seen."
The signals had been there. Quieter tone in messages. One TM going from energized to flat over six weeks. Less initiative on projects. She just hadn't been reading them.
Great managers don't just run the meeting — they read the room. Signals appear before problems do. Catching them early is the difference between a check-in and a crisis.
Foundation
Behavior Before Words
Most teams communicate through behavior long before they communicate through words. By the time someone says "I'm struggling" or "I'm thinking about leaving," they've usually been signaling it for weeks.
Absenteeism. A normally chatty Team Member going quiet. Lower output quality on a high performer. Changed tone in Slack. These are data points — and leaders who catch them early respond to problems when they're still manageable.
Leaders who miss signals respond to crises. Leaders who catch signals early respond to problems. Same situation — very different timing, very different cost.
Foundation
Three Conversations
What people say in a group meeting, what they say in a 1:1, and what they say in the hallway afterward are three different conversations. Reading the room means understanding which one is the real one — and creating space for it.
The meeting conversation is what's safe to say in front of everyone. The 1:1 conversation is what's safe to say to you. The hallway conversation is what's actually happening. Your job as a manager is to close the gap between them.
If the hallway conversation is consistently louder than the meeting conversation, you have a safety problem — or a listening problem. Either way, it's yours to solve.
The Signals
Five Categories of Signals
Team behavior communicates in patterns. Learning to recognize which category a signal falls into helps you respond with the right intervention.
⚡
Energy Signals
Lower participation, flat tone, less humor than usual, visibly disconnected in meetings. Energy is often the first thing to shift when something is wrong.
📊
Output Signals
Slower completion, lower quality work, more errors than usual. When someone's output changes without a workload change, it's worth a conversation.
🤝
Relationship Signals
Visible tension between Team Members, reduced collaboration, short responses in shared channels. Relationship friction shows up fast and spreads slow.
💬
Communication Signals
Shorter responses, less proactive initiative, avoiding check-ins or going quiet when they were previously responsive. Communication patterns shift before people say anything.
📅
Attendance Signals
More PTO requests in clusters, increased call-outs, arriving late or leaving early when that wasn't the pattern before. Attendance behavior can reflect disengagement or burnout.
Foundation
Seeing vs. Checking
Seeing is passive. You notice things as they come to you. Checking is deliberate — you actively look for signals and create conditions for them to surface.
1
Start every 1:1 with "How are you actually doing?"
Pause long enough to mean it. The silence after the question is where the real answer often lives. Don't rescue it too quickly.
2
Notice changes, not just states
Someone who's always quiet is different from someone who suddenly went quiet. The signal isn't the baseline — it's the deviation from the baseline.
3
Trust your instincts
If a Team Member's energy feels different and you can't name why, trust that and check in. Your gut is often picking up on real data before your brain has processed it.
Checking is a skill, not a trait. It improves with deliberate practice — and it starts with one habit: look for changes, not just states.

Scenario: The Withdrawn High Performer
A Team Member who was one of your most engaged voices has become noticeably withdrawn over the past month — fewer comments in meetings, shorter messages, declining optional team events. What do you do first?
ABring it up in the next team meeting — ask everyone how they're doing and see if it surfaces.
BSchedule a 1:1 check-in with a curious, open question — no agenda except understanding what's changed.
CMonitor for another few weeks to see if it's a pattern before you say anything.
DStart a performance conversation — withdrawn behavior can signal disengagement from the role.
The Signals
Scenario: Visible Tension in the Room
In a team meeting, two Team Members have a sharp exchange. The tension is visible — others notice. It's not the first time. After the meeting, what's your move?
AAddress it in the next team meeting — bring the tension into the open so everyone can clear the air.
BCheck in privately with each person separately before any group intervention.
CLet them work it out — adults don't need a manager to mediate every disagreement.
DSend a team-wide message reminding everyone of professional expectations.
Practice
Scenario: Low Energy After a Big Project
Your team just finished a major push. The project wrapped well — but now energy is flat. People are completing work but not initiating anything. The mood has shifted. How do you respond?
AGet back to business — they did a good job and now there's more work to do.
BDeliberately acknowledge the effort, name the energy shift you're seeing, and create a moment to reset before moving on.
CPush the next project immediately — momentum is easier to maintain than to restart.
DGive the team a few days off if possible and don't address it explicitly.
Practice
Scenario: Output Quality Drop
A strong performer's output quality has dropped noticeably over the past two weeks — more errors, less attention to detail. Their workload hasn't changed. What's your first move?
AHave a performance conversation — declining quality needs to be addressed directly and formally.
BOpen a 1:1 with curiosity: "I've noticed a shift in your work lately — how are you doing?"
CGive them more direct feedback on each piece of work until quality improves.
DWait to see if it resolves itself — everyone has off weeks.
Practice
Scenario: "Everything's Fine"
Your team says everything is fine. Meetings run smoothly. No one is complaining. But the signals say something different — lower energy, less initiative, more "just enough" work. How do you handle the gap?
ATrust the words — if they say it's fine, take it at face value. Don't create problems that aren't there.
BName what you're observing without accusing: "I've noticed some things lately and want to check in — not looking to assign blame, just want to understand what's real."
CRun an anonymous survey to surface what people won't say directly.
DRaise your expectations and see if performance responds — sometimes people just need a push.
