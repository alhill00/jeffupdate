When You’re Wrong, Say It First

Owning your mistakes fast is one of the most powerful things you can do as a leader.

The leader who can’t be wrong

can’t be trusted.

There’s a quiet cost to not owning your mistakes. It’s not dramatic — no one calls it out. But the team notices. They lose a little trust in your judgment. They start filtering what they tell you. They stop bringing problems because they’ve seen how you respond when things go sideways.

Leaders who own their mistakes fast don’t lose credibility. They build it. The team sees someone with integrity — someone who cares more about getting it right than looking right.

The Tool

The 3-Part Own-It Formula
 

Step 1

Say it clearly. No passive voice.

Not “mistakes were made” or “we probably could have handled that differently.” Say it directly: “I was wrong.” Two words. The rest is detail. The directness is the point.
 

Step 2

Name the impact — not just the action.

“I was wrong about the timeline — and that put extra pressure on all of you at the worst possible time.” They already know the impact. Naming it tells them you see it too. That’s what makes it land as genuine.
 

Step 3

Say what changes. Not a promise — a plan.

“Here’s what I’m doing differently going forward.” Ownership without change is just theater. A specific, observable change is what converts regret into trust. Make it concrete enough that they’ll know when they see it.

Sound Familiar?

You made a call. It was wrong. Your team knew it before you did. You’ve been hoping the moment has passed — that enough time has gone by that bringing it up now would be unnecessary or even awkward.

It hasn’t passed. The silence since then has cost you more than the wrong call did.
 

Quick Pulse — 1 Question

How comfortable are you saying “I was wrong” clearly and directly in front of your team?

Your Challenge This Week

Think of one recent call that didn’t land the way you intended. This week, name it — clearly, without qualifiers. Use the 3-part formula. You don’t need to make it a big moment. A short, honest conversation is enough. The team won’t forget it.
