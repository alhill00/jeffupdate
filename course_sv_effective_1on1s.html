Foundation
The 1:1 That Wasn't
Dana had been leading her team for 3 months and was proud of her weekly 1:1s. She showed up, asked "how's it going?", got "fine", talked through project status for 25 minutes, and moved on.
Six months later her highest performer gave notice. "I never felt like you actually knew what I was going through," she said.
Dana had been running meetings, not 1:1s. The difference matters more than most new leaders realize.
A 1:1 isn't a status meeting with a nicer name. It's the primary relationship tool you have as a leader — and most new supervisors don't know the difference until someone walks out the door.
Foundation
Status Update vs. Human Conversation
Most 1:1s fail because they become status reports. Your team member talks about tasks. You nod. Nothing changes.
A real 1:1 has a different purpose: connection before content. If you don't know how your person is doing as a human being, you can't lead them effectively as a professional.
The question to ask yourself after every 1:1: Did I learn something I didn't already know? If the answer is no, it was a project check-in — not a 1:1.
Foundation
What 1:1s Are Actually For
Three things every 1:1 should create:
👤
The team member feels seen and heard as a person.
Not just as a task performer. This is the baseline.
💡
You learn something you didn't already know.
About their work, their challenges, or how they're experiencing the team.
🧭
They leave with more clarity or support than they came in with.
Something shifted. A question got answered. A burden got lighter.
If a 1:1 doesn't do at least one of those three things, it was a status meeting with a nicer name.
The 1:1 Framework
Three Questions Every 1:1 Should Touch
You don't need a rigid script. You need three anchors that ensure your 1:1s cover what matters.
🤝
Personal Connection
"How are you?"
Not a formality. Actually listen. Pause long enough that they know you mean it.
⚡
Professional Connection
"What's energizing you? What's draining you?"
Surfaces what matters to them at work right now — the real stuff, not the polished stuff.
🙋
Support
"What do you need from me right now?"
The question most leaders never ask. Let them set the agenda. Your job is to listen, not fill time.
Key principle: Let your team member set the agenda. Your job is to listen, not fill time.
Foundation
When They Say "Everything's Good"
Marcus, your direct report, says at the start of a 1:1: "Not much to cover this week — everything's good."
What do you do?
AAccept it and use the time to give your own updates — you have things to cover anyway.
BAsk "What's been the best part of your week? And what's been the hardest?" to open the real conversation.
CEnd the meeting early — if there's nothing to discuss, don't waste the time.
DRun through the project status list you prepared to make sure nothing falls through the cracks.
The 1:1 Framework
The Agenda Question That Changes Everything
The single most powerful 1:1 habit: ask your team member to set the agenda.
"What do you most want to talk about today?" Then be quiet.
Most leaders talk too much. The person you're there for should speak 70% of the time. If you're doing most of the talking, it's a staff briefing — not a 1:1.
The 70/30 rule: Your team member talks 70% of the time. You listen, ask questions, and respond 30%. If those numbers are reversed, you've turned it into your meeting.
The 1:1 Framework
When Something Real Surfaces
You're midway through a 1:1 and your team member mentions in passing that they've been frustrated with how a cross-functional team is treating them. Your instinct is to jump to solutions.
What's the better coaching move?
AImmediately suggest how to handle the situation — you've dealt with this team before.
BAsk "Tell me more about that — what's happening?" and let them unpack it before you problem-solve.
CRedirect to the original agenda — you can circle back to this next week.
DOffer to talk to the other team's leader yourself to sort it out.
Practice
When You've Been Canceling
You've been canceling 1:1s frequently because of operational demands. Your most reliable team member hasn't said anything, but their engagement seems to have dipped slightly.
The most important step is:
ASend a message acknowledging you've been hard to reach and asking if there's anything you can do.
BBook the next 1:1, protect it, show up, and open with: "I know I've been inconsistently available lately. How are you doing — really?"
CAdd more agenda items to the next 1:1 to make up for lost time.
DWait to see if the engagement dip resolves on its own — it might just be a tough week.
Practice
When They Vent About a Coworker
A team member uses a 1:1 to vent extensively about a coworker. You've heard this before.
What's the right response?
ATell them to work it out directly with the coworker — it's not appropriate to use 1:1 time for this.
BListen, then ask "What outcome are you hoping for with them?" to move from venting to action.
CPromise to speak to the coworker on their behalf this week.
DRemind them that venting is unproductive and redirect to their goals.
Practice
When 1:1s Keep Running Over
Your 1:1s are running over time consistently.
How do you handle this?
ACut off the conversation when time is up, regardless of where you are.
BLet them run long — ending a 1:1 abruptly sends the wrong message.
CStart 1:1s with "We have 30 minutes — what are the two things you most need to cover today?" to focus from the start.
DMove 1:1s to 45 minutes to give more room.

Course Complete
You've finished Running Effective 1:1s. The leaders whose teams stay and grow are the ones who treat 1:1 time as the most important meeting on the calendar — not the most skippable one.

What effective 1:1 leaders do differently:

Let your team member set the agenda, or at least have 70% of the input
Ask "What do you need from me?" in every single 1:1
Never cancel without rescheduling same week
Listen more than you talk — aim for 70/30
Treat 1:1s as relationship infrastructure, not status reporting
