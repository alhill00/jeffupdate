Foundation
You Are the Direction
The defining test of VP leadership is not the well-resourced initiative with a clear plan and full executive alignment. It is the situation where the path is genuinely unclear, the data is incomplete, and the organization is waiting to see how you respond.
At lower levels of leadership, uncertainty can be deferred upward — "let me check with my manager." At VP level, you are frequently the most senior person in the room. The organization cannot defer above you. You are the direction.
What uncertainty does to organizations when leadership doesn't name it: anxiety rises, speculation fills the void, collaboration decreases, performance degrades, and talented people begin exploring exits. The presence of uncertainty is not the problem — it's what leadership does with it that determines the organizational outcome.
The VP's job in uncertainty is not to eliminate it before communicating. It's to name it honestly, anchor to what doesn't change, build the best available partial plan, and set a timeline for the next update.
Foundation
What Uncertainty Does to Organizations
Organizational responses to unaddressed uncertainty are predictable enough to map. Understanding the progression helps a VP intervene earlier — before the damage compounds.

Unaddressed Uncertainty

  • Anxiety rises as Team Members fill the vacuum with speculation
  • Informal networks become the primary information channel
  • Collaboration decreases — people protect their turf under threat
  • Risk tolerance drops — decision-making becomes conservative or frozen
  • Talent vulnerability increases — top performers with options begin to consider them

Named and Structured Uncertainty

  • Anxiety is reduced — the vacuum is replaced with what's actually known
  • Leadership retains credibility as the information source
  • Team Members can focus energy on things they can control
  • Trust in VP increases — honesty in hard moments builds more than reassurance
Foundation
Making Decisions in Uncertainty
The VP trap: waiting for certainty before deciding. Certainty is often unavailable on the timeline the org needs a direction. The alternative is calibrated decision-making — a process that works with incomplete information rather than being blocked by it.
Calibrated decision-making: "Based on what we know, I'm confident enough to move in this direction. Here's my confidence level and why. Here's what would cause me to revisit this call. Here's when we'll know more." Then move.
This is fundamentally different from guessing. Calibrated decisions are explicit about confidence level, explicit about the conditions for revision, and explicit about the timeline. This transparency is what makes the decision trustworthy despite the uncertainty — not the pretense of certainty that nobody believes anyway.
The cost of waiting for certainty: While you wait, the vacuum fills. People make local decisions that may be contradictory. The cost of coordination failure grows. When you do communicate, you are correcting a narrative rather than building one.
The Uncertainty Framework
Four Moves for Navigating Uncertainty
In sequence — four moves that turn unstructured uncertainty into something an organization can work with.
1
Name It
"We are in genuine uncertainty. Here's what we know. Here's what we don't know yet." Naming the uncertainty directly is stabilizing — it replaces speculation with the VP's clear-eyed acknowledgment of reality.
2
Anchor to Constants
"Here's what doesn't change: our values, our commitment to each other, our mission." In uncertainty, people need to know what the ground is. Anchoring to constants provides the stable footing from which uncertainty can be navigated.
3
Build the Partial Plan
"Here's the direction we're moving in, even though we don't have every answer." Even a partial plan beats paralysis. Direction with open questions is more useful than perfect information that hasn't arrived yet.
4
Set a Clarity Timeline
"We'll know more by [specific date]. I'll update you then." A timeline commits to a future communication and reduces the anxiety created by indefinite uncertainty. Keep the timeline.
Foundation
The Constants That Don't Change
Anchoring to constants in uncertainty requires knowing in advance what those constants are. In many organizations, they are named but not felt — values listed on a wall that don't function as real anchors in difficult moments.
For constants to work as anchors in uncertainty, they must be:

What Makes Constants Real

  • Referenced consistently in steady-state, not just in crisis
  • Connected to specific behaviors, not just words
  • Demonstrated by VP behavior — not just declared
  • Tested and held in previous moments of pressure

Useful Anchors in Most Orgs

  • The team's relationship to each other
  • The mission or purpose of the function
  • Specific commitments the VP has made and kept
  • What the VP will continue to protect regardless of what changes
The test: If you say "our values don't change" in a difficult moment, and Team Members mentally recall a recent instance where those values were violated, the anchor doesn't hold. Constants must be real to be useful.
The Uncertainty Framework
Communicating About a Shift Still Being Finalized
A significant strategic shift is being discussed at the executive level. Nothing has been decided. However, informal communication has already reached your organization — Team Members know something is being considered, and anxiety is building. You don't yet have the full picture.
What do you do?
AWait until the executive decision is finalized — communicating about an unresolved situation will increase anxiety rather than reduce it.
BName the uncertainty directly: "I know there's been some informal communication about potential changes. Here's what I know, here's what I don't know yet, and here's when I expect more clarity." Then deliver on the timeline.
CReassure your team that you're staying on top of it and that you'll communicate when there's news — this manages expectations without adding to the anxiety.
DHave your director layer address the informal communication in their team conversations — they know their teams best and can calibrate the message appropriately.
The Uncertainty Framework
Team Losing Confidence in Direction
After three months of ambiguity on a major initiative — caused by external factors outside your control — you can see your team losing confidence. They're executing, but energy is declining. Some of your best directors are asking whether the initiative is still the right priority. You don't yet have the clarity they want.
What's your move?
AIncrease the energy around the initiative — communicate more enthusiasm and urgency to build momentum.
BName the ambiguity explicitly, acknowledge the impact honestly, anchor to what remains constant (the rationale, the team, the commitment), share your partial plan, and set a specific date for the next update.
CPause the initiative until external factors resolve — protecting the team's energy through a pause is better than continued uncertainty.
DHave 1:1 conversations with each director to understand their specific concerns before communicating anything broadly.
Practice
Significant Decision with Unavailable Data
A resource allocation decision with significant implications must be made. The data you'd ideally have won't be available for another six weeks. The decision, however, needs to be made now — waiting will create a downstream cascade that costs more than a decision made with incomplete data.
What's the right approach?
APush the timeline — the decision quality improvement from having better data is worth the downstream cost of waiting.
BMake a calibrated decision based on available information: state your confidence level, the rationale, what would cause you to revisit it, and when. Then move and monitor.
CDelegate the decision to your director layer — they're closer to the operational reality and can make a better-informed call.
DMake the decision but communicate it as provisional — "we'll know if this is right in six weeks" — so expectations are managed appropriately.
Practice
Holding Steady When the Board Wants Certainty
In a board session, a board member presses you for certainty on an outcome that is genuinely uncertain. "I need to know definitively — will this strategy deliver the target by Q4?" You don't know. You could project confidence you don't have. You could say something that would be reassuring but not accurate.
What do you say?
A"Based on current trajectory, we're targeting Q4 delivery." — frame it as a target rather than a certainty, which is technically accurate without undermining confidence.
B"I'm not able to give you that certainty, and I don't want to. Here's what I'm confident about: [specific elements]. Here's what remains uncertain and why. Here's the condition under which Q4 becomes achievable." Be honest about the structure of the uncertainty.
C"Yes — our plan is built for Q4 delivery." — project confidence because a VP who expresses uncertainty to the board undermines investor confidence in leadership.
D"Let me come back to you on that with a more precise analysis — I don't want to give you an unreliable number in the room."
Practice
When You Were Wrong About a Direction You Committed To
You publicly committed to a strategic direction six months ago. New information has made clear the direction needs to change. Your team has invested significantly in the original direction. You need to announce the change. The challenge: you need to maintain credibility on the new direction while acknowledging you were wrong about the previous one.
What's your approach?
AFrame the change as an "evolution" of the original direction — credibility is preserved better if continuity is emphasized.
BBe direct: "The direction I committed to was the right call with the information available. New information changes the calculus. Here's what changed, here's the new direction, and here's how I'm thinking about what we do with the work already done."
CLet the new direction emerge naturally as you move attention toward it — the best transitions are organic rather than announced as course corrections.
DTake accountability for the error directly but minimize the discussion — dwelling on what went wrong is less useful than momentum toward what's next.

Your uncertainty leadership commitments:

Name the uncertainty before the vacuum fills — say what you know and what you don't, and do it first
