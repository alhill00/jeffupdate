Foundation
What You Don't See
As a director, your decisions affect people you don't see, in conversations you're not in, about problems you don't know exist. The further you get from the front line, the wider the gap between your mental model of how things are going and how they're actually going.
Blind spots at scale aren't a character flaw — they're structural. The role itself creates them. Your managers filter what reaches you. Your calendar fills with meetings that keep you at altitude. The people closest to the work often don't speak freely to someone three levels up.
The directors who lead best at this level are the ones who know their blind spots exist, actively name them, and build systems to reduce them — not the ones who believe they've overcome them.
Self-awareness doesn't eliminate blind spots. It just means you go looking for them deliberately instead of finding out about them the hard way.
Foundation
Three Most Common Director Blind Spots
These aren't unique to any one person — they're structural features of operating at director level.
1

Translation Gap

What you say and what reaches the front line are often very different. By the time your message passes through two management layers, the emphasis shifts, the nuance drops, and sometimes the meaning inverts. You think you've communicated clearly. Your Team Members heard something else.

2

Filter Effect

Managers curate what reaches you. They share wins more than problems, escalate issues after they've tried to fix them, and soften concerns when presenting up. The picture you get is consistently more optimistic than reality — not because anyone is lying, but because people protect themselves and you from bad news.

3

Comfort Zone

You pay more attention to functions, topics, and people you're familiar and comfortable with. The team you built, the function you used to lead, the leader who reminds you of yourself — all get more of your attention. What's unfamiliar gets less, even if it's where the risk is.

Foundation
Why You Stop Getting Honest Feedback
Title creates distance. The higher you go, the less honest information reaches you — not because people are dishonest, but because they're risk-averse. Telling a director they're wrong carries a different calculation than telling a peer.
This isn't a new problem — it's documented at every level of leadership. But it intensifies at director level because the stakes of being the person who speaks up are now real. Your Team Members and their managers are watching how you respond to challenge, disagreement, and bad news. That response pattern teaches them what's safe.
If nobody ever challenges you in meetings — not your assumptions, not your recommendations, not your interpretation of data — that's not a sign your judgment is excellent. It's a sign your culture may be suppressing candor.
The quality of information you receive is a direct function of the safety people feel sharing it. Building that safety is part of your leadership job.
The Visibility Map
Four Blind Spot Categories
Use these four categories to audit where your visibility is strongest — and where the gaps are likely to live.

1Team Health

What's not being said. Who's struggling. How engaged people really are. Whether your managers are developing their teams or holding them back.

Ask yourself: When did I last hear something that surprised me about how my team is doing?

2Org Translation

How your decisions actually land two or three layers down. Whether your messages arrive as intended. What your direction looks like in practice at the front line.

Ask yourself: Do I know how my last big decision landed with front-line Team Members?

3External Landscape

What's changing in your market, industry, or competitive environment that you haven't been tracking. What peers at other organizations are doing differently.

Ask yourself: When did I last actively seek information from outside my function?

4Personal Impact

How your own style affects others' behavior. What patterns you have in meetings, under pressure, or when challenged. What people experience when they interact with you — especially under stress.

Ask yourself: Who tells me hard truths about how I show up?
Foundation
Three Ways to Reduce Blind Spots
You can't eliminate blind spots — but you can build deliberate practices that reduce them and keep them from compounding.
1

Skip-Level Conversations

Regular time with Team Members two levels down, without their manager present. Not to evaluate the manager — to hear what's actually happening. Keep it informal, curious, and pattern-focused. What's working? What's getting in the way? What do you wish leadership understood better?

2

Deliberate Opposite-Data Seeking

After any conclusion, ask: "What would have to be true for the opposite conclusion to be right?" Then actively look for that evidence. This is the only reliable way to counter confirmation bias — because you're intentionally looking for what contradicts you.

3

A Trusted Feedback Channel

At least one person who tells you hard truths — a peer, a mentor, a direct report you've built that kind of relationship with. This person has to feel genuinely safe being honest, and you have to have demonstrated over time that you receive honesty without punishing it.

The Visibility Map
When the Summary Wasn't the Story
You made a resourcing decision based on a manager's summary of how the team was doing. Three weeks later, during a skip-level conversation with a team member, you learn the team has been struggling significantly — three people quietly job-searching, morale low after a reorg. The manager's summary never mentioned any of this. What do you do?
AConfront the manager immediately about withholding information — accurate reporting is a core expectation.
BHave a direct conversation with the manager: share what you heard, ask for their perspective, address the gap between what was reported and what's happening — and use it to reset expectations for how they communicate team health upward.
CHandle the team health situation directly yourself — go to the team and address their concerns without involving the manager.
DNote it as a data point but don't act until you have more corroborating information — one conversation isn't enough to draw conclusions.
The Visibility Map
The Communication That Didn't Land
You announced a structural change six weeks ago — clearly explained, you thought. During a skip-level, a team member tells you that people on the front line still don't understand why the change happened, and some believe it was cost-cutting rather than the strategic rationale you shared. What's your read on this — and what do you do?
AThis is a manager communication failure — they should have reinforced your message with their teams. Follow up with managers to close the gap.
BThis is both a translation gap and a communication design issue — reinforce the why directly (in writing or in an all-hands), equip managers with talking points, and build in a mechanism to hear whether it's landing this time.
CDon't revisit the announcement — reopening the communication will make the change feel less settled. Let it fade and focus on execution.
DSurvey the team to get a more statistically valid read before responding — one conversation could reflect an outlier perspective.
Practice
Running a Skip-Level Without Undermining Your Manager
You want to start having quarterly skip-level conversations with Team Members two levels down. One of your managers pushes back: "I appreciate the intent, but it makes my team nervous when leadership talks to them directly — they worry it means something is wrong." How do you respond?
ABack off — if it creates discomfort for the manager or the team, the risk outweighs the benefit.
BName the intent clearly, involve the manager in the design, communicate the purpose to the team in advance — and be transparent that this is a standing practice, not an investigation.
CDo the skip-levels anyway but don't tell the manager in advance — the value requires honest, unsolicited responses.
DTell the manager this is non-negotiable — skip-levels are a standard director practice and they need to support it.
Practice
Your Communication Style Is Being Misread
Your most trusted peer pulls you aside after a leadership meeting: "I want to share something as a friend — a few of your managers have mentioned privately that when you ask probing questions in meetings, it reads as skepticism or displeasure, even when you're genuinely curious. People are starting to over-prepare or hedge before they present to you." What's your response?
AThank your peer and take it seriously — this is exactly the kind of information that reduces a personal impact blind spot. Address it directly with your managers.
BAppreciate the feedback but note that probing questions are appropriate leadership behavior — people should be prepared to present to their director.
CStop asking probing questions in meetings to avoid the perception — the information isn't worth the impact it's having.
DAsk your peer which specific managers said this so you can address it with them directly.
Practice
The Function You've Been Ignoring
You manage four functions. Three are in your professional wheelhouse — you led them before and know them well. The fourth is newer to your scope. During an annual review, your CPO mentions that the fourth function has an unusually high number of unresolved issues this quarter, and asks what you're seeing. You realize you haven't spent meaningful time there in months. What do you say — and what do you do?
AAcknowledge the gap honestly — you haven't been close enough to that function. Commit to a specific set of actions and a timeline to get better visibility, starting this week.
BAttribute the issues to the function's manager — they're responsible for the day-to-day, and these problems should have been escalated sooner.
CDescribe the issues as situational and commit to monitoring them more closely — you don't want to overstate your level of unfamiliarity with the function.
DAsk for time to gather more information before responding — you want to give an accurate picture, not a reactive one.

Course Complete
You've finished Blind Spots at Scale. The directors who lead best at this level aren't the ones who have no blind spots — they're the ones who go looking for them deliberately, before they cause damage.

Three commitments for reducing blind spots:

Schedule one skip-level per month — make it a standing practice, not a response to a problem
Identify your trusted feedback source — and make sure they know it's safe to be honest
When you get data, ask what's NOT in it — name the gap as part of your analysis
