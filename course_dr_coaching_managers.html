Foundation
The Leverage Point Most Directors Miss
A director's organizational impact is multiplied or limited by the quality of their management layer. A director who develops excellent managers has built something that outlasts their own tenure.
The most important coaching a director does isn't to their own team — it's to the people leading their teams.
The multiplier principle: Developing one manager well reaches every Team Member on that manager's team. Your real coaching leverage lives one level down from where most directors are focused.
Foundation
What Coaching Managers Actually Requires
Coaching a manager is fundamentally different from coaching a Team Member on a task problem. When you coach a manager, you're doing three things simultaneously:
1
Building Their Leadership Judgment
The judgment that determines how their team performs — the hardest thing to develop and the most important to your organization.
2
Developing Their Self-Awareness
What kind of leader are they? Where are their blind spots? Self-awareness is the foundation of growth at every leadership level.
3
Expanding Their Leadership Identity
Who do they see themselves becoming? The identity shift from task-doer to people-developer is a fundamental transformation — and you're the one positioned to accelerate it.
Foundation
The Most Common Director Coaching Failure
The most common director coaching failure is becoming a manager's extra layer of approval.
When a director is involved in decisions their manager should own, the manager stops developing judgment and starts checking with you for every call.

What This Looks Like

  • Manager escalates decisions they should own
  • Director is managing two levels down
  • Middle layer becomes a relay station
  • Manager stops growing

What Breaks

  • Manager's judgment never develops
  • Director's time is consumed below their level
  • Organization loses a leadership layer
  • Team members feel distance from real decision-making
The result: Manager stops growing, director is managing two levels down, and the middle layer becomes a relay station instead of a leadership layer.
The Manager Development Model
Three Coaching Focus Areas
Director-level coaching of managers operates across three distinct development dimensions. Know which one each coaching conversation is serving.
1
Judgment Development
Help managers make the hard call without checking with you. Not "what should I do?" but "what are you thinking and what's driving that?" Help them articulate their own reasoning, then test it.
2
Team Health
Are their teams actually healthy? Retention, engagement, psychological safety, bench depth. Directors often see lagging indicators before managers do — "what do you see in your team that you're not seeing?" is the coaching question.
3
Leadership Range
Can they lead in different modes — directing, coaching, influencing, inspiring — or only one gear? The development challenge is expanding their range to match what each situation requires.
Foundation
Calibrating Your Involvement
Two coaching failure modes pull in opposite directions. Too directive: give them the answer, they learn to wait for it. Too permissive: step back so far they flounder and lose confidence.

Too Directive

Give them the answer → they learn to wait for it. Creates dependency. Manager stops developing judgment.

Too Permissive

Step back so far they flounder → they lose confidence. Creates anxiety. Manager stops taking initiative.

Calibration point: Match your involvement to their readiness for each type of decision. High-stakes, low-readiness = more direct coaching with shared reasoning. Lower-stakes, higher-readiness = self-directed coaching with you as sounding board. The problem type, not the person's overall level, determines the approach.
The Manager Development Model
The Escalated Performance Issue
A manager on your team escalates a performance issue about one of their Team Members, asking you how to handle it. They've had 1:1s with the Team Member and documented concerns, but want to know what you'd do.
ATell them exactly what steps to take in what order.
BAsk what they've tried, what their read on the situation is, and what they're considering next — then add your perspective.
CHandle the situation directly with the Team Member.
DConnect them with HR and step back entirely.
The Manager Development Model
Strong Results, Hesitant Team
Your highest-performing manager has a strong, directive style. Their team hits all their numbers. But you've noticed Team Members in their team seem hesitant to offer ideas in meetings.
ALeave it — results are results.
BGive the manager team health data and open a coaching conversation about what they're observing.
CAnnounce to the management layer that psychological safety is a priority.
DMove one of the hesitant Team Members to a different team.
Practice
The Cross-Functional Blind Spot
A manager keeps making decisions that create downstream work for other teams — they're not thinking cross-functionally.
AStep in when you see it happening and make the calls yourself.
BIn a coaching conversation, help them build the habit of asking "who else does this decision affect?" before finalizing.
CAdd a review step where they check with you before cross-functional decisions.
DSend a note to the affected teams to come directly to you with issues.
Practice
The Manager Who Won't Give Hard Feedback
A newer manager is struggling to give real feedback — her Team Members like her, but nobody gets the hard truth. Her team's performance is fine but slipping slightly.
AHave a direct conversation about the performance trend and what it signals.
BCoach her by having her reflect on a specific recent instance where harder feedback was warranted — what did she say, what did she leave out, and why?
CSend a training resource on giving feedback.
DModel the feedback conversation for her by giving feedback to her Team Member yourself.
Practice
No Succession Depth
You're reviewing your management layer and realize one of your managers has essentially no succession depth. If they left tomorrow, their team would be in trouble. Your manager hasn't raised this.
ARaise it in their review as a development concern.
BOpen a proactive conversation: "I want to talk about the bench on your team. If you needed to step away for 6 weeks tomorrow, who would cover, and how confident are you?"
CBegin building that relationship with their high-potential Team Members yourself.
DCreate a formal succession plan document for them to fill out.

Course Complete
You've finished Coaching Your Managers. The directors who build great organizations aren't the ones with the best answers — they're the ones who develop the best question-askers.

Five commitments of directors who develop great managers:

The next time a manager asks "what should I do?" — ask them what they're thinking first.
Have one team health conversation (not results conversation) with every manager this quarter.
Identify one manager whose leadership range needs expanding — design one experience that stretches it.
Stop being an extra approval layer. Name one decision type you'll stop reviewing.
Coach the specific instance, not the abstract principle — every coaching conversation needs a real example.
