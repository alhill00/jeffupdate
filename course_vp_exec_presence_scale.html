Foundation
The Presence You Can't Be In the Room For
At VP level, presence operates at a scale where most of the people you influence have never been in a room with you. They know you through the decisions made in your name, the stories that circulate about how you responded to something, the behaviors that your leadership makes visible and rewarded.
This is a fundamentally different problem than the presence challenge at manager or director level, where presence is personal — built in 1:1s, team meetings, and corridor conversations. At VP scale, the people three levels down in your organization form an impression of you almost entirely through indirect signals.
The VP presence question isn't "How do I show up in this room?" It's "What is the experience of my leadership for people who have never met me — and am I designing that intentionally?"
Foundation
The Presence of Absence
What you don't do communicates as clearly as what you do. When a VP is absent from a significant moment — a major operational challenge, a team milestone, a difficult conversation that needed senior presence — the org reads meaning into it.

Absence That Signals Investment

  • Unexpected visit to a front-line team
  • Showing up to a manager's meeting without being invited
  • Being physically present during a difficult operational period
  • Joining a team debrief after a major effort

Absence That Signals Disengagement

  • Missing a significant team milestone
  • Never appearing at operational levels
  • Only visible in formal review settings
  • Not responding to Team Member challenges that escalated
Presence investment is a calendar decision first. What you put time toward is the presence signal your org reads most clearly.
Foundation
Three Shifts in VP Presence
Presence at VP level requires three fundamental shifts from how presence was built at manager or director level.

From → To

  • Informing your team → Building organizational narrative
  • Direct relationships → Transmitted through managers
  • Reactive to opportunities → Proactive and designed

What Silence Costs at Scale

  • Vacuums fill with worst-case interpretation
  • Managers improvise direction without context
  • Team Members conclude leadership doesn't know or doesn't care
  • Trust erodes without a single incident to point to
The cost of VP silence is not zero. In the absence of VP signal, organizations build their own narrative — usually more anxious and less accurate than reality.
The Scale Model
Four VP Presence Channels
At scale, VP presence operates through four channels — most of them indirect. Mastering all four is what makes a VP felt organization-wide.
Channel 1
Message Architecture
3–5 themes repeated consistently across every setting, every quarter. The org learns what you stand for through repetition, not announcements. If you can't name your 3 themes, neither can your Team Members.
Channel 2
Symbol Investment
What you visibly invest in — time, attention, personal presence, budget — communicates your real priorities more credibly than any stated priority. Your calendar is your presence map.
Channel 3
Manager Translation
Your managers are your presence in 90% of conversations. If they can't articulate your intent, priorities, and direction — you are absent in those conversations even when you think you've communicated clearly.
Channel 4
Narrative Control
The stories told about you at scale — how you responded to a failure, who you recognized and why, what you did during a hard moment — are your presence for people who weren't there. Are they transmitting what you want?
Foundation
VP Communication Architecture
Presence at scale requires a communication architecture — not just good communication. The difference is intentionality and system.

Architecture Elements

  • Consistent core message (3–5 themes)
  • Predictable cadence (monthly, quarterly)
  • Multiple channels (all-hands, written, 1:1, skip-levels)
  • Bidirectional loop (input comes back up)

Bidirectional Mechanisms

  • Skip-level conversations
  • Pulse surveys with VP read-out
  • Open Q&A sessions
  • Trusted interpreters (honest insiders)
The trusted interpreter: Every VP needs 2–3 people in the org who will tell them what people are actually saying — not what they think the VP wants to hear. Without this, presence becomes one-directional and loses contact with reality.
The Scale Model
Presence Being Misrepresented Two Levels Down
You discover through a skip-level conversation that your intent on a significant initiative is being communicated incorrectly by your director layer. Team Members two and three levels down believe you're pushing the initiative for political reasons, not the strategic rationale you've articulated. The misrepresentation is creating resistance.
What's your primary move?
ASend an all-hands communication that restates the rationale directly — go around the management layer to the whole org.
BInvest in your director layer's understanding of the real rationale first — equip them to translate correctly before adding another direct communication.
CHold a skip-level session to address the misrepresentation directly with the affected Team Members.
DIdentify which director is misrepresenting the message and correct them privately.
The Scale Model
When the Org's Understanding Doesn't Match Your Intent
You conduct skip-level conversations and discover that Team Members at multiple levels describe your top priority as "efficiency and cost control" — when your stated and genuine priority has been "Guest experience and service quality." They're citing your recent decisions as evidence. The misalignment is significant.
What does this tell you, and what's the right response?
AThe message has been distorted by the management layer — correct the communication at the director level so the right message gets through.
BThe org is reading your decisions more accurately than your stated priorities. Audit the decisions you've actually made and what they signal — then align your stated and revealed priorities, or change the decisions.
CIncrease the volume of communication about Guest experience — more repetition of the stated priority will correct the perception.
DAcknowledge the perception gap in an all-hands and reaffirm the Guest experience priority clearly.
Practice
Using Your Calendar as a Presence Signal
You've said publicly that Team Member development is a top priority for your organization. A review of your calendar from the last quarter shows: 0 hours on development conversations with Team Members below director level, 0 attendance at development programs, and 22 hours in budget review meetings. An opportunity arrives to attend a two-hour leadership development session your team is running.
What's the right VP move?
AAttend and participate visibly — your presence sends a signal that no memo can replicate. Protect this time explicitly.
BSend a note of support but don't attend — your time is best protected for strategic priorities and the team runs these well without you.
CAttend briefly to open with remarks, then leave — this balances presence signal with time efficiency.
DAsk your HRBP to represent you at the session and share your perspective on development priorities.
Practice
Communicating Complex Message Through the Management Layer
You need to communicate a nuanced message about an organizational priority shift that requires context to land correctly — if communicated clumsily, it will create anxiety. You're choosing between sending the message directly to all Team Members or routing through your director layer. What's the right architecture?
ASend directly to all Team Members — directness builds trust and ensures no distortion in the management layer.
BBrief directors first so they understand the full context and can answer questions — then send the direct communication knowing they can support it accurately.
CRoute only through directors — they know their teams best and can tailor the message appropriately.
DWait until the priority is fully finalized before communicating — partial communication creates more uncertainty than silence.
Practice
When to Go Direct vs. Through the Chain
A significant Guest-impacting operational issue has emerged. Your Team Members are experiencing it directly and are watching how the organization responds. You have a choice: address the situation through your director layer (who will cascade the response) or go direct to the affected Team Members with a VP-level message.
AAlways route through the chain — VP communication that bypasses directors undermines management authority and creates confusion about hierarchy.
BIn high-visibility moments where Team Members need to know leadership sees them and the situation, go direct — and brief directors simultaneously so they're not caught off guard.
CGo direct only if you know the director layer will take too long — otherwise respect the chain.
DSend the message through directors but ask them to attribute it to you by name so Team Members know VP leadership is engaged.
