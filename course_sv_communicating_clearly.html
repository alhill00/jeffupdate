Foundation
The Email That Said Everything — And Communicated Nothing
Sam sent a long email to the team explaining a policy change. It was thorough, detailed, well-intentioned. Fourteen follow-up questions came back. Some team members thought it was effective immediately; others thought it was starting next week. Two asked if they were in trouble.
Sam had said everything important — but buried it.
The information was all there. The communication wasn't. Those are two different things — and confusing them is one of the most common leadership mistakes.
Foundation
Two Ways to Build a Message
Most people write the way they think: here's the context, here's what happened, here's the reasoning, and therefore... the conclusion.
Leaders need to flip it: here's the conclusion, and here's why.
Chronological structure buries the headline. BLUF structure leads with it. Your team shouldn't have to read to the end of a paragraph to find out what's changing.
BLUF = Bottom Line Up Front. State your conclusion first. Support it after. Every time.
Foundation
The One-Read Standard
Great leader communication passes the one-read test: after reading once, the reader knows:
1. What's happening or changing
2. What it means for them
3. What, if anything, they need to do
If your message generates follow-up questions, it failed the one-read test — not because the reader wasn't paying attention, but because the message wasn't clear enough. That's on the sender.
The BLUF Method
Bottom Line Up Front — How It Works
Four elements. Lead with B every single time.
B
Bottom LineYour main point, first
"We're changing the process for submitting time off requests starting Monday."
L
Lead with ImpactWhat does this mean for them?
"You'll now submit through Workday instead of email."
U
Underlying ReasonWhy — in one sentence
"This gives HR a single system to track all requests."
F
Follow-up ActionWhat do they need to do?
"No action needed today — you'll get a Workday tutorial by Thursday."

Spot the BLUF Message
You need to tell your team that a project deadline has moved up by two weeks. Which message is BLUF?
A"As you know, we've been working hard on the Hendrix project for several months. After some discussions with leadership this week about the client timeline, there have been some updates to our deliverable schedule. I wanted to make sure you were aware of these changes as we head into the coming weeks. The deadline has moved from December 15th to December 1st."
B"The Hendrix project deadline has moved to December 1st — two weeks earlier than planned. Leadership confirmed this with the client yesterday. Please review your task lists and flag anything that might be at risk by EOD Friday."
C"Quick update on Hendrix — can we find time to discuss this week? There have been some changes."
D"Please see updated project timelines in the shared folder."
The BLUF Method
Emails That Don't Generate Follow-Up Questions
Three rules that separate clear leader communication from everything else:
1. Subject line = your conclusion, not your topic.
"Deadline Change: Hendrix Moves to Dec 1" — not "Project Update."
2. First sentence = the most important information.
Not "I wanted to share some updates about..." — state the update.
3. End with one clear ask, not multiple.
Most emails fail on rule 3. Five asks means three of them get ignored.
The subject line is a headline. If someone reads only the subject line, they should know the most important thing in the message.
The BLUF Method
Giving Your Leader a Project Update
Your leader asks you for a quick update on where a project stands. The most effective response is:
AWalk them through everything that's been done so far, then the risks, then the current status.
B"We're on track for the December 1st deadline. One risk to flag: the vendor hasn't confirmed materials yet, which could affect week 3. I'm following up today."
C"Things are going pretty well. A few things to watch but mostly good."
DSend a detailed project status report to their inbox.

When You Have a Lot to Share
You have a lot of information to share with your team. Which approach creates the clearest communication?
AShare everything relevant — your team should have full context to make good decisions.
BWrite it all out, then go back and cut anything that doesn't directly help the reader take action.
CKeep it as short as possible — leaders should communicate in bullet points only.
DSeparate it into multiple shorter messages so it doesn't feel overwhelming.

Opening a Meeting on a Process Problem
You need to address a process problem with your team in a meeting.
How do you open?
A"I want to talk about some things I've been noticing with how we're handling X."
B"We have a gap in how we're processing shift change requests — it's causing delays. I want to solve it today, and I need your input on two specific things."
C"So there's been some confusion lately and I just want to make sure everyone's on the same page."
D"Before I share what I've been seeing, does anyone else want to share observations first?"

Course Complete
You've finished Communicating Clearly. Leaders who communicate well don't communicate more — they communicate with more intention. Bottom line first, every time.

What clear communicators do differently:

Lead with your conclusion — always. Context comes after.
Test every message: does the reader know what's happening, what it means, and what to do?
Subject lines are headlines — make them say something
End every message with one clear ask, not five
Follow-up questions are feedback — if you get them, rewrite the message, don't resend it
