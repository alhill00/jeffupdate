Foundation
Drowning in Data, Still Getting It Wrong
Every proposal Marcus brought to leadership was backed by numbers. Charts, trend lines, year-over-year comparisons. He prided himself on being data-driven. And yet quarter after quarter, the same problems surfaced. The same mistakes got made. The same conversations had to happen again.
It took a peer pulling him aside to name it: Marcus wasn't using data to make decisions. He was using data to defend decisions he'd already made. He would decide, then build the case. The numbers followed the conclusion — they never led to it.
Real data literacy looks different. It means letting data change your mind — not just validate it.
Data-driven decisions start before you look at the data. What question are you trying to answer? What would change your thinking? Those questions have to come first — or the data just confirms what you already believe.
Foundation
Three Data Traps at Director Level
The higher you go, the more data you have access to — and the easier it is to fall into these traps.
1

Confirmation Bias

Looking for data that supports what you already believe. The data you find first, weight most, and cite loudest is often the data that agrees with you. The rest gets filtered out.

2

Measurement Fixation

Optimizing for what's measured, not what matters. If your team's goal is "increase survey scores," they'll find ways to move the score — which isn't the same as improving the thing the score is supposed to measure.

3

Precision Illusion

Treating a 3-decimal-point number as more true than it is. A metric that's 73.4% feels more real than "about three-quarters" — but the precision is often false, and it creates false confidence in the underlying analysis.

Foundation
Data Doesn't Decide. You Do.
Directors who say "I always follow the data" are wrong — and they're hiding behind it. Data never makes a decision. Humans do. Data informs judgment; judgment makes the call.
This matters because when a decision goes wrong, "the data said so" is not a sufficient answer. You still made a call about which data to look at, how to interpret it, what it meant, and what to do. That's judgment — whether you acknowledge it or not.
The most dangerous data user is the one who believes they're objective. Awareness of your biases doesn't eliminate them — but pretending you don't have them makes them invisible and unchecked.
The goal isn't to eliminate judgment. It's to use data to inform better judgment — to surface what you might be missing, challenge what you assume, and slow down the decision just enough to make a better one.
The Decision Framework
Five Steps to a Data-Informed Decision
Use this sequence before and during any significant decision. The order matters — especially step one.
1

Define the Question First

Before pulling any data, write down the specific question you're trying to answer. Not "what does the data say" — what decision are you making, and what would help you make it better?

2

Identify What Would Change Your Mind

Ask yourself: what data, if you found it, would lead you to a different conclusion? If you can't name it, you're not being data-driven — you're going through the motions.

3

Look at Leading AND Lagging Indicators

Lagging indicators (revenue, satisfaction scores) tell you what happened. Leading indicators (pipeline, early engagement signals) tell you what's coming. You need both.

4

Name What the Data Doesn't Tell You

Every dataset has gaps. What's missing? What can't be measured? What do the numbers not capture? Naming these gaps explicitly is part of rigorous data analysis.

5

Make the Call — Data + Judgment

Use available data and your explicit judgment about what the data can't capture. Say out loud what the data told you and what you're adding from experience or context.

Foundation
When Data Is Incomplete (Which Is Usually)
Perfect data doesn't exist. At director level, you're regularly making significant decisions with incomplete, ambiguous, or contradictory information. The skill isn't waiting for certainty — it's deciding well anyway.
1

Be Explicit About Confidence Level

"Strong data on X, weaker on Y, uncertain about Z." Name it. When everyone in the room knows what you're confident in versus where you're working with limited data, the conversation is more honest and the decision is better.

2

Decide Which Unknowns Would Actually Change the Call

Not every gap matters equally. Identify which unknowns, if you resolved them, would actually change your recommendation. Pursue only those. The rest are noise.

3

Set a Decision Timeline and a Review Trigger

"We'll decide by Friday with what we have. If X changes, we revisit." This creates accountability without endless gathering — and gives you a structured way to incorporate new information later.

The Decision Framework
When Data Contradicts Your Instinct
You've been preparing to recommend expanding a program based on strong anecdotal feedback from your team. The data comes back and shows flat engagement — no meaningful improvement from baseline. Your instinct says the program is working. The data says something else. What do you do?
AMove forward with the recommendation — the qualitative feedback from your team is more meaningful than a single engagement metric.
BPause and investigate the gap — what's the engagement metric actually measuring, and is it the right indicator for this program's goal?
CPull the recommendation entirely — if the data doesn't support it, it's not data-driven enough to present.
DPresent both — share the qualitative feedback and note the data doesn't yet show impact, and let leadership decide.
The Decision Framework
Presenting a Recommendation at Moderate Confidence
You're presenting a resource request to your CPO. You have solid data on the cost side and strong directional data on expected return — but you can't prove ROI with certainty. Your CPO is analytical. What's the right approach?
AWait until you have stronger data — presenting with moderate confidence will undermine your credibility.
BPresent with a clear confidence breakdown: what you know with high confidence, what's directional, and what's a judgment call — and name it explicitly.
CRound up your projections slightly to make the case stronger — conservative estimates lose in competitive resource environments.
DLead with the qualitative case and only mention data if asked — your CPO trusts your judgment.
Practice
When to Stop Gathering and Decide
Your team has been analyzing options for a process change for six weeks. Every time you're close to a recommendation, someone surfaces a new variable to investigate. The analysis is getting more refined, but no better for the decision. What's your move?
AKeep gathering — incomplete analysis on a significant decision is a leadership risk. Better to take more time and get it right.
BName the pattern in the next meeting: "We've been adding variables without asking whether they'd change the recommendation. Let's identify the two remaining unknowns that actually matter and set a decision date."
CMake the call yourself without the team — they're too close to it to be objective at this point.
DEscalate to your CPO — this decision is too important for the team to resolve without executive input.
Practice
When a Team Member Uses Data to Avoid Deciding
One of your managers keeps bringing you analysis — well-built, detailed, thoughtful — but never a recommendation. Every presentation ends with "here are the options" and a look to you to choose. You realize they're using data gathering as a way to avoid making the call. What do you do?
AMake the call for them — they're giving you the analysis, and it's your job to decide at this level.
BName it directly: "I notice you've brought me analysis but not a recommendation. I want you to come back with a recommendation — your read, with your reasoning, and what you're uncertain about."
CGive them more time — they'll get comfortable making recommendations as they build confidence over time.
DReassign the decision to someone more decisive — this isn't the right person for high-stakes calls.
Practice
Interpreting Mixed Signals
Your engagement data shows high scores in "I feel valued" but low scores in "I understand how my work connects to our goals." Your retention data is flat — nothing alarming, nothing great. A manager tells you her team is energized. Another says his team is coasting. What's your read?
AThe high "I feel valued" score is the most important signal — when Team Members feel valued, retention and performance follow.
BThis is a direction issue, not an engagement issue — Team Members feel good but may not understand where they're headed, which is a leadership communication problem to address now before it erodes engagement.
CFlat retention is the decisive signal — if people are staying, the situation is acceptable.
DWait for next quarter's data — one mixed reading isn't enough to draw conclusions or act on.
