Foundation
What Executive Presence Actually Is
Executive presence isn't charisma. It isn't being the most extroverted person in the room, or the loudest, or the most polished. Those are surface features that people confuse with presence because they're visible and easy to observe.
Executive presence is the consistent ability to show up in high-stakes moments — a tense executive meeting, a hard question in front of 200 Team Members, a direct challenge from a peer in front of your CPO — in a way that commands trust and moves people to action. It's what you project under pressure, not in the easy moments.
Presence is a skill. It can be developed deliberately. And it matters more at director level than it has at any previous level — because the stakes of the rooms you're now in are higher, and the cost of going quiet or hedging when it counts is larger.
Competence gets you to director. Presence advances you beyond it — or limits how far your competence can take you.

The Presence Gap
The director who is technically excellent — sharp analysis, solid judgment, dependable execution — but goes quiet in executive rooms, hedges every recommendation with caveats, or loses composure when challenged, is hard to picture as a VP. Not because their competence is in question. Because their presence is.
The presence gap shows up in specific moments: when a CPO asks a hard question in a leadership meeting and you give a longer answer than necessary. When a peer dismisses your recommendation and you back down rather than hold your ground. When you're uncertain about an outcome and you project that uncertainty visibly rather than naming it confidently.
The presence gap isn't about being more extroverted or more polished. It's about choosing your response in high-stakes moments instead of reacting to them — and practicing that choice until it becomes the default.
The Presence Model
Three Dimensions with Behaviors
Presence is built through specific, observable behaviors. Here's what each dimension looks like in practice — and what the opposite looks like.

Gravitas

  • Calm when others are anxious
  • Makes a call when others equivocate
  • Direct without being aggressive
  • Knows when to hold and when to yield
  • Names uncertainty confidently

Communication

  • Leads with the headline
  • Doesn't fill silence with noise
  • Uses silence as a tool
  • Crisp, complete, stops
  • Answers what was asked

Credibility

  • Consistent between word and action
  • Visible track record
  • Handles being wrong with grace
  • Follows through on commitments
  • Doesn't oversell or undersell
Credibility is earned over time through consistency — but it can be undermined in a single moment by how you respond when you're wrong or caught flat-footed. How you handle those moments is presence.
Foundation
Three Behaviors That Move the Needle Most
You can't practice everything at once. These three behaviors have the highest presence impact per unit of deliberate effort.
1

Speak Less, Say More

Don't fill silence — create it. A director who gives a three-sentence answer to a complex question projects more confidence than one who gives a seven-sentence answer to prove they've thought about it. Say the most important thing. Stop. Let it land. The impulse to keep talking after a good point is an anxiety response, not a communication strategy.

2

Decisive Framing Under Uncertainty

"Based on what we know, I recommend X. Here's what would change my recommendation." This is the formula. It projects confidence (a recommendation, not a range of options) while naming the honest uncertainty (what would change it). It's not false certainty — it's confident judgment. The alternative is hedged vagueness that sounds indecisive even when the analysis behind it is strong.

3

Physical Consistency

How you enter a room. Your posture when others speak. Your non-verbal response when challenged. Leaning forward, nodding, making eye contact — or pulling back, looking away, shifting when questioned. The body communicates presence before the words do. Physical consistency under challenge — staying steady instead of contracting — is a trainable behavior.

The Presence Model
Challenged in an Executive Room
You're presenting a recommendation in an executive meeting. Mid-presentation, a peer director pushes back sharply: "I don't think the data supports this — this is more optimistic than our experience in similar situations." Several people are watching for your response. What do you do?
AImmediately walk back the recommendation — acknowledging the concern shows humility and avoids a public disagreement with a peer.
BAcknowledge the concern, hold your position with data, and separate the analytical disagreement from the personal dynamic: "That's a fair challenge — our situation differs from the reference cases in these specific ways. I'm confident in the analysis, and I'm open to looking at their specific data if it changes the picture."
CDefer to the CPO: "I think this is a judgment call — I'd welcome your read on where you land between our two views."
DAsk a series of questions to understand the peer's concern in detail — getting to understanding first before responding demonstrates executive maturity.
The Presence Model
Recovering from a Stumble
You're presenting to the leadership team and you realize mid-sentence that you've cited a data point incorrectly — a significant one. The error will be noticed. What's the high-presence response?
AFinish the presentation and address the error in a follow-up email — disrupting the flow calls more attention to the mistake.
BStop, acknowledge the error clearly and calmly — "Let me correct that — the actual figure is X" — continue from where you were. No extended apology, no excessive self-flagellation. Name it, fix it, move on.
CPause the presentation entirely: "I want to make sure I'm giving you accurate information — let me pull the correct data before we continue."
DSoften it without fully correcting it — "Actually that figure is approximate — the precise number is slightly different." Minimize without fully calling it out.
Practice
Making a Recommendation Without Full Certainty
Your CPO asks you directly in a leadership meeting: "What do you recommend here?" The data is strong but not conclusive. Two reasonable options exist. What's your response?
A"There are really two strong options here — Option A has these advantages and Option B has these advantages. I think both are defensible."
B"Based on what we know, I recommend Option A. The key assumption driving that is X — if that assumption changes, Option B becomes stronger. I'm confident in A with current information."
C"I'd want to gather a bit more data before committing to a recommendation — I don't want to give you a premature answer on something this significant."
D"What's your instinct? I'd like to calibrate against your thinking before I finalize my recommendation."
Practice
Staying Calm When a Peer Dismisses Your Proposal
In a cross-functional planning session, a peer from another function says, in front of the group: "I just don't think this is realistic — we tried something similar two years ago and it didn't work." Their tone is dismissive. You believe the comparison is flawed and your proposal is sound. What's your response?
AMatch their energy — challenge the comparison directly and make the case for why their 2-year-old reference point doesn't apply.
BStay calm and specific: "That's worth understanding — can you tell me more about what made that attempt fail? I want to know if we're solving the same problem, because this proposal is structured differently in these ways."
CAcknowledge the concern and defer the conversation: "Fair point — let me revisit the proposal with that context before we continue the discussion."
DLet it go for now and follow up with the peer privately after the meeting — public disagreements create more heat than light.
Practice
Projecting Confidence When Uncertain About the Outcome
A major initiative you're leading has an uncertain outcome — you're genuinely unsure whether it will succeed. Your team is watching you closely. A leadership team member asks how you're feeling about it. What do you say?
A"I feel great about it — we're going to make this work." (You project confidence even though you're genuinely uncertain, to protect team morale.)
B"Honestly, we're in uncertain territory — here's what I know, what I'm watching, and what I'm doing to position us for the best outcome. I'm not certain, but I'm clear on our next steps."
C"I'm worried — there are a lot of variables we can't control and I'm not sure we have everything we need to be successful."
D"I'd rather not speculate on outcome — let's focus on execution and see where we land."

You've finished Executive Presence. Presence isn't who you are — it's what you do in the hard moments. Practice the behaviors. Notice when you react instead of choose. Get better at recovering fast when you don't.

Three presence commitments for your next executive meeting:

Speak to be heard — not to fill space. When you've made your point, stop.
Practice decisive framing under uncertainty: "Based on what we know, I recommend X. Here's what would change it."
Notice how you show up when challenged — and choose your response instead of reacting to it
