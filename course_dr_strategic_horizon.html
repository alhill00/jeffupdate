The Clock You're Running On
Most directors are excellent operators. They run tight meetings, manage performance, and keep their team moving. But there's a clock problem — and it's quietly limiting how far you'll go.
You're running on a 2–4 week horizon when the role demands a 90-day view.
Start here: What problems are coming for your department in Q3?

If you don't have a clear, specific answer ready — that's the gap this course is designed to close.
The difference between a director and a VP isn't just scope. It's how far ahead they can see. Operational excellence gets you to director. Strategic horizon gets you to VP.
What you'll build in this course: A concrete habit and framework — The Three Clocks — that trains you to hold 30-day, 90-day, and 12-month thinking simultaneously, so you're anticipating problems instead of reacting to them.

What a Short Horizon Costs You
By the time a problem is visible to you, you're already reactive. Think about the situations that blindsided you in the last quarter:
1

The hiring gap

You needed to flag it 6 weeks ago. By the time it was obvious, you were scrambling — and behind on every other priority while you filled it.

2

The team morale dip that became a resignation

The flying analogy: A pilot who can only see 200 feet ahead is dangerous — not because they lack skill, but because they're operating too close to the problem to change course in time. A director who only sees 2 weeks ahead is flying the same way.

Reactive leaders get managed. Anticipatory leaders get trusted.

The Horizon Shift
Here's what most directors get wrong about this: they assume that seeing further ahead is a talent — something some people have and others don't. It's not.
The horizon shift isn't a personality change. It's a habit change.
You almost certainly have most of the information you need to see further ahead. You're just not in the habit of looking. The 30-day view is loud — it demands your attention, fills your calendar, and rewards you immediately for solving it. The 90-day view is quiet. It doesn't announce itself. It requires you to actively go looking.
This course introduces The Three Clocks — a simple framework for holding three time horizons simultaneously, without adding complexity to your week.
Your goal by the end of this course: Name one Q3 implication that's hiding in your Q2 data right now. If you can do that, you've made the shift.
The Three Clocks · Screen 1 of 4
The Three Clocks
Strategic directors don't pick one time horizon and stay there. They hold three simultaneously. Here's how to think about each clock:
🕐

Clock 1: 30 Days — Your Team's Reality

What's the work? Who's struggling? What's on fire? This is where most directors already live — and where you should spend roughly half your attention. It's the execution layer.

🕒

Clock 2: 90 Days — Your Department's Trajectory

Where are you trending? What decisions made today shape what's possible 90 days from now? This is where directors need to spend more time — and where most don't. It's the anticipation layer.

🕛

Clock 3: 12 Months — The Company's Direction

Where is Breeze going? How does your department need to evolve to match it? VP-ready directors are fluent here — not just in their own function, but in the broader business direction. It's the positioning layer.

The key insight: A director's job is to hold all three clocks simultaneously — not just the one that's loudest. The 30-day clock will always be the loudest. The skill is making sure you still hear the others.
The Three Clocks · Screen 2 of 4
Reading Leading vs. Lagging Indicators
Here's why most directors end up reactive: they're managing from lagging data — because it's cleaner, easier to measure, and directly tied to the metrics they're accountable for.
Lagging indicators tell you what already happened. Leading indicators tell you what's coming.

Pattern 1: Engagement

  • Leading: Team Member goes quiet in meetings, shorter responses, less initiative
  • Lagging: Resignation conversation

Pattern 2: Output Quality

  • Leading: Slow, consistent decline in quality scores over 4–6 weeks
  • Lagging: Missed metric, guest complaint, escalation

Pattern 3: Team Capacity

  • Leading: Repeat absences, overtime creep, skipped development conversations
  • Lagging: Burnout, attrition, performance gap
Strategic directors learn to read leading signals — not because lagging data is useless, but because by the time a problem shows up in lagging data, your window for easy intervention has already closed.
Practice question: In your department right now — what's a number that's still "acceptable" but has been trending in the wrong direction for 3+ weeks? That trend is a leading indicator. Name it before it names itself.
The Three Clocks · Screen 3 of 4
The Weekly Horizon Check
You don't need a new system. You need three questions — run weekly, consistently. Here's the habit:
Q1

What did I learn this week that I'm not thinking about yet?

Every week surfaces information you absorbed and filed away. This question pulls it forward — conversations, data points, reactions from your team. What registered but didn't get examined?

Q2

What trend am I watching that I haven't named out loud?

You're probably tracking something — a pattern forming, a team dynamic shifting, a metric slowly moving. This question forces you to put a word to it instead of letting it remain a vague feeling.

Q3

What decision is coming in 60–90 days that I should be preparing for now?

Decisions made under pressure are almost always worse than decisions made with time. This question finds the upcoming decisions — staffing, structure, planning, positioning — while you still have runway.

Run these every Friday before your week closes — or before your weekly leader 1:1. Block 15 minutes. This is not a calendar exercise. It's a mental discipline: the habit of zooming out before the next week pulls you back in.

What Your VP Is Watching
Your VP is already operating on a 12-month horizon. They're thinking about where the business needs to be, what capabilities the org needs to build, and which leaders are ready to help get there.
When you show up with only a 30-day view, you're not speaking their language — and they have to translate for you. It's not a trust issue. It's a language issue. And it quietly signals that you're managing your current role, not preparing for the next one.
The shift that changes the conversation:

When you arrive at a leadership meeting with a 90-day perspective that connects to the 12-month direction, you become someone your VP relies on — not just someone they manage. They don't have to bring you up to speed. You're already there.
This is the presence shift that happens before you even open your mouth. Walking in with horizon thinking signals readiness. And in leadership, readiness gets recognized before it gets announced.

Practice · Scenario 1 of 3
Scenario: The VP Question
The situation: Your VP pulls you aside after a leadership meeting and asks: "Where do you see your department in Q3? I want your read before our planning session."
You have strong opinions about next month. You haven't thought much about Q3. How do you respond?
A "I haven't had a chance to look that far ahead — can I get back to you after I review the data?"
B "Things are going well right now — I think we'll be in good shape."
C "Based on the trend I'm seeing, I think our biggest Q3 challenge is going to be [X]. I'm still working out how we get ahead of it, but I want to think it through before the planning session."
D "I'll put together a full Q3 plan this week and send it over."
C is right — and here's why it works even when you don't have the full answer.

Your VP isn't asking for a finished plan. They're testing whether you're already thinking ahead. Option C shows you're looking at the horizon AND can name what you see — even if you're still working on it. That's the signal they're looking for.

Option A is honest, but passive — it reveals the gap without showing the instinct. Option B is vague and says nothing. Option D over-promises when a conversation was what was needed.
Practice · Scenario 2 of 3
Scenario: The Quiet Signal
The situation: Two of your highest performers have been less engaged over the past few weeks. Still hitting their numbers. Nothing dramatic. But something feels different — quieter, less initiative, shorter responses in 1:1s. Your quarterly review isn't for 6 weeks.
What's the most strategically aware response?
A Wait for the quarterly — if numbers stay good, it's probably fine.
B Check in separately in your next 1:1 with each of them — pay attention to what they say and what they don't.
C Alert HR that you may have a retention risk forming.
D Assign them both a stretch project to re-engage them.
B — and this is exactly what reading a leading indicator looks like in practice.

A quiet disengagement is a signal that typically appears 6–8 weeks before a resignation conversation. The right move is a light-touch conversation — not a formal intervention, and definitely not ignoring it.

Option A treats the symptom as noise. Option C escalates too fast on incomplete information. Option D adds pressure without understanding the root. Option B reads the clock before it runs out — and keeps the door open without making it a big thing.
Practice · Scenario 3 of 3
Scenario: The Initiative You Didn't See Coming
The situation: In an all-hands, the CPO announces a company-wide initiative that will significantly affect your department starting in Q3. You've heard nothing about it before today. Your managers look to you for a reaction. You're processing it in real time — right alongside them.
What's the most effective response in this moment?
A Tell your team, "This is the first I'm hearing about it — I'll find out more and get back to you."
B React visibly — let your team see you're surprised so they know it's not their fault they weren't prepared.
C Stay composed, acknowledge the announcement, and tell your team you'll meet this week to talk through what it means for your work.
D Pull your managers aside immediately to vent your frustration about not being looped in.
C — composure and a clear next step is exactly the right move.

You can't always see what's coming. But you can control how you respond when it arrives. Your team is reading your reaction to calibrate their own — so steady, action-oriented leadership matters here.

The bigger question after this moment: what signals should you have been reading that could have helped you anticipate this? Your VP likely knew something was coming. Were you asking the right questions in your 1:1s? Were you looking at the 12-month clock? That's where this scenario teaches.

Pulse Check
A director is scanning their team's weekly output and notices a slow but consistent 8% decline in quality scores over the past 6 weeks. Numbers are still above threshold. What's the most strategically aware response?
A) Nothing — above threshold is above threshold.
B) Address it now as a leading indicator of a problem forming.
C) Wait until it crosses the threshold to avoid premature action.
D) Ask HR to run an engagement survey.
B is right. An 8% trend over 6 weeks is a leading indicator — and it's pointing somewhere. By the time it crosses your threshold, you're already behind. Strategic horizon thinking means you act on the trend, not the threshold. You investigate now, while you still have options.
Pulse Check · 2 of 5
Pulse Check
Which of the following best describes the difference between a lagging and a leading indicator?
A) Lagging indicators are less accurate than leading indicators.
B) Leading indicators are only useful in large organizations.
C) Lagging indicators confirm what happened; leading indicators signal what's coming.
D) They measure the same thing from different departments.
C is the clean definition. Lagging data is retrospective — it tells you the score after the game. Leading data is prospective — it tells you the trajectory while the game is still being played. Both matter, but only one gives you time to act.
Pulse Check · 3 of 5
Pulse Check
Your VP asks all directors to come to next month's planning session with a "90-day view of their department." What should you bring?
A) A status update on current projects.
B) A summary of what your team accomplished last quarter.
C) Your read on where your department is trending, what risks are forming, and what decisions you need to make in the next 90 days.
D) A headcount request and budget projection.
C is what a 90-day view actually means. A status update (A) is 30-day thinking dressed up for a planning session. Accomplishments (B) are looking backward. A headcount request (D) is a resource ask, not a perspective. A 90-day view is forward-looking — trajectory, risk, and the decisions you're navigating toward.
Pulse Check · 4 of 5
Pulse Check
Which habit is most likely to help a director build a consistent strategic horizon?
A) Reading industry news every morning.
B) Scheduling a Friday reflection using 3 forward-looking questions.
C) Adding a monthly strategy review to the team calendar.
D) Asking your VP what to look out for each week.
B builds the muscle most effectively. The weekly horizon check is frequent enough to become a discipline and specific enough to produce insight. Industry news (A) is ambient — it rarely translates to your department's specifics. Monthly is too infrequent (C) to build the habit. Asking your VP (D) is passive — and it's your job to see ahead, not theirs.

Pulse Check
A director has strong 30-day clarity but rarely thinks beyond the current month. What is the most likely leadership impact of this pattern over time?
A) Their team will outperform departments with longer-horizon leaders.
B) They'll be seen as operationally solid but not VP-ready.
C) Their VP will start giving them more strategic responsibility.
D) The gap will close naturally as they gain more experience.
B is the honest answer — and the most important one in this course. Operational excellence is a floor, not a ceiling. A director who can't hold a 90-day view will be trusted to run things well. But they won't be trusted to shape what comes next. Experience alone doesn't close the gap — only deliberate practice of the horizon skill does.
