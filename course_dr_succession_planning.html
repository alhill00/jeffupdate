Foundation
Why Most Succession Plans Fail
Succession planning is consistently cited as a top leadership gap. Most organizations do it poorly — a spreadsheet submitted once a year with names in boxes.
The directors who do it well have built something different: a culture of deliberate development that makes their organization resilient, reduces key-person risk, and demonstrates to their own leadership that they're thinking beyond their current role.
The difference: A list of names is not a succession plan. Readiness assessments, gap closure plans, and deliberate development investment — that's a succession plan.
Foundation
What Succession Actually Requires
Real succession planning requires more than identifying who could fill each seat. It demands three additional dimensions:

Readiness Assessment

  • What readiness does each person actually have for a larger role?
  • What's the gap between now and ready?
  • What's the timeline to close it?

Risk Assessment

  • What's the exposure if that person left tomorrow?
  • Which roles have no coverage at all?
  • Which have single-point risk?
Most succession conversations at director level are about names. The actual work is about readiness, gap closure, and risk.
Foundation
The Director Succession Blind Spot
Most directors do succession for their own direct reports — but not for their managers' teams (two levels down).
The result: they know who would replace their managers, but have no visibility into whether their managers' teams have depth.
The blind spot: When a key Team Member leaves two levels down, the director is often surprised. That's a succession failure. Directors who coach their managers to answer the succession questions have built the bench. Directors who only answer them for their own layer haven't.
The Bench Framework
Four-Quadrant Bench Assessment
Map every person on your management layer using performance and potential. Each quadrant calls for a different action.
High Performance + High Potential
Accelerate
Stretch assignments, executive visibility, targeted development. Move fast — these are flight risks if not invested in.
High Performance + Plateau
Sustain
Retain, recognize, leverage expertise. Don't ignore because they're stable — these are your most reliable people.
Lower Performance + High Potential
Develop
Coaching, direct feedback, clear expectations, time-boxed progress. Don't give up — but don't give unlimited runway either.
Lower Performance + Lower Potential
Manage
Clarify expectations, support to improve, transition plan if no change. Honest assessment required.
Key Person Risk: Separately identify who is irreplaceable today — regardless of quadrant — and build a plan for each.
Foundation
Two Questions Every Manager Must Answer
Every manager in your layer should be able to answer two questions without hesitation. If they can't, you have a succession gap — and a coaching opportunity.

Emergency Coverage

  • "If you were hit by a bus tomorrow, who would step in for the first 30 days?"
  • Tests immediate coverage and team resilience
  • No hesitation should be required

Bench Depth

  • "Who on your team is ready to take on a larger role in 12-18 months?"
  • Tests development investment and awareness
  • Should come with a name and a plan
Director's job: Coach your managers to have real answers to both questions — not to have the answers on their behalf.
The Bench Framework
Names Without Readiness
You're conducting a succession review with your management team. Everyone has provided names for each of their roles. The names are there — but when you dig in, nobody has a readiness assessment or development plan.
AAccept the names — at least you have coverage identified.
BReturn the reviews with a template that requires readiness level, development gap, and one concrete action for each name.
CCreate the readiness assessments yourself based on what you know.
DSchedule individual meetings to do the readiness work verbally.
The Bench Framework
"No One Is Ready"
Your best manager has no identified successor, and both of you know it. The manager keeps saying she doesn't have anyone ready.
AAccept it — you can't develop someone into readiness on a timeline.
BOpen a coaching conversation: "Let's talk about who's closest to readiness and what 12 months of deliberate development could accomplish."
CBegin hiring an external candidate as a backup.
DFlag it to HR and let the formal program handle it.
Practice
The Flight Risk Two Levels Down
A high-potential Team Member in your manager's team is becoming a flight risk — they feel overlooked, no visibility, no development. Your manager hasn't flagged this.
AHave a direct conversation with the Team Member to assure them they're valued.
BSurface what you're seeing with the manager — "I'm concerned about this Team Member. What's your read on their engagement, and what does development look like for them?"
CPut a stretch assignment together for the Team Member without involving the manager.
DWait and see — if they leave, that's retention data.
Practice
Single-Point Succession Risk
In your succession review, you realize that three of your most critical roles are each covered by the same single individual — one person is the succession plan for three seats.
AAccept it — having one strong internal candidate is better than none.
BFlag this as a key person risk and build a plan to diversify the succession coverage.
CPromote the person to reduce the backfill need.
DHire externally to create redundancy.
Practice
"That's HR's Job"
A director peer tells you succession planning is "HR's job." You disagree. How do you respond?
AAgree to avoid conflict — it's their department.
BShare your perspective: succession planning is a leadership responsibility because it requires knowledge of readiness, development plans, and org context that HR doesn't have access to.
CEscalate the disagreement to your shared leader.
DSay nothing — their bench is their problem.

Course Complete
You've finished Succession Planning. The directors who build lasting organizations treat succession as a leadership discipline, not an HR exercise.

Five commitments of directors who build great benches:

Have a succession conversation with every manager this quarter — not a form, a real conversation.
Name one key person risk and take one concrete action to reduce it this month.
Build readiness plans, not just names — every successor needs a gap and an action.
Know who would cover your own role if you were gone tomorrow — and invest in that person's development.
Coach your managers to own succession for their teams, not to defer it to you or HR.
