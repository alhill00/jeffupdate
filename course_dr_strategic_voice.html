Foundation · Screen 1 of 3
The Quiet Room Problem
There's a specific failure mode that holds talented directors back from VP conversations: they have the strategic read — they see the problem, understand the dynamics, know what should happen — but they don't say it.
Not because they're afraid. Because they're not sure it's their place. Because they're waiting for the right moment that never quite arrives. Because they've seen people speak up and get dismissed and they don't want that to happen to them.
The result: their perspective stays in their head. And the room makes a worse decision without it.
This course is about ending that pattern. Strategic voice is a learnable skill — and the OIR Framework gives you the structure to deliver your perspective clearly every time.
Foundation · Screen 2 of 3
Two Failure Modes
Directors who struggle with strategic voice usually land in one of two places:
1

Too Quiet

They listen, process, agree internally, and say nothing. Their VP doesn't know what they think. They get passed over for conversations they should be part of because nobody knows they have a view.

2

Too Much

They over-explain, add every caveat, front-load the history, and lose the room before they get to the point. Their content is good but the delivery buries it. Senior leaders tune out.

Both modes come from the same root: uncertainty about how to structure and deliver a strategic perspective. The OIR Framework solves both.

What Your VP Actually Wants From You
Your VP doesn't just want you to execute — they want your read on the situation. They're in rooms where they need director-level perspective to inform VP-level decisions. When you stay quiet, they're operating with less information than they should have. When you speak with clarity, you become someone they rely on.
There's a direct line between using your strategic voice well and being pulled into higher-stakes conversations.
This course gives you the framework to do it consistently.

The OIR Framework · Introduction
The OIR Framework
Three-part structure for delivering any strategic perspective. OIR keeps you from being too vague (just observation) or too prescriptive (jumping to recommendation without grounding).
O

Observation

What you're seeing. Specific, grounded in data or direct experience. Not opinion yet — just what's true.
"I've noticed that across my three teams, onboarding completion rates have dropped 18% since the new process launched."

I

Implication

What it means. Why it matters. The so-what that connects your observation to something the room cares about.
"If this continues, we'll see it in 90-day retention numbers by Q3."

R

Recommendation

What you think should happen. Clear and direct, with appropriate confidence calibration.
"I'd recommend we pause the new process for roles with less than 60-day onboarding and review the design before full rollout."

The OIR Framework · Confidence Calibration
Confidence Calibration
You don't have to be certain to use your strategic voice. The most credible directors calibrate their confidence explicitly — they don't project certainty they don't have, and they don't hedge everything into mush.

High Confidence

  • "I'm seeing this consistently across all three teams — I think we need to act now."

Medium Confidence

  • "I'm not certain, but based on what I'm seeing, my read is X. I'd want to validate with Y before we commit."

Low Confidence

  • "I might be missing something — but here's what I'm noticing and I think it's worth naming."
Calibrated language builds more credibility than false certainty or excessive hedging. The goal is honesty about what you know — not performing confidence you don't feel.
The OIR Framework · Timing
Reading the Room for When to Speak
Timing matters as much as content.
✓

Signs You Should Speak Up

The room is going in a direction that's missing something important. A decision is about to be made without your perspective in it. Your VP directly asks your view. You're the most informed person on a topic being discussed without enough context.

✗

Signs to Wait

Someone more senior is mid-thought on the same topic. The decision has already been made and the meeting has moved on. You need 5 minutes to formulate your OIR before you're ready to deliver it well.

Note: "I need more time to formulate this" is a valid reason to hold — but it should lead to you raising it before the meeting ends, not an offline email three days later.
The OIR Framework · Handling Pushback
Handling the Pushback
When your perspective gets challenged — or dismissed — how you respond shapes your credibility more than the original statement did.
Three responses to avoid:
1

Immediately Backing Down

Signals you didn't believe it yourself. If a single challenge makes you fold, the room learns your views aren't worth engaging with.

2

Over-Defending With Volume

Signals insecurity. Repeating yourself louder or with more intensity doesn't make your point stronger — it makes the room want to move on.

3

Going Silent for the Rest of the Meeting

Signals bruised ego. It removes you from the conversation entirely and tells the room your participation is conditional.

The right move: Acknowledge the pushback genuinely, hold your observation if you still believe it, and invite more information if you're uncertain. "That's a fair point — I'm still watching [X] because I'm not sure we've fully accounted for it" keeps you in the conversation without being combative.
Practice · Scenario 1 of 3
The Blind Spot in the Room
You're in a VP/Director leadership meeting. The group is aligned on a go-forward decision that you think has a significant blind spot — specifically, nobody has accounted for how it will land with frontline Team Members in your department. You have direct knowledge they don't. The decision is about to be made.
What do you do?
A Stay quiet — you're not sure it's your place to challenge a room that seems aligned.
B Wait until after the meeting and tell your VP privately.
C Use OIR: "I want to name something before we close on this. I'm seeing [observation] in my teams, which makes me think [implication]. Can we talk through [recommendation] before we finalize?"
D Say "I have some concerns" without specifics, to flag that you're not fully on board.
The right move is C. A vague "I have concerns" (D) signals disagreement without contributing anything useful — it's the worst of both worlds. Option B means the decision gets made without your input. Option C uses OIR to make your perspective concrete, grounded, and actionable. That's how you use strategic voice in a live room.
Practice · Scenario 2 of 3
"What Do You Think?"
Your CPO turns to you mid-meeting and says, "You're closest to this — what's your read?" There are five more senior people in the room. You have a view. You've been sitting on it.
How do you respond?
A Deflect: "I think everyone has covered the key points pretty well."
B Over-qualify: "I'm not sure I have enough data to say definitively, but maybe one consideration could potentially be..."
C Deliver a clear OIR: "My read is [observation]. I think the implication is [X]. My recommendation would be [Y], though I'd want to pressure-test that with [Z]."
D Give a summary of what others have already said to avoid sticking your neck out.
The right move is C. When a CPO asks "what do you think," they want your read — not a summary of the room or an elaborate hedge. Option B is the most common failure: over-qualification that buries the actual perspective. OIR lets you be direct and calibrated at the same time. "I'd want to pressure-test with Z" is confidence calibration, not hedging — it shows intellectual honesty.
Practice · Scenario 3 of 3
The Dismissed Observation
You surface an observation in a leadership meeting using OIR. A senior leader responds quickly: "I don't think that's the issue — we looked at that already." The meeting moves on. You still believe your observation is valid.
What do you do?
A Let it go — a senior leader dismissed it, which probably means you were missing context.
B Repeat yourself more forcefully to make sure you were heard.
C Acknowledge their response, hold your ground gently, and offer to follow up: "Fair point — I might be missing context on what was reviewed. I'd like to follow up with you on [specific element] because I'm still seeing [X] in my teams."
D Go quiet for the rest of the meeting — you don't want to push back on someone more senior.
The right move is C. A single dismissal doesn't invalidate an observation — it means the room moved on, which happens for many reasons. Option C keeps you in the conversation without being combative: you acknowledge their point, you don't abandon your view, and you create a path to follow up. That's what strategic persistence looks like at director level.
