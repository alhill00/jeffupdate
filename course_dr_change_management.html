Foundation
Change at a Different Scale
Managing change as a director means driving a transition through managers you lead, across functions you influence, and through systems you don't control. The people most affected by your change may never meet with you directly. Their experience of the transition will be filtered through a management layer you're responsible for preparing.
This is structurally different from manager-level change. A manager can brief their team directly, answer questions in real time, and adjust as they go. A director is operating at scale — one level up from the people experiencing the change, with a management layer as the primary communication channel.
That layer can be your greatest asset in a change — or the point where your message gets filtered, softened, delayed, or undermined. Preparing it is not optional.
The director change failure pattern: announce the change, give the deck, assume execution follows. Six weeks later: inconsistent rollout, confused Team Members, managers who can't answer basic questions.
Foundation
What Director-Level Change Requires
Manager-level change is largely about your team. Director-level change requires three things that managers don't have to think about in the same way.
1

Cross-Functional Alignment Before Execution

Changes that affect one function often ripple into others. Before you announce, you need the functions whose work intersects with yours to be informed — and ideally supportive. A change that surprises a peer director mid-execution creates friction that delays the change and damages relationships.

2

Manager Enablement

Your managers are the change messengers — the people who will sit with your Team Members' questions, concerns, and resistance. If they're not prepared to answer what's coming, every question that doesn't get a good answer erodes trust in the change. Preparing your managers is not briefing them — it's equipping them.

3

Simultaneous Up and Down Communication

You're communicating "on track" to leadership AND "here's what's hard" to your team — at the same time. These messages have to be compatible. If you're overstating progress upward or sugarcoating difficulty downward, the dissonance surfaces — and it's expensive when it does.

Foundation
The Director Change Failure Pattern
The most common director-level change failure isn't resistance — it's under-preparation. The director makes the decision, briefs the management team, and expects execution to follow naturally. What they miss is that managers can absorb a briefing without being equipped to lead the change.
There's a meaningful difference between a manager who understands the change and a manager who can answer "why is this happening?" from a frustrated Team Member at 7am before a shift. Briefing produces the first. Enablement produces the second.
The question your managers will get: "Did you know about this?" "Why didn't we have more input?" "What does this mean for my job?" If your managers can't answer those questions confidently and consistently, the change is already in trouble — regardless of how clear your announcement was.
The Change Architecture
Four Elements of a Change Architecture
Don't launch a change without these four elements in place. The absence of any one of them is a predictable failure point.

Change Coalition

Who needs to be aligned before you start? Which peers and functions need to know or support it? Build the coalition before you announce — not during execution. A coalition built after the announcement is damage control, not architecture.

Manager Enablement

What do your managers need to know, say, and do? What questions will they get? Give them the talking points, the rationale, the anticipated concerns, and the answers — before the announcement reaches Team Members.

Communication Cadence

What gets communicated when and by whom? A single announcement is rarely enough. Plan your first communication, your follow-up after questions surface, and your "here's what we're hearing" update that closes the loop.

Feedback Loops

How will you know if it's working? What signals are you watching? Create a structured way to hear what's actually happening at the front line — not just what's being reported up. Feedback loops let you adjust in time to matter.

Foundation
Pacing the Change
The timing of how you launch and communicate a change is as important as the content of the change itself. Three principles for getting the pacing right.
1

Announce with Enough Lead Time for Managers to Prepare

If your managers hear about the change at the same time as Team Members, they have no time to prepare, process, or position themselves as informed leaders. They'll look as surprised as everyone else — which undermines both the change and their credibility as leaders of it.

2

Communicate the Why Before the What

People tolerate change better when they understand why it's happening. Lead with purpose and rationale, then the specifics of what's changing. "We're doing this because X" before "here's what will be different" — not after it.

3

Build Two-Way Communication Moments

Not just "here's the change" but "here's what we're hearing from you." Regular updates that close the loop — acknowledging the concerns people have raised and what's being done about them — demonstrate that feedback is being received, not just collected.

Launching Before Alignment Is Complete
You're driving a process change that intersects with three other functions. Two of the three are aligned. The third — a key one — hasn't fully bought in yet. Leadership pressure is mounting to announce and begin. What do you do?
AAnnounce and begin — you have two-thirds alignment, and the third function will come around once they see the change in motion.
BHold the announcement. Request specific time with the unaligned function this week to understand their concern and find a path to alignment. Then set a firm announcement date with leadership.
CAnnounce internally to your own org while continuing to work on cross-functional alignment — you can manage both tracks simultaneously.
DEscalate to your CPO — if alignment is blocked, this is an executive-level decision, not yours to resolve.

A Manager Actively Undermining the Change
Two weeks into a change rollout, you hear through a skip-level conversation that one of your managers has been telling their team privately that they don't think this change is the right move. Team members are confused about whether to follow the new process. What do you do?
AHave a direct conversation with the manager: acknowledge their concern is valid, but name the impact their behavior is having — and set a clear expectation: disagree privately, commit publicly.
BGo directly to the manager's team to reinforce the change — bypass the manager for now and restore clarity directly.
CLet it play out — the manager will either come around as they see the change working, or their resistance will surface in performance data over time.
DFind out from other managers whether this is widespread — if it's an isolated case, you can manage it quietly.
Practice
When the Change Is Taking Longer Than Expected
A change you announced six weeks ago with a 90-day timeline is running behind. Adoption is inconsistent across your org. You're reporting to leadership that things are "on track." Your managers know they're not. What do you do?
AKeep the optimistic messaging to leadership for another two weeks — you're confident you can close the gap and don't want to cause concern prematurely.
BUpdate leadership accurately: the timeline is at risk, here's the specific adoption gap, here's your recovery plan. Then have a direct conversation with your managers about closing the gap — together.
CExtend the deadline quietly — adjust the internal milestone and continue reporting on the revised plan without flagging the change to leadership.
DAsk your managers to push harder on their teams to close the adoption gap — they're responsible for their teams' compliance with the change.
Practice
Navigating a Change You Personally Disagree With
Your CPO has directed a structural change you're responsible for implementing. You have significant reservations — you think it solves the wrong problem. You've shared your concerns privately and been heard, but the decision stands. Now you're standing in front of your management team to roll it out. What's your approach?
ABe transparent with your managers: "Honestly, this isn't the direction I would have chosen, but here's why leadership made this call and here's how we're going to make it work."
BCommit fully to the change in how you present it — your personal reservations stay private, and your job now is to lead the execution with full conviction.
CGive your managers the information they need to execute while being honest that you're still working through it yourself — authenticity builds trust.
DPresent it as a work-in-progress and keep the door open for adjustments — this signals to your team that the change isn't fully committed.
Practice
When to Pause a Change
Three weeks into a rollout, your feedback loops are showing significantly more disruption than anticipated — Team Member confusion is high, two managers are struggling, and a critical process dependency wasn't accounted for. The change is causing operational problems. What do you do?
APush through — operational disruption in the first few weeks of a change is expected. Pausing signals weakness.
BAssess the data, then make the call: if the dependency gap is fixable and the confusion is addressable, name the specific interventions and a timeline. If the change has a structural flaw, recommend a pause to leadership — with the specific problem and a revised plan.
CRoll back to the previous state and restart the change process from the beginning — a clean restart is better than a messy rollout.
DReassign the two struggling managers to different teams temporarily — the change is sound, the execution team is the problem.

Course Complete
You've finished Leading Change at Director Level. The directors who lead change well aren't the ones with the best decks — they're the ones who build the architecture before they announce, equip the managers who carry the message, and stay close to the front line as it unfolds.

Four change commitments at director level:

Never announce before building the coalition — alignment before execution, every time
Equip your managers to answer the questions their teams will ask — talking points, rationale, and anticipated concerns
Create a feedback loop before you need one — know how you'll hear what's happening at the front line
Over-communicate the why — more than you think is necessary, and more often
