Foundation
The Math Nobody Does
A 10-person, 1-hour meeting costs 10 hours of company time. Not 1 hour — 10. Most managers run 5 to 10 meetings per week. Multiply that across a department of leaders, each running their own meeting load.
Meetings are the single largest discretionary use of team time. They're also the most underexamined.
The question isn't "should we meet?" — it's "what justifies this investment?" Every meeting is a choice to spend collective time on something. The best managers treat that choice with the same rigor they'd apply to any resource decision.
Reality check: If your team spends 6 hours per week in meetings, and the meeting quality is low, that's 6 hours per person per week that could be redirected to actual work — or to fewer, better meetings.
Foundation
Three Traps That Waste Everyone's Time
Most meeting problems fall into one of three categories. Most managers have experienced all three — sometimes in the same week.

Trap 1: Should've Been an Email

  • Status updates with no decisions
  • FYIs that don't require dialogue
  • Information that could be read in 3 minutes

Trap 2: No Clear Outcome

  • No one knows what "productive" looks like
  • Meeting ends with "great discussion"
  • Action items are vague or absent

Trap 3: Wrong People in the Room

  • Decision-maker is absent — no one can actually decide
  • Too many passengers — people attending who have nothing to contribute
  • Relevant expertise missing — discussion circles without resolution
The fix for all three starts before the meeting is even scheduled.

Agenda vs. Purpose — They're Not the Same
Most meeting invites have an agenda. Very few have a purpose. These are different things.

Agenda

  • A list of topics to cover
  • "Discuss Q3 priorities"
  • "Review project status"
  • Tells you what you'll talk about
  • Ends with: "Good discussion"

Purpose

  • An outcome you're driving toward
  • "Align on top 3 Q3 priorities and assign owners"
  • "Identify blockers and decide who resolves each"
  • Tells you what success looks like
  • Ends with: decisions and next steps
If you can't write a one-sentence purpose for the meeting, the meeting isn't ready to be scheduled. The purpose also answers the first question anyone should ask: "Does this need to be a meeting?"

Running the Room
The hardest part of running a meeting isn't the structure — it's managing what happens in the room. Three situations every manager faces:

The Tangent

  • The discussion drifts somewhere important-but-not-now
  • Move: "That's worth a real conversation — let me park it."
  • Capture it visibly. Come back to it only if time allows.

The Time Crunch

  • 15 minutes in, 20 minutes of content left
  • Move: "We're getting close on time — let's get to the decision."
  • Name the trade-off, make the call, move on.

The Quiet Room

  • One or two voices dominate; others go silent
  • Move: Directed questions — "Sam, what's your read on this?"
  • Quieter voices often hold the most useful perspective. Create the opening.
Running the room is a skill. The three moves above handle 80% of the situations you'll face.
The Meeting Architecture
The Meeting That Should Have Been an Email
You're 15 minutes into a 30-minute team meeting. You realize nothing here requires a decision and there's no real-time dialogue needed — you're mostly reading updates. What do you do?
AFinish the meeting — ending it early would feel disorganized and people already made time for it.
BStop, acknowledge it, and give everyone 15 minutes back — then move this content to a shared doc going forward.
CSpeed through the rest as fast as possible so at least the content gets covered.
DOpen it up for discussion to justify the time — even if there's nothing to decide.
The Meeting Architecture
Pulling a Derailed Discussion Back
A team meeting that was supposed to decide on a new process has turned into a 20-minute debate about a historical decision that can't be changed. The group is spinning. You have 10 minutes left.
ALet the conversation run — if it's important to them, it's important to address.
BInterrupt clearly: "I want to park this — we can't change what happened, but we can decide on the path forward. Let's use the last 10 minutes to do that."
CCall a new meeting to address the historical issue separately before making any decisions today.
DEnd the meeting early since the purpose can't be accomplished — try again next week.
Practice
When the Group Isn't Converging
Your team has been discussing three possible approaches to a project for 25 minutes. No one's wrong — but no one's agreeing either. The meeting is supposed to end in 5 minutes with a decision. What do you do?
ACall for a vote — majority rules, and you need to move forward.
BName the two closest options, state the trade-off clearly, and make the call — or explicitly delegate the decision with a deadline.
CSchedule another meeting to give people more time to think it through.
DAsk everyone to send you their preference by email after the meeting so you can decide in private.
Practice
Decision-Maker Is Absent
You're in a meeting to decide on a budget reallocation. The person who needs to approve it didn't show up. The team is ready to discuss. What do you do?
AMake a provisional decision as a group — document it and get approval later.
BUse the time to do the prep work — align on options, document the trade-offs, so the decision-maker can review and decide quickly.
CCancel the meeting and reschedule when everyone can attend.
DProceed with the full discussion and decision anyway — you'll brief them separately.
Practice
No Clear Decision Was Reached
A meeting just ended. The discussion was good — but you're not confident everyone left with the same understanding of what was decided or who owns next steps. What do you do in the next 24 hours?
ATrust that people will figure it out — the discussion was thorough enough.
BSend a follow-up message within 24 hours that names the decision made (or not made), lists action items with owners and deadlines, and sets the next checkpoint.
CAsk one team member to send out notes so you don't have to.
DSchedule a follow-up meeting to close the loop in person.
