Foundation
The Ceiling of the Silo
Most of the work that actually matters crosses departmental lines. A product decision needs Operations. A Guest experience initiative needs Tech. A people process needs Finance and Legal. Cross-functional work isn't the exception — it's where outcomes happen.
A leader who can only get things done within their own team has a ceiling. Their impact stops at their org chart.
A leader who knows how to partner across functions multiplies their impact — and builds the visibility that leads to broader responsibility. Cross-functional credibility is one of the clearest signals of readiness for more.
Foundation
Why Cross-Functional Is Hard — and It's Not Personal
Cross-functional collaboration fails constantly. Not because people are difficult — but because the structure makes it hard.

The Structural Realities

  • No authority over the other team
  • Competing priorities and different deadlines
  • Different definitions of success
  • Unclear ownership at the seams
  • No shared accountability system

The Wrong Interpretation

  • "They don't care about our work"
  • "Their leader doesn't prioritize this"
  • "It's a personality conflict"
  • "They're not collaborative people"
  • "We need to escalate to get anything done"
The mistake is treating structural problems as personal failures. Most cross-functional friction has nothing to do with the people involved — it's about the system they're operating in.

The Pattern That Plays Out Every Time
Here's the most common cross-functional failure sequence — you've probably seen it, or been in it.
1
The AssumptionYour priority = everyone's priority
"This is urgent for us — they'll know it needs to happen."
2
The SilencePartner team doesn't respond
They're managing 12 other priorities that are also urgent to someone.
3
The EscalationGoes to your leader
"I can't get their team to move on this."
4
The ConflictVPs get involved, relationship damaged
Now it's a political problem, not a work problem.
Most of this was avoidable with one early conversation. The kind where you name both sides' priorities and ask what success looks like for both teams.
The Partnership Model
Four Elements of a Real Partnership
Effective cross-functional work is built on partnership, not requests. The Partnership Model gives you four things to establish before you need anything.
1
Shared ProblemUnderstand before asking
"Here's what we're trying to solve — does that match what you see?"
2
Named ConstraintsBoth sides' limits on the table
"Here's what I can and can't do — what about your team?"
3
Shared SuccessWhat does "this worked" look like?
Define the win together — not just what's a win for you.
4
Clear RhythmSingle POC + communication cadence
Who talks to whom, how often, on what — agreed upfront.
The relationship account: Deposit before you withdraw. Build goodwill with cross-functional partners before you need a favor. The manager who only shows up when they need something is the one who gets deprioritized when priorities compete.
Foundation
Influence Without Authority
You can't order a cross-functional partner to prioritize your work. So how you frame the ask matters enormously.

The Requester Frame

  • "I need your team to do X by Friday."
  • Positions you as the demanding party
  • Creates a transactional dynamic
  • Easy to deprioritize when things get busy

The Partner Frame

  • "We have a problem I think we can both help solve."
  • Positions you as a collaborator
  • Creates shared ownership
  • Harder to ignore when it's your problem too
Shared problem, not your problem that needs their help. The frame changes the conversation — and the relationship.
The Partnership Model
Partner Team Isn't Responding
You've sent two messages to a cross-functional partner requesting a data pull your team needs. No response after a week. The work is blocked. What do you do?
AEscalate to your leader — if they can't respond to your messages, their leader needs to know.
BPick up the phone or walk over — find them directly, acknowledge they're probably slammed, and ask for 5 minutes to align on timing.
CSend a third message, this time CC'ing your leader to signal urgency.
DWork around them — find the data another way so you're not dependent on their team.
The Partnership Model
Competing Priorities Stalling Shared Work
A cross-functional project you're co-leading has stalled. The partner team keeps deprioritizing your shared milestones because of internal deadlines. The project is falling behind. What's your move?
AEscalate to leadership on both sides — if it's not getting done, the right people need to weigh in.
BSit down with the partner lead, surface both teams' current priorities, and renegotiate the timeline or scope together — then document the agreement.
CTake over the work yourself so it gets done — your team can absorb the additional load for now.
DWait them out — their internal deadlines will pass and then they'll have capacity.
Practice
How to Escalate Without Burning the Bridge
A cross-functional blocker is affecting your team's ability to deliver. You've tried to resolve it directly and haven't been able to. You need to escalate. What's the right approach?
ATell your leader about the problem and let them handle it however they see fit.
BTell your leader you're escalating and why — and let the partner lead know you're doing so before your leader reaches out. Keep the relationship intact by not making it a surprise.
CSend an email to both your leader and the partner leader at the same time, so everyone has the same information.
DDon't escalate — it will damage the relationship and you'll need them again in the future.
Practice
When Cross-Functional Work Fails
A joint initiative between your team and another department didn't deliver. The outcome fell short and both teams are pointing at each other. You need to debrief it. What's your move?
ADocument your team's contributions so it's clear where the gaps were on their side.
BRequest a joint debrief with both teams — focus on what structural gaps (ownership, communication, clarity) led to the failure, not who failed.
CDebrief your own team separately and move on — cross-functional post-mortems rarely go well.
DRaise it with your leader and let them facilitate the debrief to remove bias.
Practice
Building Goodwill Before You Need It
You know you'll need significant help from the Finance team in Q4. It's currently Q2. You have no current asks. What's the highest-leverage thing you can do right now?
ADo nothing — you don't want to come across as transactional by building a relationship you'll later need something from.
BReach out to your Finance counterpart now — learn what they're working on, offer to help where you can, and build the relationship before the pressure is on.
CWait until Q3 and give them plenty of advance notice of your Q4 needs.
DAsk your leader to connect you with the Finance leader — relationships built at that level carry more weight.
