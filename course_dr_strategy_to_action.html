Where Strategy Goes to Die
Company strategy doesn't usually fail at the top — it fails in the middle. Specifically, at the director level.
The CPO announces a direction. The VP translates it for their team. The Director... forwards the deck. Or says "we'll figure out what this means for us." Or holds a meeting where everyone leaves more confused than when they arrived.
This isn't a knowledge gap — directors understand the strategy. It's a translation gap. And it costs more than most organizations realize.
Your role at the director level isn't simply to receive strategy and pass it along. It's to make it legible — to turn company-level direction into department-level clarity that your managers and teams can actually act on.
In this course: You'll learn The Translation Model — a three-step framework for closing the gap between what leadership announces and what your team actually does differently.

The Translation Gap
When a director can't translate strategy into action, several things happen simultaneously — and most of them are invisible until they compound.

Managers Misinterpret

  • Each manager makes their own interpretation
  • Usually incomplete or incorrect
  • Inconsistency spreads across the department

Teams Disconnect

  • Team Members feel separate from company direction
  • Same initiative lands differently in every department
  • Effort goes toward wrong priorities

The Director Stalls

  • Becomes a pass-through, not a multiplier
  • Loses strategic credibility with both VP and managers
  • Reactive instead of directional
Frame it clearly: Your job isn't to pass the message down. It's to make it mean something for your context. A director who forwards the deck isn't translating — they're delegating the translation to people who don't have the context to do it well.
The translation gap isn't about intent — it's about skill. Most directors who struggle here are working hard and care about alignment. They just haven't been given a framework for how translation actually works.

What Good Translation Looks Like
A director who translates well does three things consistently — before any cascade conversation begins.
1

Connect the company's why to your department's what

They bridge the gap between the executive-level rationale and what that rationale means for how their specific department operates day to day.

2

Tell managers what changes and what stays the same

They're specific — not "we need to align" but "here's exactly what shifts and here's what we can stop worrying about." Both parts of that message matter equally.

3

Define success at the team level

Not just what success looks like for the company — what it looks like for their department in 30 days. Concrete, observable, and owned by the director's team.

The Translation Model
Three steps. Run them every time a new company priority lands on your desk. The whole model takes 20–30 minutes of focused thinking — and it's the difference between a cascade that sticks and one that evaporates.
1

Company Priority → Department Implication

What does this initiative mean for how your department operates? Not what it means in general — what it means here. This is the step most directors skip. They repeat the company priority instead of translating it.

2

Department Implication → Behavior Change

What do your managers and teams need to start doing, stop doing, or do differently? Be specific enough that a manager can walk out of a meeting and act — not just agree. "Be more Guest-focused" is not a behavior change. "Include one Guest feedback data point in every weekly standup" is.

3

Behavior Change → Success Signal

How will you and your team know the translation worked? What does it look like in 30 days if the message actually landed? Define this before you cascade — so you can check it after.

The Three Manager Questions
Every manager on your team needs three questions answered before they can cascade effectively. If any one of these is missing, they'll fill the gap themselves — inconsistently.
1

What changes?

What are we doing differently because of this? Name it specifically. Vague answers here create vague execution across every team below you.

2

What stays the same?

What can we stop worrying about or deprioritizing? This question is just as important as the first — it gives managers permission to focus, and prevents them from treating every initiative as additive to an already full plate.

3

What does success look like?

How will we know if we're doing it right? Give managers something they can measure or observe — not just a direction to move toward.

The test: If you can answer all three clearly before your manager meeting, your cascade will work. If you can't, your managers will fill in the gaps themselves — inconsistently.

The Translation Test
Before any cascade meeting, run this three-question test on yourself. It takes two minutes and tells you whether you're ready to translate.
1

Can you describe the company initiative in one sentence?

Not a paragraph. Not "it's complicated." One clear sentence that any manager on your team could understand without having been in the room.

2

Can you describe what it means for your department in one sentence?

Again — one sentence. This is your translation. If you're reaching for multiple caveats and qualifiers, you're not ready yet.

3

Can you name one thing that will be different for your team next Monday?

Specific. Observable. Grounded. Not "we'll have a better culture of alignment" — something concrete that will actually change.

If you can't answer all three in plain language, you're not ready to translate yet. Go back one level — talk to your VP, re-read the brief, or sit with it longer. A confused director makes a confused department.

When the Strategy Pivots Mid-Year
The hardest translation isn't the new initiative — it's the pivot. When leadership changes direction on something your team has been working toward, the translation challenge is doubled.
You have to un-translate the old direction while translating the new one — without making leadership look chaotic or your team feel jerked around.
Four principles for pivot translation:
1

Frame the pivot as new information, not a failure

"We learned something that changes the picture" lands differently than "that wasn't working." One moves forward; the other assigns blame.

2

Acknowledge the work that was done

Name it explicitly. "The work we did on X built the foundation for this next direction." Team Members need to know their effort wasn't wasted before they can fully commit to what's next.

3

Be clear about what it unlocks

The new direction should open something up, not just shut something down. Name what becomes possible now that wasn't before.

4

Don't editorialize about why the change happened

Your team is watching how you frame this. Editorializing — even sympathetically — undermines trust in leadership and puts you in an awkward position. Translate; don't narrate.

Scenario: The All-Hands Moment
At a company all-hands, the CPO announces a new Guest Experience initiative — every department is expected to "align their work to the Guest journey." Your managers turn to you in the meeting. After it ends, they're in your office in 10 minutes asking what it means for your team.
What do you do?
A "I'm still processing it — let me get clarity from my VP first before we do anything."
B "It applies to customer-facing teams, not really us."
C "Here's my initial read: for our team, aligning to the Guest journey means [X]. I want to hear your thinking too — let's meet Thursday to work through what changes."
D "Forward everyone the slides — they'll explain it better than I can."
Correct. You don't have to have all the answers in the first 10 minutes. But you do have to have a read — and a plan to get to clarity together. Option C shows strategic ownership without pretending to have certainty you don't yet have. Option A defers the work upward instead of doing it. Option B makes an unfounded assumption. Option D is the textbook pass-through move.
Not quite. The right move is C. You don't need all the answers in the first 10 minutes — but you do need a read and a plan. Option A defers the work upward instead of doing it. Option B makes an unfounded assumption that shuts down the conversation. Option D is the textbook pass-through — not a translation.

Scenario: The Inconsistent Cascade
Three weeks after a company initiative launched, you're hearing different things from your managers' teams. One team thinks the initiative is a top priority. Another thinks it's background context. A third has barely heard about it. Numbers are fine. Nobody's complained. But the inconsistency bothers you.
What do you do?
A It's probably fine — if numbers are good, the translation was good enough.
B Bring your managers back together and create a shared understanding of what the initiative means for your department — then check how each of them has communicated it.
C Send a department-wide email clarifying the initiative yourself.
D Ask HR to run a communication audit.
Correct. Inconsistent cascade is a translation failure — usually the director's, not the managers'. If three managers interpreted it three different ways, the original translation was probably too vague. The fix isn't a corrective memo — it's a shared alignment conversation, then better translation before the next initiative. Going around your managers with a department-wide email (Option C) undercuts them and doesn't address the root cause.
Not quite. The right answer is B. Inconsistent cascade is usually a director-level translation failure. If three managers heard three different messages, the original framing was too vague. The fix is a shared alignment conversation — not a memo that bypasses your managers, not complacency because the numbers look okay, and definitely not a formal audit.

Scenario: The One-Sentence Test
Your VP asks you to "represent the team's perspective" on a new company initiative in tomorrow's leadership meeting. You've read the brief. You understand it generally. But you've been so busy you haven't thought through what it means specifically for your department.
What do you do?
A Wing it — you know enough to speak generally, and you can be honest that you're still working through the implications.
B Decline to speak until you've had more time to prepare.
C Block 30 minutes tonight to run the translation model: company priority → department implication → behavior change. Come prepared with one specific, grounded perspective.
D Email your VP saying your team is "fully aligned" to avoid looking unprepared.
Correct. Thirty minutes of translation prep is the difference between sounding strategic and sounding like you're winging it. The translation model isn't a lengthy exercise — it's three focused questions. Running it the night before means you show up with a point of view, not just presence. Option A risks credibility in the room. Option B is unnecessary — the prep is available to you. Option D is worse than going unprepared.
Not quite. The right answer is C. The translation model is a 30-minute exercise — not an all-night project. Running it before the meeting means you show up with a grounded point of view rather than general agreement. Winging it (A) risks your strategic credibility. Declining (B) is unnecessary when the prep is within reach. False confidence (D) erodes trust when the truth surfaces.

A director receives a new company initiative and immediately forwards the memo to their managers with the note "FYI — let me know if you have questions." What's missing?
A) A deadline for when managers should respond.
B) The director's own translation — what it means for their department specifically.
C) A link to the original announcement.
D) Approval from HR before distributing.
Correct — B. The "FYI + let me know if questions" approach is the textbook pass-through. The director's job is to arrive with a translation, not to forward information and wait for managers to surface confusion. Without the director's interpretation, every manager will create their own — inconsistently.
The answer is B. The "FYI + let me know if questions" approach is the textbook pass-through. What's missing is the director's translation — what this initiative means specifically for their department. Deadlines, links, and approvals are secondary. The core gap is strategic interpretation.

One of the three manager questions is "What stays the same?" Why is this question important?
A) It helps managers avoid doing extra work.
B) It gives managers permission to deprioritize things that haven't changed, so they can focus energy on what actually needs to shift.
C) It's a formality — most things stay the same anyway.

Correct — B. Without "what stays the same," managers treat every initiative as additive — piling new priorities onto an already-full list. Giving explicit permission to hold certain things steady frees up cognitive and execution bandwidth for what actually needs to change. It's not about avoiding work — it's about focusing effort where it matters.
The answer is B. "What stays the same" isn't a formality or a laziness enabler — it's a focus tool. Without it, managers treat every new initiative as an addition to their list instead of a replacement or shift. Giving permission to hold certain things steady is what makes bandwidth for the change that actually matters.

The answer is B. If you can't name one concrete thing that changes next Monday, you haven't finished translating. Cascading anyway means cascading vagueness — and every manager fills that gap differently. Go back one level, get clearer, then cascade. This is the Translation Test working exactly as designed.

Your organization is pivoting away from a major initiative your team has been working on for two months. How do you translate this to your managers?
