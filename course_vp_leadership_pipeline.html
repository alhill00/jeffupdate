Foundation
The Measure of VP Legacy
The measure of VP legacy is whether the organization can build the next thing without them. Pipeline development builds the pool from which successors come — it requires a longer horizon and more deliberate investment than succession planning alone.
Succession planning asks: who fills this seat? Pipeline development asks: are we systematically building the leaders who will fill the next ten seats — five years from now?
What pipeline requires that performance management doesn't: a clear leadership model, designed development experiences, coaching infrastructure, and visibility architecture — all working together as a system, not a series of individual decisions.
Foundation
Experience Alone Doesn't Develop Leaders
The most common pipeline myth: put people in challenging roles and they'll develop. Experience is necessary but not sufficient.

Experience Without Pipeline

  • Stretch roles assigned — no coaching infrastructure
  • High-potentials learn from trial and error
  • Some develop, others burn out or leave
  • Development is accidental, not systematic

Experience With Pipeline Infrastructure

  • Stretch roles paired with coaching and reflection
  • High-potentials process experiences with support
  • Learning is accelerated and transferred
  • Development is systematic and compounding
The formula: Experience + Processing = Leader development. Experience alone builds muscle memory. Experience plus coaching, reflection, and feedback builds judgment — the thing you actually need leaders to have.
Foundation
The VP's Personal Role in Pipeline Development
Pipeline development isn't something VPs delegate entirely to L&D. The VP's personal involvement is one of the highest-return activities in their role — and one of the most commonly underdone.
1
Know Two Generations of Leaders
Not just direct reports, but leaders two levels down who are the future of your function. Knowing their names, strengths, and development arcs is the first move of a VP who's building a pipeline.
2
Create Direct Visibility Moments
Bring high-potentials into executive settings. Give them projects with real leadership exposure — stakeholder responsibility, cross-functional ownership, decisions with consequence. Visibility accelerates development and retention simultaneously.
3
Be a Demanding Coach
Develop the ones who need the most work — not just the ones who are easiest to develop. The highest return on coaching investment comes from people who are close to readiness with a specific gap, not from the ones who would have succeeded anyway.
The Pipeline Model
Four Pipeline Components
A leadership pipeline is a system with four components. Weakness in any one component limits the whole system's output.
1
Leadership Model
What effective leadership looks like at each level — behavioral descriptions, not values statements. Without a clear model, development has no target and assessment has no standard.
2
Development Experiences
Specific experiences that build leadership characteristics: stretch roles, turnaround assignments, cross-functional projects, significant stakeholder responsibility. Not just any challenge — the right challenge for the development need.
3
Coaching Infrastructure
Relationships, feedback, and reflection that convert experience into learning. Experience alone builds muscle memory. Experience plus coaching builds judgment — which is what leaders actually need.
4
Visibility Architecture
Mechanisms for leadership potential to be seen by the right people across the organization. High-potentials who aren't visible to senior leadership don't get selected, regardless of their capability.
Foundation
Pipeline vs. Succession: The Difference That Matters
Succession planning and pipeline development are related but distinct. Confusing them leads to underinvestment in the longer horizon.

Succession Planning

  • Who fills specific seats when they open?
  • Shorter horizon — 0-24 months
  • Role-specific readiness
  • Reactive to current organizational structure

Pipeline Development

  • What pool of leaders is the org building toward?
  • Longer horizon — 3-7 years
  • Leadership characteristics, not role-specific
  • Proactive investment in future organizational capacity
The relationship: A strong pipeline feeds succession. Organizations that do succession well but skip pipeline development will run out of ready candidates. Both are required — at different time horizons.

The Hidden High-Potential
In a skip-level conversation, you meet a Team Member two levels below you who demonstrates exceptional judgment, asks sharper questions than most of your directors, and articulates a strategic perspective you haven't heard from anyone at their level. Their manager hasn't flagged them in any talent conversation.
AFile the observation away — one conversation isn't enough to make a talent judgment, and it's the manager's role to surface pipeline candidates.
BFollow up with the manager: share what you observed, ask about their development arc, and explore what visibility and development opportunities exist — or should.
CBegin mentoring the Team Member directly without involving the manager, since the manager has missed this.
DWait to see if the Team Member surfaces through a formal talent review process before taking any action.

Designing the Right Development Experience
A high-potential director in your org needs to develop cross-functional influence — the ability to drive alignment without authority. What's the right development experience?
ASend them to an executive education program focused on influence and persuasion.
BGive them ownership of a cross-functional initiative that requires alignment across teams they don't control — with coaching support to process what they learn.
CHave them shadow you in cross-functional meetings for a quarter to observe how you approach alignment.
DAssign them to read three books on influence and discuss key takeaways in their next development conversation.
Practice
The Management Layer Not Investing Below Them
You realize your management layer is doing succession planning for their own direct reports but has no visibility into or investment in the leadership development of Team Members two levels below them. This means your pipeline starts two levels too high.
AAccept it — managers can only be reasonably expected to develop their direct reports.
BMake pipeline visibility two levels down an explicit expectation for directors, create the conversation structure for it, and coach directors on what this looks like in practice.
CBuild a centralized talent program that identifies and develops high-potentials org-wide, independent of the management layer.
DConduct quarterly skip-level meetings yourself to identify pipeline candidates two levels down.
Practice
Building a Leadership Model for Your Function
Your function doesn't have a leadership model — there's no shared language for what great leadership looks like at each level, and development conversations lack a common standard. How do you approach building one?
AAdopt the company's core values as the leadership model — they apply to everyone at every level.
BBuild behavioral descriptions of what effective leadership looks like at each level in your function — specific to your context, observable, and usable in development conversations.
CUse a well-known leadership competency framework from a consulting firm — it's faster than building your own.
DSurvey your managers on what great leadership looks like and compile the responses into a document.
Practice
Pipeline as a Shared VP Responsibility
Pipeline development in your organization is currently seen as each VP's individual responsibility for their own function. The result: high-potentials with cross-functional leadership characteristics are invisible to peers, and cross-functional development experiences don't happen. How do you change this?
AContinue managing pipeline within your function — changing peer behavior is beyond your influence.
BBring a proposal to the leadership team: create a cross-functional talent visibility forum where VPs share high-potentials and design cross-functional development experiences together.
CAsk HR to build a centralized program that creates cross-functional visibility without requiring VP coordination.
DIdentify high-potentials in peer functions yourself and propose individual development experiences directly to peer VPs.
