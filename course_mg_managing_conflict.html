The Conflict He Waited Too Long to Address
Marcus had two Team Members with growing tension. Different working styles, different opinions on how to prioritize, different ways of communicating — and it was starting to show. Short responses in meetings. Pointedly not asking each other's input. The energy in the room had changed.
Marcus noticed it. He hoped it would work itself out. It didn't. Four months later, one of them started job searching. Not because the work was bad — because the team environment had become something he didn't want to come to anymore.
By the time Marcus finally stepped in, the tension had calcified. What could have been a two-conversation fix became a months-long team recovery.
Early intervention is always cheaper than late. Conflict doesn't resolve on its own — it just finds a quieter place to live until it doesn't.
Foundation
Why Managers Avoid Conflict
The instinct to avoid is understandable. Most managers who let conflict go unaddressed aren't negligent — they're uncertain. And the uncertainty is real.

What They're Afraid Of

  • Making it worse by getting involved
  • Not knowing what to say
  • Being seen as taking sides
  • Damaging the relationship further

What Actually Happens When You Wait

  • The tension grows roots
  • Others on the team feel it
  • One or both people start disengaging
  • Your credibility as a leader erodes
Avoidance is a decision. Not stepping in is a choice — and it has consequences just as real as stepping in badly.
Foundation
What Avoidance Actually Does
The most common manager belief about unaddressed conflict: "I'm not getting involved — I'm preserving the relationship." This is almost never true.
Avoiding conflict doesn't preserve the relationship. It lets the relationship decay at a slower rate until it fails completely. The difference is only timing.

What Managers Think

"If I leave it alone, they'll work it out. It's not my place to get in the middle."

What's Actually Happening

Both people are interpreting your silence as permission — or as evidence that you don't see it, don't care, or can't help.

Conflict that goes unaddressed doesn't go away. It goes underground — where it's harder to reach, more expensive to fix, and more likely to cost you someone good.
The Conflict Approach
Three Types of Team Conflict
Not all conflict works the same way. Before you decide how to respond, understand what kind you're dealing with.
Task ConflictProductive when managed

Disagreement about what to do — approach, priority, strategy. Can surface better solutions when handled well.

Manager's move: Encourage it. Channel it toward the decision.
Process ConflictUsually disruptive

Disagreement about how to work together — roles, workflows, communication norms. Creates friction, slows the team.

Manager's move: Add structure. Clarify the working agreement.
Relationship ConflictAlways harmful

Personal tension — frustration with the person, not the problem. Almost always damages performance and team health.

Manager's move: Address directly. Do not wait for it to pass.
Foundation
The Three-Conversation Approach
When relationship conflict requires your intervention, don't skip straight to a group confrontation. The order matters.
1
Individual conversations first. Talk to each person separately before you ever bring them together. Listen for their perspective. Understand what they're experiencing without triangulating — don't share what the other person said.
2
Facilitate the joint conversation. Once you understand both sides, bring them together in a structured, focused conversation. Keep it anchored in behaviors — not character. Move toward a shared path forward, not a verdict.
3
Follow up separately. Resolution isn't a moment — it's a process. Check in with each person individually after to confirm the agreement held and surface anything that surfaced after the conversation.
Individual conversations before the joint one. Going directly to the group conversation without understanding each perspective first usually escalates — you're walking into the room without the information you need.

When It's Your Highest Performer
The conflict is between your highest performer and a newer Team Member. Your instinct is to take the experienced person's side — they probably see the situation more clearly. What's the risk of that instinct?
ANone — experienced Team Members usually do have better read on team dynamics.
BYou may be letting seniority substitute for evidence — which reinforces the behavior of the person with more status, not the person who's right.
CThe newer Team Member might feel unsupported — but that's manageable.
DYou risk losing the newer Team Member, who might be your problem in this situation.
Practice
When It Resurfaces
You had what you thought was a successful conflict resolution conversation three weeks ago. Now the tension is back — more subtle, but clearly there. What does this tell you, and what do you do?
AThe original conversation didn't work — try a different approach entirely this time.
BResolution takes time and needs reinforcement — check in individually, acknowledge that things have gotten tense again, and revisit what was agreed.
CEscalate to HR — a recurring conflict is beyond manager-level resolution.
DWait and see — sometimes the tension spikes before it finally resolves.
Practice
To Step In — Or Not
Two Team Members are clearly irritated with each other — but the tension seems contained to them, not spilling into the team. They're still hitting their work. How do you decide whether to intervene?
AIf work is getting done, don't intervene — adults manage their own professional relationships.
BCheck in individually with each person — not to address the conflict yet, but to understand whether it's actually contained or just not yet visible.
CAddress it publicly so the team knows you see it.
DWait for a clear performance impact before involving yourself.

Course Complete
You've finished Managing Conflict. The managers who keep great teams together are the ones who address tension early — before it becomes a story about why someone left.

What conflict-competent managers do differently:

Name conflicts early — not after they've fractured the team.
Have individual conversations before the joint one — always.
Keep the conversation anchored in behavior, not character.
Follow up after — resolution is a process, not a single conversation.
Recognize that avoidance is also a decision — and it has a cost.
