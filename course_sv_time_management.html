The math doesn't work. And if your calendar is controlled by other people's requests, you're not leading your time — you're just reacting to it.

What Actually Needs Your Attention
Not all work is equal. The mistake most new leaders make is treating all tasks as equally urgent.
The Eisenhower framework cuts through the noise: Urgent AND important = do it now. Important but not urgent = schedule it. Urgent but not important = delegate it. Neither = drop it or do it last.
Most leaders spend too much time in "urgent but not important" and not enough in "important but not scheduled." That second category is where leadership actually happens.

What's the right prioritization move?
AHelp your team member immediately — supporting your team is your primary job now.
BAsk your team member what specifically they need, give them 10 minutes of direction now, and reconnect after your report.
CTell your team member you'll be available after your report — they should have planned ahead.
DDelegate the report to someone else so you can help your team member fully.

Saying No Without Damaging Relationships
Most leaders say yes to everything until they can't deliver anything. There are three professional ways to decline work:
1. Trade-off offer: "I can do X, but I'll have to push Y — which would you like me to prioritize?" (Let your leader decide.)
2. Capacity statement: "I don't have capacity for that this week — here's what I can commit to."
3. Redirect: "That sounds important — let me find the right person to take this on."
Saying no early protects your team and your credibility. Saying yes to everything and underdelivering is the faster way to damage trust.

When Your Leader Asks You to Take on More
Your leader asks you to take on a new project "on top of" your current responsibilities. You're already at full capacity.
What do you do?
AAccept it and figure out how to manage — leaders are expected to handle more than they think possible.
BDecline clearly — if you can't do it well, you shouldn't do it at all.
CAccept and immediately bring your leader a prioritization conversation: "To take this on, I need your input on what to deprioritize."
DAccept and quietly let something else slip — your leader will understand.

Constant Interruptions Fragmenting Your Focus
You notice you're checking messages constantly throughout the day, which fragments your focus.
The most effective fix is:
ATurn notifications off permanently — focus is worth protecting.
BDesignate specific check-in windows (e.g., 8am, noon, 4pm) and protect focus time between them.
CReply to everything immediately so your inbox stays clear.
DAsk your team to reduce how often they message you.
