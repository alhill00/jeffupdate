Financial Acumen for Leaders

Director Level  ·  17 Screens  ·  ~25 min

Finance is strategy

At Breeze, financial acumen isn't an accounting skill — it's a leadership skill. Every decision you make as a Director has a dollar impact: staffing, scheduling, vendor selection, overtime, training spend, equipment, process changes.

The leaders who move fastest toward VP don't wait for finance to tell them how they're performing. They already know — because they own their numbers.

Breeze's core financial philosophy:
"Spend smartly — not don't spend." Every dollar should drive a return. The goal is not to minimize spend; it's to maximize the value of every dollar deployed.

How Breeze Makes Money

Before you can understand your department's finances, you need to understand Breeze's core business model. We're an ultra-low-cost carrier with a specific competitive advantage built around one aircraft.

The A220-300 Advantage

Our entire financial model is built on the A220-300. Here's why it matters:

  • 137 passengers — a unique gauge that fits underserved routes larger planes can't profitably fly
  • Lowest cost per trip of any aircraft in its class — gives us a structural cost advantage
  • Opens markets that are unprofitable for mainline carriers flying larger jets

This isn't just trivia — when your decisions affect utilization, staffing levels, or turn times, they directly affect how efficiently we're using this advantage.

The Language of Airline Finance

You'll hear these terms in every financial conversation at Breeze. Knowing them makes you a more credible voice in the room.

ASMs — Available Seat Miles (Capacity)

The total capacity we've put into the market. One seat flying one mile = one ASM. This is the denominator for most of our per-unit cost calculations.

RPMs — Revenue Passenger Miles (Traffic)

How many of those ASMs we actually sold. One paying passenger flying one mile = one RPM.

Load Factor

RPMs ÷ ASMs. The percentage of available seats we filled. Higher load factor = more revenue from the same cost base. A full plane is a profitable plane.

CASM — Cost per Available Seat Mile (in cents)

Our total operating cost divided by ASMs. This is the headline efficiency metric. Your job as a Director: don't let your department inflate CASM without a good reason.

TRASM — Total Revenue per Available Seat Mile (in cents)

Total revenue divided by ASMs. Includes ticket revenue, ancillary, bag fees, etc. The goal: TRASM > CASM. That's profit.

Budgeting: Zero-Based, Every Year

Breeze uses a zero-based / activity-based budgeting approach. This is the company's philosophy — not a suggestion.

The Breeze standard: "This is the Way."

Every dollar in your budget must be justified from zero each cycle. Last year's budget is not a starting point — it's a reference at best.

What this means for you

  • You can't assume you'll get what you got last year
  • Every line item needs to connect to an activity or outcome
  • Requests without justification don't survive budget season

The Annual Cycle

  • October–November: Budget planning season — this is when you build and defend your request
  • January: Approved budgets take effect
  • Quarterly: Budget updates and reforecasting — you'll review actuals vs. plan and explain variances

Your Department P&L

As a Director, you own a P&L — a profit and loss statement for your area. This is your financial scoreboard. You're responsible for knowing it, explaining it, and improving it.

Focus on controllable expenses

Not everything on your P&L is in your control — but a significant portion is. Controllable expenses are where your decisions show up: staffing costs, overtime, vendor spend, training budgets, supplies, and discretionary purchases.

The uncontrollable items (fuel allocation, aircraft depreciation, corporate overhead) matter for context — but your job is to own the controllable line.

Invoice-level accountability

At Breeze, financial review goes deep. Be ready to explain individual invoices, not just totals. "I'm not sure" is not an acceptable answer in a P&L review.

Know who approved what, why, and whether it was in the plan.

Monthly reviews

You should be reviewing your P&L monthly at minimum — before anyone else asks about it. The goal: no surprises. If something is trending wrong, you flag it before your leader does.

FP&A: Your Partner, Not Your Auditor

Many leaders treat FP&A like an oversight function — a group that checks your work and asks hard questions. At Breeze, that's the wrong frame.

The right frame: FP&A is a collaborative partner. They have data and context you don't. Use them.

What FP&A can do for you

  • Help you model out the financial impact of a decision before you make it
  • Show you trends in your department's spend before they become problems
  • Help you build a compelling budget case with the right numbers
  • Flag risks in your forecast that you might not see

How to work with them well

  • Schedule regular touchpoints — don't only call when something's wrong
  • Come to meetings with your own read of the numbers, then compare
  • Ask: "What am I not seeing in this data?"
  • Loop them in early on new initiatives with spend implications

Cost-Benefit Thinking

Every Director-level decision has a financial dimension. Even if money isn't the primary driver, cost-benefit thinking makes your proposals more credible and helps you prioritize.

Three questions before you spend

  1. What does this cost — fully loaded? Include labor time, vendor cost, and any downstream maintenance or operational costs.
  2. What does this return? Be specific. Efficiency gain, error reduction, revenue impact, retention improvement — tie it to something measurable.
  3. What's the cost of not doing it? Sometimes inaction is expensive too. Make the case for both sides.
The Breeze standard: "Spend smartly — not don't spend."

A $50K investment that saves $200K is a great decision. A $5K purchase with no clear return is a bad one. Think in returns, not just costs.

Scenario Practice

Work through these real-world situations. There's no single right answer — the goal is to practice thinking financially about leadership decisions.

Scenario 1 of 5

It's October. Your VP asks you to submit your department budget for next year. You pull last year's approved budget as a starting point and add 8% for expected growth.

What's wrong with this approach, and what should you do instead?

The problem: Breeze uses zero-based budgeting — last year's number is not a valid starting point. Adding a percentage to last year's budget won't survive review because it doesn't justify any individual line item from zero.

What to do instead: Start from the activities your department will perform next year. What does each activity cost? What staffing, tools, and resources does each require? Build up from zero, line by line. Then connect each request to a business outcome.

Scenario 2 of 5

Your monthly P&L shows that your department ran $42K over plan in September. Your CFO asks for an explanation in the Friday exec review. You didn't review your P&L this month.

What went wrong, and what's the standard going forward?

What went wrong: You were caught flat-footed. At Director level, "I didn't know" is a credibility problem — not just a knowledge gap. The CFO now owns the narrative about your department's spend.

The standard: Review your P&L every month before your leader does. When you're over plan, you should already know why — at the invoice level — and have a plan for correction or a rationale for variance. No surprises.

Scenario 3 of 5

You want to add a new software tool for your team — $28K annually. Your VP asks: "What's the return?" You say it will "improve efficiency and team morale."

How do you strengthen this case?

The problem: "Efficiency and morale" aren't financial returns. At Breeze, spending decisions need to connect to something measurable.

Stronger case: Estimate hours saved per week × average hourly cost × number of team members = hard dollar value. Then compare to $28K. If the tool saves 3 hours/week per 10 team members at $35/hr, that's ~$54K/year in recovered time — a clear positive return. Lead with that number.

Scenario 4 of 5

FP&A sends you a data pull showing your department's overtime spend is trending 22% above plan through Q3. You haven't talked to FP&A since the budget was approved in January.

What's the ideal FP&A relationship, and what would you do differently?

The pattern to avoid: Treating FP&A as a check-in-at-crisis function. If the first time you hear about a trend is when they reach out, you've already lost a quarter to a problem you could have caught earlier.

The ideal relationship: Schedule monthly touchpoints. Ask FP&A to flag anything trending unusual in your department before it becomes material. Come to those meetings having already reviewed your P&L — so you're comparing reads, not hearing news.

Scenario 5 of 5

A peer asks you: "What's the difference between TRASM and CASM, and why should I care as a department leader?"

How would you explain it?

CASM is cost per available seat mile — what it costs us to operate. TRASM is total revenue per available seat mile — what we earn. When TRASM exceeds CASM, Breeze is profitable on that capacity.

As a department leader: your decisions affect CASM directly. Inefficiency, unnecessary spend, and poor workforce planning inflate CASM. Your job is to deliver outcomes without inflating it unnecessarily.
