The Manager Who Communicated Thoroughly — and Nobody Got It
David was meticulous. Every staff meeting he gave thorough, detailed updates — context, background, nuance. His team nodded. Nobody questioned him. He felt good about it.
Two weeks later, the same team was heading in two completely different directions on the same project. When he dug in, he discovered half the team had interpreted the meeting one way, and the other half had heard something entirely different.
David had communicated. Nobody had received.
Communication isn't measured by what you said — it's measured by what landed. A message isn't "sent" until it's been received and understood.
Foundation
The Transmission Myth
Most leaders believe the problem is not enough communication. So they add more — more updates, more Slack messages, more all-hands, more emails. The quantity goes up. The confusion doesn't go down.
The real problem is almost never quantity. It's clarity.
Clarity is not dumbing down your message. It's respecting the listener's time and cognitive load enough to be precise. Clear communicators aren't less sophisticated — they're more disciplined.
The pattern: When a message fails, most managers send more of the same message rather than a clearer version of it. More repetition amplifies confusion — it doesn't resolve it.
Foundation
Four Modes. Most Managers Overuse One.
Every manager communicates across four distinct modes. Each one has a purpose, and each one fails when you use it in the wrong context.
Mode 1
Informing
Sharing context, updates, and FYI. No action required. The listener just needs to know.
Mode 2
Directing
Clear expectations, tasks, and deadlines. The listener needs to do something specific by a specific time.
Mode 3
Coaching
Questions, reflection, and development. The listener needs to think, not be told.
Mode 4
Influencing
Persuasion, advocacy, and change. The listener needs to be moved — not just informed.
Most managers overuse one mode — usually informing or directing — and underuse the others. The skill is matching mode to moment.
The Clear Message
The BLUF Framework — Bottom Line Up Front
Most communication buries the point. BLUF flips the structure: lead with what matters, then back it up.
1
Lead with the pointWhat do you need them to know or do?
"We're shifting the Q3 launch to October. Here's why and what changes."
2
Context nextWhy does it matter? What does it connect to?
"The vendor delay affects two dependencies. Leadership made the call Tuesday."
3
Details lastOnly what they actually need
"The new milestones are below. You own items 3 and 5."
Also critical: match the channel to the message. Urgent or complex = synchronous (call or meeting). FYI or async-okay = Slack or email. Sensitive or high-stakes = 1:1, never text.
The anti-pattern: Starting with backstory, burying the ask, and leaving people to infer the point. This isn't thoughtfulness — it's transferring your mental load to your listener.
Foundation
When Clarity Feels Unkind
Leaders often go vague to soften a hard message. "We might need to rethink some things" when the reality is "we're changing this process and some of you won't like it."
It feels compassionate. It isn't.

Vague delivery

  • Creates anxiety — what does "rethink" mean?
  • Fuels rumors — people fill the gap themselves
  • Erodes trust — they sense you're not being straight
  • Delays the team's ability to adapt

Compassionate clarity

  • Direct about the fact
  • Thoughtful about the delivery
  • Gives people what they need to move forward
  • Respects their ability to handle hard news
Vagueness doesn't protect people — it creates a vacuum that anxiety fills. Being clear about a hard thing is an act of respect, not cruelty.
The Clear Message
Delivering Hard News to Your Team
You need to tell your team that a project they've worked on for months has been deprioritized by leadership. It's not cancelled — but work stops now and it's unclear when it restarts.
What's the right way to deliver this?
ASend a Slack message that morning explaining the news briefly — no need to make it a bigger deal than it is.
BCall a team meeting, lead with the decision, then give the context and Q&A time — acknowledge the work they put in.
CDrop it into the weekly update without singling it out — it's one of several updates and you don't want to over-dramatize it.
DTell each person individually over the next few days so no one is blindsided in a group setting.
The Clear Message
Giving Honest Feedback on a Presentation
A team member asks for feedback on a presentation deck they've prepared. You review it — it needs significant revision. The structure is off, the core message is buried, and two slides won't land with the audience.
What do you do?
ASay "Good start — a few things to polish" and give general encouragement to avoid discouraging them.
BBe specific about the 2-3 most important things that need to change and why — name the actual problems.
CRewrite the key sections yourself and send back the revised deck — it's faster and they'll learn from seeing the fix.
DSay it looks fine and let it go — the presentation itself will show them what needs to change.

Choosing the Right Channel
A team member makes a significant error that affected a guest experience. The situation is resolved, but you need to address it with them. What's the right channel?
ASend a Slack message — it's documented and gives them time to think before responding.
BSchedule a 1:1 meeting — this is sensitive, complex, and deserves a real conversation.
CEmail the team member with the feedback — it creates a record and is less confrontational.
DBring it up at the next team meeting — others might benefit from hearing it too.

Structuring a Time-Sensitive Ask
You need something from a cross-functional partner by end of day — a data pull that will affect a decision tomorrow morning. You're going to Slack them. Using BLUF, which message lands best?
A"Hey — been working on the Q3 analysis and running into a gap. We're presenting tomorrow and I think the data you have would really help fill out the picture. Any chance you could send the August report when you get a chance?"
B"Quick ask: I need the August report by 5pm today for tomorrow's Q3 presentation. Can you send it over? Happy to explain the context if helpful."
C"Do you have a few minutes to talk about the Q3 analysis? I have some questions about the data."
D"Not sure if this is in your wheelhouse — but if you have the August report that would be amazing. No worries if not!"
Practice
When Someone Misunderstood Your Message
You sent a clear directive last week. A team member acted on it — but in the wrong direction. When you follow up, they cite your exact message as the reason for what they did. What's your move?
ARe-read your original message and point out where it clearly said what you meant — ask them to re-read it.
BAcknowledge the disconnect, clarify what you meant now, and note how you could have said it more clearly the first time.
CAccept that miscommunication happens and focus entirely on fixing the outcome rather than the communication.
DSend the corrected direction to the whole team so everyone is aligned going forward.
