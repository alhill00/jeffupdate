Foundation
The Meeting That Existed Because It Always Had
Chris inherited a weekly team meeting. Nobody was engaged. People were on their phones. At the end, nobody could say what was decided.
Chris thought the team was disengaged. The team thought the meetings were a waste of time. Both were right.
The meeting existed because it always had — not because it served a purpose.
A meeting that serves no clear purpose isn't a neutral event — it actively costs trust. Your team will start showing up less prepared, less engaged, and less present. That starts with how you design the meeting.
Foundation
Three Types of Meetings — and Why Mixing Them Kills All Three
Most failed meetings mix three types without labeling which is happening. People don't know whether to listen, think, or vote.
Inform
Share news — no discussion needed
Everyone leaves knowing the same thing. Could often be an email.
Discuss
Work through a problem together
You need their thinking. Multiple perspectives. No single right answer yet.
Decide
Reach a conclusion the team will act on
Everyone leaves knowing what was decided and who owns what next.
Name the type at the start of every meeting. "We're here to decide X" or "I need your thinking on Y." That single sentence prevents most meeting dysfunction.
Foundation
The Cost of a Bad Meeting
A 45-minute team meeting with 8 people = 6 hours of collective time. If that meeting produces no decisions and no new understanding, you just spent 6 hours of your organization.
The standard for running a meeting should be simple: could this have been an email? If yes, make it an email.
Protecting your team's time is a leadership act. Every unnecessary meeting you eliminate sends a message: I value what you're working on more than I value the ritual of gathering.
The Meeting Framework
Four Elements of a Meeting Worth Attending
Every meeting that earns its time has all four of these.
🎯
PurposeWhy are we here?
State it at the start: "We're here to decide X / discuss Y / share Z." Without this, people fill the time differently.
📋
AgendaWhat will we cover?
Shared in advance — even if it's just three bullet points. Lets people prepare instead of react.
⏱️
FacilitationSomeone controls the flow
That's you. Your job is to hold the purpose, manage the time, and bring the group to a conclusion.
✅
CloseWhat was decided? Who owns what?
Before people leave: name the decision, the next step, and who owns it. If you can't do this, the meeting failed.

When the Conversation Drifts
You call a team meeting to discuss a scheduling conflict. Halfway through, the conversation drifts into a discussion about a different process issue.
What do you do?
ALet the conversation flow — if it's relevant, it's worth discussing.
BNote the process issue on a "parking lot" list, stay on the scheduling conflict, and say "let's tackle the process question next week."
CEnd the meeting and reschedule once you have a cleaner agenda.
DLet the team decide which topic to finish first.
The Meeting Framework
The Three-Minute Close
Most meetings end when time runs out, not when work is done. The last three minutes of any meeting should answer three questions:
1. What did we decide?
2. Who's doing what by when?
3. Is there anything important that didn't get discussed?
If you can't answer question one clearly, the meeting failed its purpose.
The three-minute close creates accountability without a follow-up email. Everyone leaves knowing what was decided and who owns what. That's more valuable than another 45 minutes of discussion.
The Meeting Framework
When Meetings Consistently Run Over
Your team meetings consistently run over time. The best fix is:
ASchedule meetings for longer so there's buffer time.
BStart the meeting by naming the agenda and time for each item, then hold to it.
CCut agenda items until the meeting fits the time.
DEnd meetings on time even if the agenda isn't done — discipline will train the team to be more concise.
Practice
When Three People Dominate the Room
Three people dominate your team meetings and the quieter team members rarely speak.
How do you handle this?
AAfter the meeting, privately ask the quiet members for their input.
BIn the meeting, directly invite quieter members: "Jordan, you've been closest to this — what's your read?"
CSend a survey before the meeting so introverts can share their thoughts in writing.
DLet the louder team members lead — they're probably most engaged.
Practice
When Two Team Members Clash in the Meeting
You're running a team meeting and two team members get into a tense disagreement.
What do you do?
ALet them work it out — healthy debate is good.
BTake a side to help resolve it faster.
CAcknowledge the tension, say "I want to come back to this when we've had time to think" and move the agenda forward.
DEnd the meeting early and reconnect with each person separately.
Practice
When the Topic Only Affects Part of the Team
Your team meeting is for the whole team, but a key agenda item really only affects 3 of the 8 people in the room.
What's the best approach?
ARun the meeting as planned — keeping everyone informed is part of the culture.
BMove that agenda item to a smaller working session with only the relevant people.
CInclude it in the agenda but let people step out who it doesn't affect.
DHandle it via email instead.

Course Complete
You've finished Running Team Meetings. The teams that move fastest aren't the ones that meet most — they're the ones whose meetings actually mean something when they happen.

What effective meeting leaders do differently:

Name the purpose of every meeting at the start — decide, discuss, or share
Share the agenda in advance, even if it's just three bullet points
Use a parking lot to capture off-topic ideas without losing the agenda
Close every meeting with: what was decided, who owns what, by when
Cancel or convert to email any meeting that could be one
