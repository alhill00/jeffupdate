The Question That Changes Everything

Liz Wiseman spent years studying two types of leaders. Both were smart. Both were experienced. Both cared about their teams. But they produced radically different results.

Multipliers created environments where people's intelligence expanded. Their teams did their best thinking, solved hard problems, and grew in capability. Diminishers — often without realizing it — created environments where people held back, waited to be told what to do, and played it safe.

The question Wiseman asks: Which one are you?

"Multipliers get at least 2× the capability from their people — not by working them harder, but by using them more fully."

— Liz Wiseman, Multipliers

Multipliers vs. Diminishers

Same situation. Completely different outcomes. The difference isn't IQ — it's how they use the intelligence around them.

Multiplier
Assumes everyone has something to offer
Asks questions instead of giving answers
Creates space for people to think
Challenges people at the edge of their ability
Gives ownership and accountability
Diminisher
Believes they're the smartest in the room
Has the answer before the conversation starts
Dominates the room with their thinking
Micromanages because they know best
Keeps control, creates dependency
2×
Multipliers consistently extract twice the capability from the same people.
Not by adding resources — by changing how they lead.

The Accidental Diminisher

Here's the uncomfortable truth Wiseman surfaces: most diminishers have no idea they're diminishing. They think they're helping. They think they're being a great leader. The behavior that feels supportive from the inside actually shuts down thinking on the outside.

💡
They intend to inspire

The Idea Guy

Always has a new idea. Always excited. Comes in with a new direction every week. The team nods along and quietly stops generating their own ideas — because why bother?

🛠️
They intend to be helpful

The Always-On Leader

Available 24/7. Jumps in fast. Fixes problems before the team gets to struggle with them. The team learns: don't worry, the boss will handle it.

🎯
They intend to drive results

The Pacesetter

Sets a high bar and demonstrates excellence. The problem: when they consistently show how it's done, the team stops trying to figure it out themselves and waits to be shown.

The hard question: Think about the last time you jumped in to solve a problem your team was working on, shared an idea before they'd finished theirs, or answered a question you could have turned back to them. You were being helpful. But what did you take away?

The 5 Disciplines of Multipliers

You don't have to master all five at once. Start with one.

Discipline 1
The Talent Magnet
They find and use everyone's genius
What it looks like

Multipliers see capability in people that even those people can't see in themselves. They ask: "What are you uniquely brilliant at?" — and then they put people in positions that use that brilliance. They grow the talent pool by making people feel capable.

"
People don't just work for Multipliers. They grow under them.

Try This Week

Name one person on your team whose best skill isn't in their job description. Find one way to use it this week.

Discipline 2
The Liberator
They create space that requires your best
What it looks like

Liberators create an intense environment — not a relaxed one. But the intensity comes from the standard, not from fear. They give people room to think, make mistakes, and recover. When they speak, they ask: they don't tell. The implicit message: your thinking matters here.

"
They create both safety and demand — a combination that brings out extraordinary thinking.

Try This Week

In your next team meeting, ask a question and let silence sit for 10 full seconds before adding your own thinking. See what emerges.

Discipline 3
The Challenger
They stretch people past what they think is possible
What it looks like

Challengers don't hand solutions to their team — they hand them problems. Hard ones. Ones that require real thinking. They seed ideas and create space for the team to find the path. The result: people rise to the challenge because someone believed they could.

"
They don't give people the answer. They give them the question — and then believe they'll find it.

Try This Week

When someone brings you a problem, answer with: "What do you think?" and mean it. Don't follow it with your own answer.

Discipline 4
The Debate Maker
They drive decisions through real debate
What it looks like

Diminishers make decisions in their heads and announce them. Multipliers create a rigorous debate process where the best thinking wins — not the loudest voice or the highest title. The team may not always agree with the outcome, but they own it, because they were in it.

"
The best decisions don't come from the smartest person in the room. They come from the best conversation in the room.

Try This Week

Before your next significant decision, ask the team: "What am I missing?" and let the answers change your thinking.

Discipline 5
The Investor
They invest in others to get results — not do the work for them
What it looks like

Multipliers give ownership, not tasks. They define what success looks like and then step back — providing resources and coaching, not oversight. They create leaders under them, not followers. When they leave a project, it runs better than when they were in it.

"
The goal isn't to be needed. It's to build a team that doesn't need you.

Try This Week

Pick one thing you're currently doing that someone on your team could own. Hand it off this week — fully, with the context and the authority to make decisions.

Self-Check

Which type of leader showed up in your last team meeting?

Your team is working through a difficult problem. Halfway through the discussion, you have a clear idea of the answer. You can tell the conversation is going to take another 20 minutes if left to run. What do you do?
The Answer
B — Ask a question that guides without replacing

Option A is the Diminisher move — it's efficient in the short term but signals "your thinking process isn't worth the time." Over time, your team stops engaging deeply because they know you'll land the plane anyway. Option C is worse — it creates a rescue dynamic. Option D is admirable but sometimes impractical. Option B is the Multiplier move: you use your knowledge to accelerate the team's thinking, not replace it. They arrive at the answer. They own it. That's worth the extra 5 minutes every time.
