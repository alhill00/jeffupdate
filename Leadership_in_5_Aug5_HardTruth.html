How to Tell a Hard Truth

Difficult conversations that build trust

“

Kindness without honesty
isn't kindness.

It's conflict avoidance
wearing a mask.

Hard conversations avoided become bigger problems compounded. The feedback you don't give someone is a debt you'll pay later — usually with interest. And the longer you wait, the harder it gets and the more trust you quietly lose.

The goal isn't to be brutal. It's to be real — with care, with clarity, and with respect for the person across from you.

The Tool

The 5-Step Truth Frame
 

Step 1

Choose the right moment.

Private. Not in the heat of it. Not in front of others. Give them — and yourself — the conditions to actually hear each other.
 

Step 2

Lead with your intent.

"I'm telling you this because I want you to succeed here." That sentence changes the entire frame of what follows. It makes the conversation a gift, not an attack.
 

Step 3

Be specific about what you observed — not what you concluded.

"In the last two team meetings, I noticed you interrupted two colleagues before they finished" is specific. "You don't respect the team" is a conclusion. Start with the fact. Let the meaning emerge together.
 

Step 4

Make space for their perspective.

"What's your read on that?" You might be missing context. More often, they're missing awareness. Either way, you can't get to a real solution without hearing their side.
 

Step 5

Leave with a clear next step, agreed on together.

Don't let the conversation end in vague goodwill. Name one specific change, by when, and how you'll both know it's working.

Sound Familiar?

Someone on your team has a habit that's affecting others — consistently late to briefings, dismissive in group settings, missing handoffs. You've noticed it. Others have noticed it. No one has said anything.

Every week you don't say something, the behavior becomes more normal — and harder to address later. The kindest thing you can do is tell the truth, early, before the damage is done.
