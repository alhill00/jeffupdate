Foundation
The Manager Who Did Everything Herself
When Maya got promoted to manager, she kept doing what had always made her successful — the work itself. She was fast, accurate, and high-quality. Her team appreciated that nothing slipped through the cracks. She felt in control.
Six months later, she was exhausted. Her team was underutilized. Nothing moved when she was out. Projects piled up on her desk alone. Her team had capable people — but they had stopped developing because Maya was always the one doing it.
The lesson Maya eventually learned: she had confused "doing good work" with "managing." They are not the same thing.
Delegating isn't losing control — it's multiplying your capacity. The manager who does everything has a ceiling. The manager who delegates effectively keeps raising it.
Foundation
Doer vs. Multiplier
Most new managers default to Doer mode — it's what got them promoted. The shift to Multiplier is the actual job of management.

The Doer

  • Handles work themselves
  • Team is underutilized
  • Creates a bottleneck
  • Ceiling = personal output
Fast in the moment. Limits the team long-term.

The Multiplier

  • Assigns work strategically
  • Builds team capability
  • Scales impact
  • Ceiling keeps rising
Slower upfront. Compounds over time.
The question isn't whether you can do it better. It's whether someone else can do it well enough — and grow in the process.

Why Managers Don't Delegate
Every manager has heard of delegation. Most still don't do it enough. Here's why — and why these reasons, while valid, are also barriers.
1
"I can do it faster myself."
True — this time. But next time, and the time after that, you'll still be the one doing it.
2
"I don't trust them yet."
Trust is built through experience. They won't earn your trust without the opportunity to try.
3
"If it fails, it reflects on me."
Yes — and your job is to set them up well enough that it doesn't. That's the manager's role.
4
"I actually like this work."
Completely understandable. Also the most honest reason — and the one that's hardest to argue with. But liking the work doesn't make it yours to keep.
The antidote isn't pretending these feelings aren't real. It's having a process that addresses each one — which is exactly what the Delegation Matrix gives you.
The Delegation Matrix
Match the Task to the Approach
Not every task should be delegated the same way. The Delegation Matrix helps you decide your approach based on two variables: how complex the task is, and how ready your Team Member is.
High Complexity + Low Readiness
Do It Yourself
Or coach side-by-side. Don't delegate complex work to someone not yet ready — it sets them up to fail.
High Complexity + High Readiness
Brief and Trust
Delegate with one scheduled check-in. Set the standard clearly, then get out of the way.
Low Complexity + Low Readiness
Coach Through It
Assign with heavy guidance. Use it as a development opportunity — they learn, you get it done.
Low Complexity + High Readiness
Assign and Release
Fully hand off with minimal check-in. This is the clearest delegation opportunity — take it.
The most common mistake: treating all tasks and all people the same. Match your approach to the quadrant — not your comfort level.

The Four Delegation Moves
Deciding to delegate is only the first step. How you delegate determines whether it works.
1
Choose the right person
Skill match AND capacity match. A capable person with no bandwidth will fail just as predictably as someone without the skill.
2
Brief fully
Not just what to do — but why it matters and what "done right" looks like. Context creates ownership. Without the why, they're executing a task. With it, they're solving a problem.
3
Grant authority
They need access, permission, and visibility to actually do it. Delegating without authority is setting someone up to fail before they start.
4
Check in once, then trust
Schedule one touchpoint. That's accountability — not hovering. After the check-in, trust the process.
The Delegation Matrix
Scenario: The Right Assignment
Raya has been on your team for 3 years and has handled similar projects before. You're swamped, and a new reporting deliverable is 70% in her wheelhouse. What do you do?
ADo it yourself — it's too important to risk on someone else.
BDelegate to Raya with a full briefing and one midpoint check-in.
CAsk Raya to shadow you while you do it so she learns for next time.
DDeprioritize the deliverable — you don't have the bandwidth either way.
The Delegation Matrix
Scenario: When the Work Needs Rework
You delegated a project to Jordan, a newer Team Member. At the check-in, the work needs significant rework — Jordan missed key requirements. What's your next move?
ATake the project back and finish it yourself — it'll be faster.
BCoach Jordan through what needs to change and why — let him fix it.
CGive Jordan a complete redo with fully rewritten instructions.
DMark it as done and lower your standards this one time.

Scenario: The Task You've Always Done
You have a recurring task you've done for 2 years — monthly data reporting, about 3 hours, well-documented. Your Team Member Sam has asked for more operational responsibility. What's the best move?
AKeep doing it yourself — you know it best and it's efficient.
BDelegate to Sam with clear docs, then review the first output together.
CWait until Sam specifically requests this exact task before delegating.
DSplit it — Sam does half, you do half, for now.
Practice
Scenario: Missing Authority
You want to delegate a task to a Team Member, but you realize they won't have the system access needed to complete it. What do you do first?
ADelegate anyway and see if they can figure out the access themselves.
BEnsure access is in place before briefing them on the task.
CDo it yourself this time and plan to properly delegate it next cycle.
DAsk them to request access themselves without giving them the full context yet.
Practice
Scenario: Different Method, Same Result
Your Team Member completes a delegated task. The results meet the standard — but they did it completely differently than you would have. What's the right call?
ARedo the work your way before it goes out — consistency matters.
BAccept the output — different method, standard met, move on.
CHave a conversation about doing it your way next time.
DReduce how much you delegate to this person going forward.
