Foundation
Culture Is Not the Values on the Wall
Walk into almost any organization and you'll find a culture statement somewhere — on a poster, in the annual report, at the bottom of a slide deck. It describes the culture leadership aspires to. It rarely describes the culture that actually exists.
Culture, in practice, is the answer to one question: How do things actually get done here? It's what people do when no one is watching. It's what gets rewarded and what gets tolerated. It's what new hires learn in their first 90 days — not from orientation, but from observation.
The gap between stated culture and lived culture is the most important gap a VP can close. And closing it isn't a values exercise. It's a design problem.
The architecture insight: Culture is not built by declaring values. It is built by designing the systems, stories, incentives, and behaviors that make certain ways of working natural — and others unlikely.
Foundation
The Gap Between Stated and Lived Culture
Most culture failures aren't failures of intent. Leaders genuinely believe in the values they declare. The failure is a design failure — the systems, incentives, and day-to-day behaviors that surround Team Members don't reinforce the stated values. They contradict them.

Stated Culture

  • "We value transparency"
  • "We develop our people"
  • "We act with urgency"
  • "We put the Guest first"

What the System Says

  • Bad news gets punished
  • Promotions go to top performers regardless of leadership
  • Decisions take three approval layers
  • Internal metrics are the real scorecard
The rule: When stated culture and system design conflict, the system wins. Every time. Culture change that doesn't redesign the system is aspirational at best.
Foundation
Culture Change Is Self-Reinforcing
One reason culture change feels impossible is that culture is self-reinforcing. The behaviors that are rewarded attract more of those behaviors. The stories that get told teach everyone else how to succeed. The norms that go unchallenged become the rules everyone follows without being told.
This is bad news if you inherited a culture you want to change. It's good news once you understand that the same self-reinforcing loop works in your favor when you intervene thoughtfully.
The self-reinforcing loop: Incentives shape behavior → Behavior generates stories → Stories teach norms → Norms shape what gets tolerated → Tolerance signals what incentives really reward → back to start.
VPs who change culture interrupt this loop at multiple points simultaneously. Changing only one element — say, the stated values — leaves the rest of the loop intact. Change the system and the tolerance and the stories, and the loop starts working for you.
The Architecture Model
Five Culture Architecture Levers
Culture doesn't change through declarations. It changes through five specific levers that VPs control — and can redesign.
Lever 1
Incentive Design
Diagnostic: What behaviors does your performance and recognition system actually reward — not what it claims to reward?
Lever 2
Story Architecture
Diagnostic: What stories does leadership tell, repeat, and celebrate? Who are the heroes in those stories, and what did they do?
Lever 3
System Design
Diagnostic: What does your process make easy versus hard? What decisions require approval that shouldn't? Where does urgency go to die?
Lever 4
Tolerance Calibration
Diagnostic: What behaviors continue unchecked in your org? What norm violations has leadership let slide — and what has that signaled?
Lever 5
Norm Setting Through Behavior
Diagnostic: What does your own behavior model? Your Team Members study how you act in pressure, in conflict, in recognition — and replicate it.
Foundation
The Lag Effect — Why Culture Change Feels Invisible
Here is the reason most culture change efforts fail or get abandoned: the results of culture investment are invisible for 12 to 18 months. You change an incentive system in January. You don't see the behavior shift reliably until Q3 or Q4. Leaders conclude the change didn't work. They reverse course. The culture learns that announced changes don't stick.
The lag effect is not a sign of failure. It is the natural rhythm of system change. What you do today becomes visible in the culture well after you've moved on to the next priority.
The critical corollary: The first behavior you let slide after announcing a new norm tells the org the real rule. Not your words — your tolerance. If you announce a culture of direct feedback and then say nothing when a senior leader bulldozes someone in a meeting, the culture has received its real instruction.
Change the system before waiting for the behavior. Then protect the norm through what you tolerate — or refuse to.
The Architecture Model
When the Incentive System Contradicts the Culture
Your org has a stated value of "Team Member development." In your quarterly talent review, you realize your recognition program has awarded only individual contributors who hit personal metrics — no recognition has gone to managers who developed their teams, even when team outcomes were exceptional. Your director layer has noticed.
What's the right VP move?
AAcknowledge the gap in the next all-hands and reaffirm your commitment to development as a value.
BRedesign the recognition criteria to explicitly reward development outcomes — and make the first award visible and meaningful.
CAsk HR to add a development metric to performance reviews and monitor it next cycle.
DSend a note to your director layer reminding them that development is a priority and to track it more deliberately.
The Architecture Model
The High Performer Who Is a Culture Tax
One of your highest-performing directors consistently hits their numbers. They're also consistently described by their direct reports as dismissive, siloing information, and competing with peers rather than collaborating. Your other directors have started behaving more territorially. You believe it's because they're following the model they see rewarded.
What do you do?
ACoach the director privately but leave their role and recognition status unchanged — performance results are hard to argue with.
BAddress the behavior directly and make clear it is part of how you evaluate leadership — separate from individual results. Make the standard explicit to your whole director layer.
CTalk to the other directors about not modeling the behavior they observe — each leader is responsible for their own choices.
DMove the director to a role with fewer people dependencies so the culture tax is minimized.
Practice
Designing a Recognition Moment That Transmits Signal
You want to use an upcoming all-hands to recognize a Team Member in a way that deliberately transmits a culture message — specifically around the value of helping others succeed at their own expense. Which recognition approach sends the clearest signal?
ARecognize the top performer of the quarter by name and result — this sends a clear message about what outcomes matter.
BTell a specific story: name the Team Member, describe the moment they gave up something to help a colleague succeed, and explain why that behavior is exactly what you're building toward.
CDo a team-level recognition so no one feels singled out, but make the category about collaboration.
DAdd a "culture champion" award category and let the team nominate each other — participation builds buy-in.
Practice
Two VPs With Different Standards
You and a peer VP lead adjacent organizations. Your peer publicly tolerates behaviors you've specifically worked to change in your org — interrupting in meetings, dismissing input from front-line leaders, making unilateral decisions that affect shared Team Members. Your own Team Members are noticing the difference and asking why standards differ across VPs.
What's the right approach?
AReinforce your own standards clearly with your org and let the peer VP manage their own culture — you're only accountable for yours.
BRaise the inconsistency with your peer VP directly — not as a complaint, but as a shared leadership standard issue that affects both of your organizations.
CEscalate to the CPO that peer VP standards are misaligned — this is a leadership team coherence issue.
DTell your Team Members that different leaders have different styles and help them understand that your org's norms are what they should follow.
Practice
Making a Change That Goes Against Past Norms You Set
You've decided to change a norm you yourself established two years ago — specifically around how decisions get escalated to you. You previously encouraged everything to come up. You now want your directors making more of those calls themselves. Team Members are confused. Some are resisting. A few directors aren't sure they have your backing.
What do you do first?
AAnnounce the new expectation clearly in a team meeting and begin holding directors accountable immediately.
BName the shift explicitly — acknowledge that you're changing what you previously modeled, explain why, and confirm that directors have your backing when they make the call and it doesn't work out perfectly.
CLet the new norm emerge organically — you'll start redirecting decisions back to directors without making a big announcement.
DDocument the new decision rights framework and share it as an org chart update so it's official.
