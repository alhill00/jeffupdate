Foundation
You Are Building a Narrative Now
At VP level, the communication job has changed. You are no longer primarily informing your team about what's happening. You are shaping how an entire organization understands its work — what it believes its purpose is, where it believes it's headed, and what it believes matters.
This is organizational narrative. It determines whether Team Members feel like they're part of something with direction and meaning — or executing tasks in a vacuum. The VP who builds narrative intentionally creates alignment that multiplies effort. The VP who doesn't creates an org that narrates itself, usually less accurately and more anxiously than reality warrants.
Three shifts that define VP communication: Informing your team → Building organizational narrative. Speaking directly → Transmitting through managers. Reactive to questions → Proactively shaping understanding before questions form.
Foundation
What Silence Costs at Scale
The VP communication failure most often isn't bad communication. It's absent communication. When a VP goes silent — between announcements, during uncertainty, when they're focused on other things — the org doesn't stay neutral. It fills the vacuum with the most available interpretation, which is almost always the most anxious one.

What Silence Produces

  • Worst-case speculation spreads laterally
  • Managers improvise direction without context
  • Team Members read absence as disengagement
  • Trust erodes without a single incident to name
  • By the time VP communicates, the narrative must be corrected rather than built

What Proactive Communication Produces

  • VP controls the narrative before vacuum forms
  • Managers have context to represent VP intent accurately
  • Team Members experience active VP leadership even in steady-state periods
  • Uncertainty lands differently when direction exists

How to Write at VP Scale
VP-level written communication carries weight that manager-level communication doesn't. Every org-wide message, every quarterly letter, every strategic update is read more carefully and interpreted more broadly than the VP typically expects. Three rules for writing at scale:
Rule 1: Every sentence earns its place. Long VP messages get skimmed. Important content buried in paragraph six doesn't exist. Write shorter than you think you need to. Every sentence should either advance the narrative, provide essential context, or ask something of the reader.
Rule 2: Write to the most skeptical reader. Your message will be read by people who are skeptical, anxious, or looking for contradictions. If a sentence can be misread, it will be. Read your draft as someone who doesn't trust the framing yet.
Rule 3: The first sentence determines if it gets read. If the opening is generic ("I'm excited to share..."), you've already lost the reader's best attention. Start with the most important thing.
The VP Message Architecture
VP Communication System
Effective VP communication isn't a series of good individual messages — it's a system that operates predictably, consistently, and bidirectionally.
Element 1
Core Narrative
3–5 consistent themes repeated in every channel, every quarter. The org learns what you stand for through repetition. If your themes change every quarter, the org has no narrative — only updates.
Element 2
Communication Cadence
Predictable rhythm: monthly all-hands, quarterly letters, weekly signal of some kind. Predictability reduces anxiety — the org knows when VP communication is coming and doesn't fill the gap with speculation.
Element 3
Bidirectional Loops
Skip-levels, pulse surveys, town halls with open Q&A, trusted interpreters. VP communication that has no honest return channel loses contact with reality. The loop is what keeps the narrative accurate.
Element 4
Manager Enablement
Brief your director layer before major announcements. If they learn from the same message as the rest of the org, they cannot support it accurately. Pre-briefed managers are your communication multiplier.

The VP Communication Failure Patterns
Three VP communication failure patterns that are common enough to name specifically:

Information Without Narrative

  • Updates that describe what's happening without connecting to meaning or direction
  • Org receives data but not interpretation
  • Team Members are informed but not aligned

Volume Without Cadence

  • Irregular bursts of communication
  • Periods of silence followed by floods
  • Org can't build rhythmic understanding — it's reactive

One-Directional Without Loop

  • VP sends messages but has no reliable mechanism for accurate signal to return
  • Communication becomes increasingly detached from what Team Members actually experience
  • VP is surprised by engagement data, survey results, or retention patterns they didn't anticipate
The VP Message Architecture
Communicating a Strategy Pivot Org-Wide
You need to communicate a significant strategy pivot to your organization — one that changes priorities many people have been working toward. The pivot is well-reasoned and has executive alignment. Your concern is that the message lands as credible and energizing rather than disorienting or trust-damaging.
What's your communication architecture for this?
ASend an all-hands communication to the full org simultaneously — directness and transparency are more important than sequencing in a high-trust situation.
BBrief the director layer first with full context and Q&A, then communicate to the org with the core narrative — ensuring managers can support the message before it lands.
CLet the director layer communicate the pivot to their teams in their own words — it will land better in smaller, more relational contexts.
DSend the communication simultaneously to the org and directors — treating everyone equally signals respect for the whole organization.
The VP Message Architecture
Message Badly Misinterpreted Through the Chain
You sent a message about resource prioritization that was intended to clarify strategic focus. It has been interpreted by many Team Members as a signal that a major restructure is imminent. Several directors communicated it in ways that amplified the anxiety. Your trusted interpreters are telling you the misinterpretation is widespread.
What's your first move?
ASend a correction directly to all Team Members clarifying your original intent — going direct is faster and prevents further distortion through the management layer.
BCorrect the message directly to the org and simultaneously debrief with directors on what amplified the anxiety — so you fix the current misread and prevent future distortion.
CLet directors correct the misinterpretation in their teams — they know their people and can address the anxiety in the right context.
DWait and observe — correcting the message immediately may amplify the anxiety by drawing more attention to the misread.
Practice
Quarterly All-Hands That Produces Energy, Not Info Overload
You're designing your quarterly all-hands. Last quarter's session was described in survey feedback as "information-heavy" and "I'm not sure what we're supposed to do differently after." You want this one to produce genuine alignment and energy, not just information transfer.
Which design principle guides the session?
AIncrease the amount of content to be more comprehensive — "information-heavy" feedback means they wanted more context, not less.
BDesign around 2–3 core narrative themes — everything in the session reinforces those themes, with time for genuine Q&A and a clear "what changes for you" takeaway.
CShift from VP presentation to panel format — variety in speakers reduces information fatigue.
DCut the all-hands to 30 minutes — brevity signals respect for the team's time and forces prioritization.
Practice
Communicating a Difficult Org-Wide Message
You need to communicate an org-wide message that involves a significant reduction in headcount in one part of the organization. You've been asked by the CEO to communicate it as a "strategic realignment." You believe that framing is accurate but also incomplete — the human cost is real and the org will see it.
How do you draft the communication?
AUse the "strategic realignment" framing as directed — executive alignment on messaging is important and the framing is technically accurate.
BUse the strategic framing and explicitly acknowledge the human cost — both are true, and acknowledging only one damages credibility with people who can see the other.
CLead entirely with the human impact — Teams Members will respond better if they feel leadership prioritizes people over strategy.
DHave HR draft the message — they're best positioned to navigate the sensitive human dimensions of this communication.
Practice
Building a Communication Rhythm When There Isn't One
You've realized your communication with your organization has been largely reactive — you communicate when something important happens, but there's no predictable cadence. Team Members sometimes hear about significant org developments informally before they hear from you. You want to change this.
What's the right first step?
ASend an all-hands immediately to demonstrate active communication — breaking the reactive pattern is most important, content can develop over time.
BDesign the cadence first: decide on monthly all-hands, a quarterly letter, and a weekly signal — then commit to the rhythm publicly so the org can predict when VP communication will come.
CAsk your director layer to communicate on a more consistent schedule — the cadence issue is most visible at the manager level, not the VP level.
DIncrease the volume of communications you send — more messages will naturally create the perception of regularity over time.
