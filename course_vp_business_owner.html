Foundation
Two Ways to Hold a VP Seat
There are two distinct versions of VP leadership, and they produce very different organizational outcomes.

Senior Management Mode

  • Executing well within the plan
  • Reporting accurately on activities
  • Supporting strategy set by others
  • Staying in functional lane

Business Ownership Mode

  • Deeply accountable for outcomes
  • Proactively making calls when needed
  • Treating the function like it's yours to build and protect
  • Taking positions beyond functional lane
The distinction matters: Senior managers wait for the strategy. Owners help shape it, pressure-test it, and hold themselves accountable for executing it — even when it's hard.
Foundation
What Business Ownership Actually Means
Operating as a business owner at the VP level isn't a mindset shift — it's a behavior shift. Three behaviors signal the difference:
1
Proactive Risk Surfacing
Owners raise risks before they're asked. They don't wait for the quarterly review to say "we have a problem." They surface it when they first see it — with a recommended path.
2
Resource Calls Based on Outcomes
Owners treat budget and headcount as investments, not entitlements. They make resource calls based on what produces outcomes — not what's politically safe or historically allocated.
3
Taking Positions Beyond Your Lane
Owners speak up on business questions outside their functional lane when they have a relevant perspective. They don't stay quiet to avoid conflict. They share the view and let the right person decide.
Foundation
Three Things That Stop VPs From Operating as Owners
These three barriers are real. They're also the reason many VPs plateau before reaching C-suite.
1
Functional Tunnel Vision
Deep expertise in your function creates a frame that makes everything look like a functional problem. Owners zoom out. They ask what the business needs — not just what their function is good at delivering.
2
Risk Aversion on Positions Outside Your Lane
Speaking up on questions outside your function feels risky — you might be wrong, you might overstep, someone might push back. Owners speak anyway, with appropriate humility and data.
3
Short-Term Pressure
Quarterly metrics, immediate deliverables, and visible urgency crowd out the long-horizon thinking that business owners do. Owners protect time for the 12-24 month view, even when the 30-day view is loud.
The Owner Framework
Four Ownership Behaviors
Business ownership at the VP level shows up in four specific behavior categories. Rate yourself honestly on each.
1
Proactive Accountability
Surface risks before asked. Own outcomes regardless of where the problem originates. Don't wait to be caught — bring the issue with a recommended path.
2
Resource Stewardship
Treat budget, headcount, and time as investments — not entitlements. Make resource calls that protect what's most strategic, not what's most visible or politically safe.
3
Cross-Functional Judgment
Take positions on business questions beyond your function when you have a relevant perspective. The VP who stays quiet outside their lane is perceived as functional, not executive.
4
Long-Horizon Thinking
Decisions that optimize for 12-24 months, not just the next quarter. Owners ask: what does this decision create or foreclose two years from now?
Foundation
What Owners Do That Managers Don't
The difference between business ownership and senior management becomes most visible in three specific situations.

Manager Response

  • Waits to be asked about the risk
  • Defends the budget as allocated
  • Stays quiet outside functional domain
  • Optimizes for this quarter's metrics

Owner Response

  • Surfaces the risk proactively with a path
  • Reallocates resources to protect what matters most
  • Offers the cross-functional perspective
  • Asks what this decision creates in 18 months
The ceiling: VPs who operate in manager mode consistently will plateau at director-plus. Business owners are the ones the C-suite selects from. The behaviors are learnable — but they require a deliberate shift.
The Owner Framework
Problem Outside Your Lane
In a cross-functional meeting, you observe a dynamic in another team's work that you believe is creating significant Guest experience risk. It's not your function. Raising it will likely create friction with a peer VP. What do you do?
AStay quiet — it's not your function and raising it will create unnecessary conflict.
BRaise it in the meeting: "I want to flag something I'm seeing that I think creates risk for the Guest experience. I could be missing context — here's what I'm observing."
CBring it to your CEO privately so they can address it without you taking the political hit.
DEmail the peer VP after the meeting with your concern so it's documented but low-conflict.
The Owner Framework
Budget Shortfall
You're told mid-year that your function needs to cut 15% of its budget. You have the discretion to decide where the cuts come from. What's your ownership approach?
ACut evenly across all team budgets to distribute the pain fairly and avoid political conflict.
BIdentify which investments most directly drive strategic outcomes and protect those — cut harder in areas of lower strategic impact, even if that's politically harder.
CPush back on the cut entirely — accept no reduction unless the business case is made to your satisfaction.
DCut the newest or lowest-visibility programs first, since they have the fewest stakeholders to disappoint.
Practice
Short-Term Right, Long-Term Wrong
A decision is on the table that would hit this quarter's numbers but would create significant capability debt over the next 18 months — deprioritizing a development investment that your team needs. Your CEO is leaning toward the short-term move.
AAgree with the CEO — they have broader context and you don't want to appear to prioritize your function's interests.
BShare your long-horizon concern directly: "I want to flag the 18-month implication of this before we decide. Here's what I think it costs us." Let them decide with the full picture.
CAgree in the meeting, then quietly preserve the development investment by reallocating budget without disclosure.
DDefer the decision by asking for a 30-day analysis period to build the business case.
Practice
Owning What Isn't Technically Yours
A Guest experience failure occurred because of a breakdown between your function and an adjacent team. Technically the failure originated in the other team's process. Your CEO asks what happened.
A"The breakdown was in the other team's process — our part worked as designed. They need to fix their side."
B"There was a handoff failure between our teams. I own the coordination that should have prevented this — here's what I'm doing to make sure it doesn't happen again."
CAsk the other VP to take the lead on the explanation since the failure originated there.
DProvide a detailed technical explanation of exactly where the breakdown occurred and whose system was responsible.
Practice
Outcome vs. Activity
Your function has completed every planned initiative this year — on time, within budget. But the strategic outcome those initiatives were supposed to produce hasn't materialized. In your end-of-year review, what's your business owner stance?
APresent the activity record — every initiative delivered as planned. The outcome gap is a broader business problem.
BOwn the outcome gap directly: "We executed our plan. The outcome we were after didn't materialize — and that's on me to understand and fix. Here's my diagnosis and what I'm changing."
CAttribute the outcome gap to external factors — market conditions, dependencies on other teams, resource constraints.
DRequest a new set of success metrics that better reflect what your function can control.
