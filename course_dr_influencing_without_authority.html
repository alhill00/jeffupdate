Foundation
The Director's Real Leverage
At the director level, the most important work crosses functional lines. A director who can only drive results within their own team has a permanent ceiling.
A director who can shape decisions, align stakeholders, and move cross-functional initiatives forward operates at executive scale — regardless of their title. The org chart tells you who reports to you. It doesn't tell you how far your impact actually reaches.
The threshold question: Can you get things done when no one has to listen to you? That answer defines your leadership range more than your job description does.
Foundation
Why Directors Underestimate This Skill
Most promotion criteria reward expertise and team delivery. Influencing without authority is rarely measured explicitly — but it's what separates directors who stay at director and directors who move to VP.
It shows up in whether you can get things done when no one has to listen to you. Whether a peer responds to your request or ignores it. Whether an executive takes your recommendation seriously before you have a track record with them.
The gap: Directors who rely only on their formal authority hit a ceiling the moment they need something from someone who doesn't report to them. At director level, that's most of the important work.
Foundation
The Myth of the Memo
Many leaders believe that if they present a logical, well-reasoned proposal, the org will align behind it. Build a good enough deck and the stakeholders will come around.
The org doesn't run on logic — it runs on trust, relationships, and perceived shared interest. The proposal matters. The relationship that makes the proposal land matters more. You can be right and still lose the room.
The reframe: Before asking "how do I make a better case?" ask "do I have the relationship and trust to make any case land with this person?"
The Influence Model
Five Principles of Influence Without Authority
These aren't manipulation tactics — they're the foundation of how durable cross-functional influence actually works.
1
Understand Their Interests
Not just their positions. What do they actually care about? What are they measured on? What would a win look like for them?
2
Connect Your Ask to Their Priorities
Frame the proposal as helping them, not just you. Show how your initiative advances something they already care about.
3
Build Trust Before You Need It
Relationship deposits before withdrawal. Credibility is earned in the quiet moments — not invented in the moment you need alignment.
4
Use Coalition
Who else believes this? Who has credibility with this stakeholder? A peer endorsement is often more persuasive than a direct ask.
5
Know Your Alternatives (BATNA)
If they say no, what's your path? Knowing your Best Alternative to Negotiated Agreement keeps you from over-investing in any single stakeholder.
Foundation
Persuading vs. Pressuring
Pressure moves people temporarily — they comply because they have to. Persuasion moves people durably — they agree because they want to. At the director level, pressure (escalation, urgency, rank) is available but expensive. Each use draws down your relational account.
Persuasion is slower to build and more valuable. The question is always: do I need compliance or commitment? Compliance gets the task done once. Commitment gets you a partner for the next five initiatives.
The practical test: If you removed the urgency or the authority, would this person still say yes? If the answer is no, you have compliance — not influence.
The Influence Model
The Resistant Peer
You need alignment from a peer director on a shared initiative. You've sent two emails with no response. You're on a tight timeline. What's your move?
AEscalate to your shared leader — you've tried, they're not engaging, and you need a decision.
BRequest a 20-minute conversation to understand what's blocking them — find out their actual concern before pushing harder.
CMove forward without their alignment and document that you tried to include them.
DAsk a mutual colleague to advocate on your behalf before trying again directly.
The Influence Model
Building a Coalition
You have a proposal your leader is initially skeptical of. Before presenting it formally, you want to build enough organizational support that it gains momentum. What's the right approach?
ADon't pre-sell it — let the strength of the proposal speak for itself in the formal presentation.
BIdentify two or three peers who share the problem this solves, get their input, and ask if they'd be willing to co-present or voice support.
CPresent it to your leader's peers first so it arrives with peer-level endorsement before your leader sees it.
DGather data to strengthen the proposal — if the numbers are compelling enough, skepticism won't matter.
Practice
Disagreeing with an Executive
In a leadership meeting, an executive proposes a direction you believe is a mistake. You have data and experience that supports a different path. What do you do?
AStay quiet in the room and raise your concern privately afterward — public disagreement is too risky.
BAsk a clarifying question that surfaces the tension without making it adversarial: "Help me understand how this addresses [the concern you're seeing]."
CPresent your alternative directly: "I'd push back on this — here's what I'm seeing and what I'd suggest instead."
DAgree in the room, then build opposition to the decision through backchannels.
Practice
The Stakeholder Who Won't Engage
A key stakeholder keeps declining meeting invites and doesn't respond to your requests. Your initiative depends on their input. You've been patient. What's the next move?
AKeep sending meeting invites — eventually they'll have to respond.
BFind out what's actually driving the non-engagement — ask a trusted mutual connection what's going on before trying again.
CEscalate immediately — their non-engagement is blocking the initiative.
DReframe your ask entirely — send a one-question email that takes 90 seconds to answer instead of requesting a meeting.
Practice
Recovering After a Failed Attempt
You pushed hard for a cross-functional initiative, it didn't get approved, and the stakeholder relationship feels strained. How do you recover your influence with that person?
AGive it time — the relationship will normalize on its own after the situation fades.
BFind a low-stakes way to be genuinely helpful to them in the near term — rebuild the relational account before your next ask.
CAcknowledge directly that you pushed hard and that you want to make sure the relationship is solid regardless of the outcome.
DB and C — acknowledge the push AND find a way to be useful before you need anything from them again.

Course Complete
You've finished Influencing Without Authority. The directors who shape organizations aren't always the most senior ones — they're the ones who make things happen across lines that don't report to them.

Commitments for directors who lead across the org:

Before your next cross-functional ask, understand their interests first — not just their position.
Build the relationship before you need the favor — invest in stakeholders when nothing is at stake.
Know what compliance vs. commitment looks like for this situation — and be honest about which you're getting.
Identify one person in your network who can expand your coalition on a current initiative.
After any failed influence attempt, diagnose what happened before trying again — was it the relationship, the framing, or the timing?
