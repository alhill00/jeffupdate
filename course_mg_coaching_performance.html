Foundation
The Manager Who Always Had the Answer
When Marcus got promoted to manager, he was excited to finally be in a position to help. Every time someone on his team came to him with a problem, he solved it — fast, confidently, and well. His team appreciated it. He felt useful. It seemed like good management.
Six months later, something was wrong. His team couldn't make a decision without him. They'd stop by his desk a dozen times a day. They were capable people — but they'd stopped trusting their own judgment.
Marcus hadn't failed as a manager. He'd just been answering when he should have been asking.
Coaching for performance isn't about fixing your team's problems. It's about asking the questions that help them fix their own — and build the capability to handle the next one.
Foundation
Two Modes. One Skill.
When a team member comes to you with a problem, you have two options. Most managers default to the first one without thinking about it.
Telling Mode

You have the answer

  • You listen
  • You diagnose
  • You give the answer
Fast. Feels helpful. Creates dependency. They come back with the same problem next month.
Coaching Mode

They find the answer

  • You listen
  • You ask
  • They work it through
Slower upfront. Builds capability. Next time, they solve it themselves.
Neither mode is always right. The skill is knowing which to use — and coaching more often than you think.
Foundation
The Long Game
Every time you answer instead of coach, you solve one problem. Every time you coach instead of answer, you multiply your capacity as a leader.
When You Tell
  • Problem solved — once
  • Team member returns next time with the same type of problem
  • You become the bottleneck
  • Your team's ceiling is your availability
When You Coach
  • Problem solved — and they learned the process
  • Next time, they figure it out themselves
  • You free up capacity
  • Your team's ceiling keeps rising
The trap: Telling feels more productive in the moment. Coaching feels slow. But telling is borrowing time from the future — every answer you give creates another question tomorrow.
The GROW Model
A Framework for Every Coaching Conversation
The GROW model gives you a simple structure to follow. You don't have to say the letters out loud — it's a thinking tool, not a script.
G
GoalWhere do we want to get to?
"What would a good outcome look like for you?"
R
RealityWhere are we right now?
"What's happening right now? What have you tried?"
O
OptionsWhat could we do?
"What could you do? What else?"
W
Way ForwardWhat will we actually do?
"What will you do? When? What do you need from me?"
Key principle: Don't skip Goal. Most managers jump straight to Options — brainstorming solutions before both people agree on what success looks like. Start with Goal every time.
Foundation
Coach It or Answer It?
Alex, one of your direct reports, stops you in the hallway: "Hey — I've been going back and forth with a vendor and I'm not sure how to handle their latest pushback. Do you have a minute?"
What's the right call?
AGive Alex specific advice on how to handle vendor pushback — you've dealt with this before and can help fast.
BAsk Alex what's happened so far and what they've already tried before offering anything.
COffer to take the call yourself — it's faster and you can handle it cleanly.
DSend Alex a template you've used before for vendor pushback situations.
The GROW Model
The Questions That Actually Work
GROW is only as good as the questions you ask inside it. Here are the most useful questions at each stage.
G — Goal
"What would a good outcome look like for you?"
"What are you hoping to achieve here?"
"What does success look like in 3 months?"
R — Reality
"What's happening right now?"
"What have you already tried?"
"What's getting in the way?"
O — Options
"What could you do?"
"What else?" — ask this 2-3 times. First answer is rarely the best one.
"What would you do if you knew you couldn't get it wrong?"
W — Way Forward
"What will you do — specifically?"
"When will you do it?"
"What support do you need from me?"
"What else?" is the single most powerful coaching question. After any answer, ask it again. It pushes past the obvious first idea — which is usually not the most useful one.
The GROW Model
Walk It Through
Your direct report Jordan says: "I want to get better at presenting to senior leaders, but I don't know where to start."
You want to coach using GROW. What do you say first?
A"Here's what I've found works when presenting to executives — lead with the recommendation, then the data."
B"What would success look like for you in six months when it comes to senior presentations?"
C"You should connect with L&D — they have an executive presence course that might be perfect."
D"Why do you think you struggle with senior presentations right now?"
Practice
They Want You to Solve It
Sam comes to you frustrated: "I can't figure out how to get the Operations team to prioritize our requests. They keep deprioritizing us. What should I do?"
What's your coaching response?
A"Here's what I'd do — schedule a recurring sync with their ops lead and get on the standing agenda."
B"What have you already tried with them? Walk me through what's happened."
C"I'll talk to their VP — I have a relationship there and can get this moved."
D"You should be escalating this to me sooner when it becomes a pattern."
Practice
Performance Gap — Coaching Approach
One of your team members, Casey, has been missing deadlines consistently. You sit down for a 1:1. A coaching approach to open this conversation looks like:
A"I want to talk about the missed deadlines. Walk me through what's been happening from your perspective."
B"You've missed three deadlines this month. This needs to stop — what's your plan?"
C"Let me share some time management strategies that have helped others on the team."
D"What do you think I should do about this situation?"
Practice
Career Development Conversation
Your direct report Taylor says in a 1:1: "I want to grow, but I'm not really sure what direction." You want to coach, not tell. Your next move:
A"Based on what I've seen from you, I think you should develop your project management skills."
B"What does growth look like to you in the next year or two? What would feel meaningful?"
C"Let me connect you with L&D — they have a career pathing framework that's great for this."
D"Tell me about a time you felt really energized by your work — what were you doing?"

Course Complete
You've finished Coaching for Performance. The managers who change careers are the ones who ask better questions — and do it consistently, even when it's slower.

What great coaching managers do differently:

They resist the urge to answer — and ask what the person has already tried first
They start with Goal, not Reality — so the conversation is forward-facing
They ask "What else?" at least twice in every coaching conversation
They close with a specific commitment: what will you do, and when
They use coaching and feedback as distinct tools — not the same conversation
