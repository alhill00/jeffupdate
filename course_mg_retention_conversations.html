Foundation
The Exit Interview Is Too Late
By the time a Team Member is filling out an exit survey, the decision has already been made. The decision was probably made weeks or months ago — and the interview is a formality.
The highest-leverage retention conversation isn't the exit interview. It's the stay conversation — the one you have while there's still nothing to lose and everything to gain.
Most managers have never had one. Not because they don't care — but because they assume engaged Team Members don't need one. That assumption is exactly backward. The people you most need to stay are the ones you're least likely to worry about.
The stay conversation is a proactive investment. It doesn't happen when something is wrong — it happens before something goes wrong. And it's often the difference between a Team Member who stays and one who quietly stops trying.

The Stay Conversation
Four Questions for a Quarterly Stay Conversation
The stay conversation isn't an interview or an interrogation. It's a 20-minute listening conversation — one of the most valuable investments you make in a quarter. Four questions. That's all.
1

"What about your work is most energizing right now?"

Opens with strength and engagement — establishes a positive frame before the harder questions.

2

"What's been most frustrating or draining?"

The question most managers avoid — but the one that surfaces what's actually at risk. Listen. Don't immediately fix.

3

"Is there anything you'd like more of — or less of — in your role?"

Invites specific input on the conditions of their work. Often surfaces something small and actionable.

4

"Where do you want to be in 1–2 years, and how can I help?"

Connects to development and shows you're invested in their future — not just their current output.

This is a listening conversation, not a performance conversation. Don't fix everything in the room. Listen, take notes, and follow up on one thing.
Foundation
What to Do With What You Hear
A stay conversation with no follow-through is worse than no conversation. If you ask and don't act, you've signaled that the conversation was performative — and trust goes down, not up.

After the Stay Conversation:

Act on one thing — not everything, but something specific they named.
Name it when you do: "You mentioned X was frustrating — I changed Y." Closing the loop is as important as the action itself.
Check back next quarter — and in the next 1:1. Ask if it helped.
Keep a note of what they said — use it to be a more informed manager in every future conversation.
The compound interest of listening + acting is the retention investment. One stay conversation, consistently followed up, builds more trust over a year than almost any other management behavior.

The Bored Strongest Performer
Your strongest Team Member seems bored lately — less energized in meetings, shorter in 1:1s, going through the motions. You haven't had a stay conversation in six months. What do you do?
AWait for the annual review — address disengagement formally when you have the structured opportunity.
BHave a stay conversation now — start with what's energizing and what isn't, and listen for what's pulling her away or keeping her engaged.
CAssign her a new project without discussing it — give her something new to be interested in.
DTell her you've noticed a change and ask directly if she's thinking about leaving.
The Stay Conversation
When They Mention a Recruiter
In a stay conversation, a Team Member mentions she got a call from a recruiter last week. She says she's not seriously looking — but the call was interesting. How do you respond?
AAsk her directly if she's seriously considering leaving — you need to know.
BThank her for sharing it, ask what the role was and what about it caught her attention.
CRemind her of what's valuable about her current role and why she should stay.
DEscalate to HR immediately — this is a flight risk situation that needs HR involvement.
Practice
When You Can't Fix What They Named
In a stay conversation, a Team Member names something that's draining her — but it's something you genuinely can't change (compensation limits, an org structure decision above you). How do you respond?
AAcknowledge what she said and move on — don't dwell on something you can't fix.
BBe honest about what's within your control and what isn't — then ask what you can do that would help most.
CPromise to look into it — buy time and follow up when you have more information.
DExplain why the constraint exists — help her understand the business reasoning.
Practice
The "Everything's Fine" Team Member
You try to have a stay conversation with a Team Member and he gives you vague, positive answers to everything. "Things are great." "I really like the team." You sense there might be more but can't get to it. What do you do?
AAccept it — if he says everything is fine, take him at his word.
BGo deeper with more specific questions — ask about a specific recent project, a moment he felt most/least energized, or what he'd change about one aspect of his role.
CTell him you sense there might be more and push him to be honest.
DAsk his teammates if they know what's going on.
Practice
Staying Proactive With the Satisfied
One of your Team Members is genuinely happy — great relationship, interesting work, no red flags. You're tempted to skip the stay conversation. Should you?
AYes — save the stay conversation for people who are at risk. Satisfied people don't need it.
BNo — satisfied people today can be unsatisfied people in six months, especially if they feel their manager stopped paying attention. The stay conversation is an investment, not a rescue.
CDo a shorter version — 5 minutes instead of 20, just to check the box.
DSend a quick survey instead of a conversation — it's less time-intensive and still gets the signal.
